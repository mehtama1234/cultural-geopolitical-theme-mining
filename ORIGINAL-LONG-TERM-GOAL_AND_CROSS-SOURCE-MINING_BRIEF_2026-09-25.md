# Original long-term goal and cross-source mining brief

**Created:** 2026-09-25  
**Status:** active governing orientation  
**Program:** Cultural, Social, and Geopolitical Theme Mining

## The actual long-term goal

Build a living, source-traceable map of the forces changing American life and
American power.

The project uses many different kinds of evidence to identify recurring
societal, cultural, political, economic, consumer, institutional, firm,
labor, infrastructure, technological, and geopolitical patterns. It is not
fundamentally a single-household panel study, a universal stress index, or a
collection of isolated news summaries.

The central question is:

> Across all the evidence, what is changing underneath visible events, who
> gains or loses practical room to act, who absorbs the cost or risk, how do
> people and institutions adapt, and what happens to trust, culture, politics,
> public capacity, firm power, state leverage, and geopolitical position?

## The cross-source research path

For each important pattern, follow the longest defensible route:

```text
condition, shock, decision, or capability
  -> exposure and incidence
  -> change in money, time, access, health, status, control, or security
  -> practical alternatives and room to maneuver
  -> adaptation by people, consumers, workers, firms, and institutions
  -> redistribution of cost, risk, ownership, information, waiting, and power
  -> trust, identity, cultural meaning, legitimacy, or social behavior
  -> political judgment, collective action, switching, exit, or non-use
  -> firm/sector capacity, public capacity, dependence, state leverage,
     alliance position, or geopolitical consequence
```

This is an investigation path, not a claim that every source or theme closes
the whole chain. Every connection must be labeled as measured, reported,
estimated, experimentally identified, longitudinally associated, compared,
inferred across sources, or still open.

Different datasets may reveal different parts of a recurring pattern. They must
not be written as though they follow the same person, household, firm, place,
or country unless identifiers and timing actually support that claim.

## The source universe

The evidence base includes:

- household, income, spending, debt, health, hardship, and financial surveys;
- time-use, care, childcare, eldercare, transportation, and family sources;
- opinion, identity, trust, belonging, civic-life, voting, and participation
  surveys;
- public-benefit, administrative, court, enforcement, payment, and remedy
  records;
- consumer complaints, privacy, safety, wage, refund, and regulatory records;
- worker, employer, firm, market, ownership, bargaining, and concentration data;
- platform, digital-use, AI, moderation, deletion, cancellation, and exit data;
- housing, insurance, disaster, place, mobility, provider, and local-capacity
  sources;
- energy, transmission, transit, construction, reliability, rate, and public-
  input records;
- defense, trade, sanctions, alliance, supply-chain, procurement, and
  geopolitical records; and
- NBER, Census CES, academic, international, and other studies that measure a
  specific mechanism or outcome.

Representative source families include Census, SIPP, CPS/ASEC, HTOPS, SHED,
MEPS, ATUS, NHTS, ACS, BLS, PSID, UAS, HRS, Pew, ANES, CCES, CIRCLE, GAO,
HUD, FEMA, FNS, Medicaid, CFPB, FTC, EEOC, NLRB, BJS, DOE, FERC, PJM, FCC,
NIST, NATO, Commerce, OFAC, SIPRI, NBER, and Census CES working papers.

The purpose of using this range is comparative: household evidence can show
burden, administrative evidence can show institutional response, surveys can
show meaning and trust, firm evidence can show market power, infrastructure
records can show capacity and incidence, and geopolitical records can show
state or alliance consequences.

## The 14 standing themes

Maintain coverage across:

1. household room, prices, debt, and financial security;
2. time, care, family life, and social reproduction;
3. consumer power, recourse, and service dependence;
4. platforms, data, attention, and digital agency;
5. work, control, bargaining, and labor-market change;
6. health, disability, aging, and unequal care access;
7. housing, place, mobility, and insurance;
8. unequal exposure, status, race, gender, age, and belonging;
9. trust, identity, cultural meaning, and changing norms;
10. public systems, administrative access, and policy feedback;
11. political judgment, civic participation, and collective action;
12. firm, market, ownership, and concentration power;
13. infrastructure, energy, technology, and dependency; and
14. geopolitical and state consequences.

No individual deep lane—AI and work, health costs, housing, consumer
recourse, household constraints, migration, public systems, infrastructure, or
defense—defines the program. Each is an evidence lane inside the map.

## Cross-cutting patterns to mine and test

The initial synthesis should continually test these recurring structures:

### Offered help versus usable help

Benefits, rights, services, refunds, privacy controls, and infrastructure can
exist formally while remaining difficult to use because of cost, time, waiting,
distance, paperwork, language, provider refusal, or weak appeals.

### Capacity versus lived outcome

Applications processed, dollars allocated, jobs created, flights completed,
models deployed, or megawatts planned are meaningful but do not automatically
prove restored food, housing, health, money, safety, trust, or deterrence.

### Visibility versus correction

Being recorded by a benefit, platform, firm, or government system does not
guarantee that a person can understand the status, challenge an error, reverse
the decision, receive a remedy, or remain secure afterward.

### Adaptation as hidden cost transfer

Families, workers, consumers, and communities often preserve continuity by
absorbing unpaid care, debt, delay, travel, reduced consumption, worse work,
informal support, surveillance, or non-use. Apparent resilience can therefore
hide redistributed burden.

### Formal alternatives versus real alternatives

An alternative is usable only when people can reach it with their money, time,
information, health, geography, status, social connections, and switching
capacity. The project should treat unequal optionality as a central comparative
lens.

### Fragmented authority and changing trust

People increasingly combine government, expert, employer, family, peer,
influencer, platform, AI, religious, and community authority. Track not only
whether people trust institutions, but whom they consult, what they can verify,
and whether trust changes use, action, switching, or exit.

### Classification and administrative legibility

People must often be correctly named, counted, timed, and categorized before
they can receive protection. What cannot be measured or classified may vanish
from the institution's view.

### Infrastructure bargains and dependence

Energy, hospitals, broadband, transport, data centers, defense systems, and
transmission distribute cost, reliability, land, ownership, public voice,
dependency, and strategic leverage. Separate announced capacity, operating
capacity, local incidence, public benefit, and geopolitical effect.

### Material pressure becoming political meaning

Prices, debt, health costs, work insecurity, housing, immigration, and service
access may become judgments about government, corporations, national decline,
cultural identity, fairness, or political legitimacy. Do not assume one
automatic political response; seek differences, counterexamples, and timing.

### Exit and non-use as power indicators

Not applying, not complaining, not seeking care, not switching, deleting an
account, leaving a place, or refusing a service may reflect cost, futility,
fear, dependence, distrust, or lack of alternatives. Non-use is not merely
missing participation.

## What each source pass must produce

Every pass should record:

- the source, unit, population, place, date range, denominator, and vintage;
- the measured claim and method;
- the mechanism the claim may support;
- who gains room, who loses room, who waits, and who controls the decision;
- affected groups, unequal exposure, and available alternatives;
- institutional, firm, community, cultural, or political response;
- a counterexample or competing explanation;
- what the source cannot show; and
- the smallest next test that would strengthen or weaken the interpretation.

The working transformation is:

```text
source -> measured claim -> method and limit -> mechanism
      -> affected groups and alternatives -> response and redistribution
      -> meaning, trust, or action -> wider capacity or power consequence
      -> counterevidence -> next test
```

## The products to maintain

The durable outputs are:

- source-specific records and finding pages;
- machine-readable trend observations;
- the 14-theme inventory and evidence matrix;
- subtheme maps and cross-theme connections;
- cross-source trend portfolios;
- cultural/political meaning, digital-life, infrastructure, and geopolitical
  syntheses;
- strongest-chain and open-arrow registers;
- counterexample and source-reuse audits;
- plain-language societal readouts; and
- the connected HTML reading atlas.

The later same-unit or recipient-level episode work is a depth test for one
open arrow. It is not the definition of this long-term goal.

## Governing interpretation

The current working proposition is:

> American systems are becoming more capable and more visible, but people do
> not receive equal practical power from them.

Treat this as a hypothesis to test, narrow, or disprove. The program advances
when a new source reveals a recurring mechanism, a counterexample, a neglected
group, a new cross-theme connection, or a later stage of an existing trend.
