# Source expansion plan

The two starting sources answer different questions. HBS Working Knowledge explains research for a broad audience. NBER gives access to working papers, programs, metadata, and public-use data. We need other source families so a theme is not built from one institution's view.

## The source stack

### Research and interpretation

- [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/topics/) for policy research across work, technology, public opinion, cities, and global affairs.
- [Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/topics) for international security, trade, resources, institutions, society, and technology.
- [Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/) for regional and country analysis.

These sources help form questions and competing explanations. They should not be treated as final proof.

### People and social meaning

- [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/) for surveys, international attitudes, technology use, demographics, and identity.
- [UNDP Human Development Data](https://hdr.undp.org/data-center) for living conditions, inequality, gender, and human development.

These sources help us see how a market or policy change is felt by people. Survey answers are not the same as observed behavior, so we record question wording, sample, field dates, and weighting.

### The material base

- [World Bank Open Data](https://data.worldbank.org/) for country and development indicators.
- [OECD Data Explorer](https://data-explorer.oecd.org/) for comparable data on work, firms, tax, education, and living conditions.
- [IMF Data](https://data.imf.org/en) for inflation, fiscal accounts, trade, balance of payments, and financial conditions.
- [ILOSTAT](https://ilostat.ilo.org/) for work, wages, informal activity, migration, and labor standards.
- [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/) for US economic series, releases, and revisions.

These sources ground a cultural or geopolitical story in money, work, prices, population, and state capacity.

### Security and hard power

- [SIPRI databases](https://www.sipri.org/databases) for arms transfers, military spending, arms companies, and peace operations.

Use this layer when an economic dependency may become a security lever. Check definitions and missing data before making comparisons.

### The hidden pipes: money, energy, and measurement

- [IMF publications by Manmohan Singh](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/publications-by-author?author=Manmohan%20Singh&name=Manmohan%20Singh) for author-level work on money, finance, crises, and macro policy.
- [BIS Papers](https://www.bis.org/publications/bis-paper) for central-bank and financial-system research. BIS notes that the views in these papers are not always the BIS position.
- [BEA Learning Center](https://www.bea.gov/resources/learning-center) for the meaning and construction of national-account measures.
- [IEA reports](https://www.iea.org/analysis?type=report) for energy supply, demand, transition, technology, and security scenarios.
- [Office of Financial Research](https://www.financialresearch.gov/) for financial-system structure and systemic-risk data.
- [World Bank Research](https://www.worldbank.org/en/research) for development research that links markets, institutions, poverty, climate, and country change.

These sources help expose the systems beneath visible events: how money moves, how energy limits choices, how financial risk travels, and how a statistic is made. Treat forecasts and scenarios as conditional views, not facts.

## Suggested review rule

For a major theme, aim for at least:

- one original study or dataset;
- one source that measures people or households;
- one source that measures firms, money, or material conditions;
- one source that measures institutions or state power;
- one source that could disagree with the proposed finding.

The final memo should say which layer is strong, which layer is inferred, and which layer is missing.
