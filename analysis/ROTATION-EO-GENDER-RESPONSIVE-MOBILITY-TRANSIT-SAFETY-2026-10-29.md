# Rotation EO — Gender-responsive mobility, transit safety and route freedom

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** transport / gender / public space / care mobility / work / education / safety / grievance

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers practical transit mobility, public-space safety, school
attendance, public toilets, street lighting, disability access and women’s work.
This rotation isolates the gendered safety and route-freedom layer of mobility:
whether women, girls, LGBTQ+ people, disabled riders, migrants, informal
workers and caregivers can make necessary journeys at the time, price and speed
required without harassment, violence, coercion or unremedied fear.

A new line, bus, station, panic button, women-only carriage, lighting project or
reporting protocol can improve a system while people still avoid night travel,
choose expensive routes, miss work or school, travel only with companions, or
accept surveillance and restrictive norms as the price of movement. The unit is
the complete named journey, including first and last mile and what happens after
an incident—not ridership or infrastructure alone.

## Research question

When a city, transport operator, employer, school, police service, technology
provider or development programme changes vehicles, stations, fares, staffing,
lighting, women’s employment, reporting, emergency response or route design,
under what conditions does it produce safe, affordable, accessible and
repeatable mobility that expands work, education, care, health and cultural
participation without shifting risk to riders or increasing surveillance and
retaliation?

## Conversion chain

```text
care/work/school/health/cultural trip + gendered risk + time and fare constraint
  -> route, vehicle, station, street, staffing, fare, lighting and safety policy
  -> first-mile / waiting / boarding / journey / transfer / last-mile encounter
  -> safe, affordable, accessible and predictable completed trip
  -> work, education, care, health, income, public-space use and autonomy
  -> reporting power, trust, operator accountability and freedom to choose routes
```

The unit must be a named person, journey, route, stop, vehicle, incident,
operator or response with dates, time, cost, accessibility, harassment,
reporting, remedy and later participation fields. Ridership, station count,
camera coverage, panic-button installation, women’s workforce share or safety
perception is an intermediate signal—not proof of safe route freedom.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Safety changes travel behaviour and therefore opportunity

World Bank evidence from women’s mobility research reports that safety concerns,
night travel, harassment, social norms, affordability, waiting and last-mile
conditions influence women’s transport choices. In one documented public-
transport study, women changed routines after harassment, including avoiding
travel after dark, reducing public-transport use, travelling in groups and
switching to more expensive or longer routes.

The material consequence is not merely fear. It is lost time, higher cost,
restricted work and education access, reduced care flexibility and a narrower
right to occupy the city.

Source: [World Bank women’s public-transport safety evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099060425063610382/pdf/P177653-45fb904f-cfb2-4d14-981a-dd223ecd3819.pdf).

### 2. A safe system includes the whole route and effective remedy

The World Bank’s 2025 women-in-transport toolkit identifies harassment across
first and last mile, stops and vehicles. It notes that overcrowding, deserted
stations, night travel, weak emergency helplines and ineffective grievance
mechanisms compound vulnerability, while under-reporting can reflect retaliation
fears, community stigma, lack of awareness and distrust.

This closes the route-and-remedy mechanism. Cameras, panic buttons or reporting
channels do not close whether a rider can safely use them, whether staff respond,
whether evidence is protected, or whether the operator changes behaviour.

Source: [World Bank She Drives Change: urban transport](https://shedriveschange.worldbank.org/urban-transport).

### 3. Transport safety and women’s employment reinforce each other

The International Transport Forum’s 2025 women-in-transport work reports that
women make up only about 12% of global transportation and storage workers and
calls for action on education, employment and promotion barriers. Women’s
presence as drivers, operators, managers, planners and decision-makers can
change service design and institutional response, but workforce share alone does
not establish rider safety or decent work.

The conversion must track both sides: the passenger’s journey and the worker’s
exposure to harassment, violence, scheduling, pay, protective systems and voice.

Source: [ITF Women in Transport](https://www.itf-oecd.org/women-transport-supporting-women-leaders-and-employees).

### 4. Institutional response can change perceived and actual route safety

The World Bank’s Quito Metro case documents a prevention and response protocol,
reporting channels, staff training and a women’s workforce quota. It reports that
96% of women felt safe during the first five months of implementation and that
women’s workforce participation exceeded the target.

This is a useful positive counterexample and implementation mechanism, but its
reported period and perception measure do not establish long-run incident rates,
under-reporting, accessibility, night-time coverage, affordability, last-mile
safety or remedy. The packet keeps it as evidence of a plausible mechanism, not
a universal causal result.

Source: [World Bank Quito Metro case study](https://shedriveschange.worldbank.org/case-study/case-study-7-quito).

### 5. Design can support access while norms still constrain movement

World Bank research on Dhaka reports that women across income groups experienced
night-time safety concerns, harassment, affordability and overcrowding barriers,
while social norms discouraged some forms of independent travel. It recommends
gender action plans, safer vehicle and street design, improved walking and
cycling conditions and measures addressing harassment.

The cultural test is therefore not only whether infrastructure is present. It is
whether families, employers, operators, police and communities treat women’s
independent movement as legitimate, and whether a rider can use the system
without changing dress, timing, companions or destination to manage others’
behaviour.

Source: [World Bank transport mobility and adaptive behaviour of women](https://connect4impact.worldbank.org/topic/exploring-transport-mobility-issues-and-adaptive-behavior-women-developing-country-2024).

### 6. Technology can improve response while creating new risks

The World Bank Chennai case describes AI-enabled panic buttons, cameras and an
integrated command centre intended to improve women’s bus safety, while noting
that cultural barriers and fear of drawing attention may make women reluctant to
use the buttons. Safety technology must therefore be assessed for usability,
privacy, data retention, false alarms, operator response, retaliation and
whether it changes the underlying environment.

Source: [World Bank Chennai gender-responsive urban transport case](https://shedriveschange.worldbank.org/urban-transport).

## Bounded proposition 146

**A new bus or rail line, station, lighting project, women-only carriage,
panic button, camera system, safety audit, fare subsidy, reporting protocol,
gender action plan or women’s transport-workforce initiative is not by itself
safe mobility, route freedom, work or school access, care continuity, income,
public-space belonging, gender equality or justice.** Ridership, coverage,
station counts, installed devices, reported incidents, safety perceptions,
women’s workforce share, response-time targets or complaints received do not
establish that named riders completed affordable, accessible and safe journeys,
could travel at needed times, avoided retaliation, obtained remedy, or expanded
their participation after the intervention.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Mobility is a permission structure.** Families, employers, operators,
   police and communities can enable or restrict independent movement even when
   infrastructure exists.
2. **The trip is gendered and chained.** Care, work, school and shopping create
   multiple short journeys, waits and transfers that standard commute averages
   miss.
3. **Safety is relational.** Fellow passengers, drivers, staff, bystanders,
   police, platforms and reporting institutions determine whether a risk becomes
   survivable, normalized or escalated.
4. **Women’s employment in transport is both labour and design.** It can improve
   representation and trust, but workers also need decent conditions and
   protection from violence.
5. **Technology is not neutral.** A panic button can create agency or attention;
   cameras can support accountability or surveillance; data can protect or
   expose a rider.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A new station can improve capacity while the walk, wait or last-mile route
  remains unsafe, inaccessible or unaffordable.
- Women-only transport can expand travel for some riders while treating women’s
  exclusion as a segregation problem and leaving operator accountability weak.
- More cameras can increase evidence while reducing privacy or failing to change
  staff and police response.
- A reporting protocol can improve formal complaint counts while survivors avoid
  reporting because of retaliation, stigma or disbelief.
- A fare subsidy can reduce cost while frequency, crowding, disability access or
  night safety still prevents use.
- A positive safety perception in an early project period can coexist with
  under-reporting, selective ridership, unmeasured incidents or later decline.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. World Bank and
ITF evidence supports the gendered route, safety, employment, technology,
reporting and cultural mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2**
because public sources rarely follow the same rider and worker through repeated
journeys, incidents, remedy, participation and long-run route freedom.

## Next test

Follow named women, girls, disabled riders, LGBTQ+ riders, caregivers and
transport workers across a complete first-mile-to-last-mile journey, including
night and off-peak trips. Compare a redesigned route with an ordinary route and
track one reported or unreported incident through operator, police and survivor
outcomes. Include affordability, accessibility, privacy and family or employer
permission.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the gendered route, behaviour, safety, employment,
technology, reporting and cultural mechanisms. It does not close named repeated
journeys, incident rates, affordability, accessibility, retaliation, operator or
police remedy, work/school/care participation, privacy or durable route freedom.
Those remain acquisition targets.
