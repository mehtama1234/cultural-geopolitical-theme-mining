# Rotation EE — Artificial light at night, dark-sky access and night-ecology justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** environment / public health / biodiversity / culture / geography

## Why this rotation

The atlas now covers ambient air, noise, pesticides, heat, public space and
night-time economy. This rotation isolates artificial light at night (ALAN):
street lighting, buildings, sports facilities, construction, ports, airports,
offshore infrastructure, advertising and security systems.

Light produces genuine benefits—wayfinding, safety, work, commerce, care and
cultural life—but it can also disrupt sleep, circadian rhythms, wildlife,
pollination, migration, astronomy and the cultural meaning of night. The key
question is not “more or less lighting”; it is who controls intensity, spectrum,
timing, geography and exceptions, and who receives safety without losing
darkness, health, ecological function or cultural access.

## Research question

When a city, developer, port, sports venue, transport authority, energy system
or conservation body installs, upgrades, dims or restricts lighting, under what
conditions does it improve safe access and social life while protecting sleep,
wildlife, dark skies, cultural practice and equitable control of the night?

## Conversion chain

```text
darkness / safety / work / mobility / ecology / cultural-night need
  -> lighting design, timing, spectrum, planning, standards and enforcement
  -> dwelling, street, school, workplace, habitat, sky and night-time economy
  -> light exposure, safety, sleep, energy, wildlife, access and social use
  -> health, biodiversity, culture, belonging, productivity and legitimacy
  -> adaptive control, quiet/dark-space protection, accountability and remedy
```

The unit must be a named street, dwelling, school, workplace, habitat,
astronomical or cultural site, species, venue, neighbourhood or lighting system,
with spectrum, intensity, timing, duration and intervention dates. Satellite
brightness, lamp replacement, energy savings, complaints or illumination levels
are intermediate signals, not proof of safety, sleep, biodiversity or cultural
continuity.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Artificial light is an emerging but under-regulated environmental pressure

The EEA describes artificial light at night as a neglected threat to biodiversity
that spreads through urban and infrastructure growth into nocturnal ecosystems.
It reports that truly dark spaces in the EU-27 shrank by 5% between 2014–2015 and
2020–2021, while noting that evidence and legislative controls remain incomplete.
It also identifies disrupted sleep and several health outcomes as researched
human pathways, but does not establish a universal causal effect for every light
source or population.

Source: [EEA light-pollution biodiversity signal](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/zero-pollution/ecosystems/signals/biodiversity-signals).

### 2. Nocturnal ecosystems depend on darkness and timing

UNEP reports that artificial light can disrupt insects’ movement, foraging,
reproduction and predation, as well as disorientate turtles, seabirds and
shorebirds. It cites increasing global light emissions and notes that better
lighting design can reduce harm. The pathway is species- and place-specific:
light colour, direction, duration, season, habitat and migration route matter.

The evidence supports a biodiversity mechanism, not a claim that every lit area
has the same ecological effect.

Source: [UNEP global light-pollution evidence](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/story/global-light-pollution-affecting-ecosystems-what-can-we-do).

### 3. Pollination and food systems may be affected

UNEP summarizes research showing that artificial light disrupted nocturnal
pollination networks and reduced visits to flowers in a Swiss study. This is a
named empirical signal, not a global yield estimate. The relevant test for an
agricultural or restoration project is whether lighting changes pollinator
behaviour, fruit set, crop income or biodiversity in that place and season.

Source: [UNEP light pollution and pollinators](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/story/study-suggests-light-pollution-hurts-pollinators-crop-yields).

### 4. Human safety and health involve trade-offs

WHO’s environmental-health material identifies artificial lighting, sleep,
stress and the built environment as connected health issues, while broader
urban-health guidance treats safety, transport and healthy living conditions as
interdependent. Lighting can make routes legible and reduce perceived danger,
but glare, over-lighting, uneven coverage, surveillance, energy cost and light
intrusion may create new burdens.

The test is not whether a place is brighter. It is whether named people can
move, sleep, work and use public space safely, with lighting controlled in ways
they can understand and contest.

Source: [WHO urban-health evidence](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/urban-health).

### 5. Dark skies have cultural and scientific value

UNESCO’s World Heritage astronomy work treats the sky as part of the environment
and recognizes cultural sites connected to astronomical observation and
traditional knowledge. Its thematic study distinguishes different dark-sky
purposes: astronomical observation, cultural landscapes, wildlife conservation,
natural areas and nightscapes, each requiring different zones and management.

This supports a cultural-conversion mechanism: dark skies are not merely an
absence of infrastructure. They can be part of navigation, ceremony, seasonal
knowledge, scientific practice, landscape meaning and intergenerational identity.

Sources: [UNESCO Astronomy and World Heritage](https://whc.unesco.org/en/astronomy/)
and [UNESCO/ICOMOS/IAU dark-sky thematic study](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/activities/documents/activity-631-1.pdf).

### 6. Safety and conservation require negotiated design

UNEP’s wildlife guidance recognizes possible conflicts between human safety and
wildlife conservation and recommends impact assessment, wildlife-friendly
design and management near susceptible species. This is a useful governance
pattern: lighting decisions should specify purpose, place, timing, alternatives,
monitoring, exceptions, accountability and review rather than treating permanent
illumination as the default.

The same design discipline applies to communities: residents, workers, disabled
people, women, children, night-time users, astronomers and Indigenous or local
knowledge holders may value darkness and light differently.

## Bounded proposition 136

**A street-lighting programme, LED retrofit, security-lighting system, sports or
port illumination, illuminated development, dark-sky reserve, dimming policy,
night-time safety intervention or wildlife-friendly lighting standard is not by
itself safer mobility, better sleep, lower energy burden, biodiversity recovery,
preserved cultural night or equitable control of the dark.** Lamp counts,
brightness, energy savings, crime complaints, satellite radiance, dark-sky
designation, wildlife-friendly design or public consultation do not establish
that named residents, workers, children, species and cultural sites experienced
safe access, lower intrusion, protected sleep, maintained ecological behaviour,
affordable energy, cultural continuity, equal enforcement or remedy when light
was displaced or exceptions expanded.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Night is a shared temporal commons.** Lighting changes who can rest, work,
   travel, gather, observe, worship, navigate and experience a landscape.
2. **Brightness is not safety.** Direction, glare, contrast, timing, maintenance,
   social presence and transport may matter more than total illumination.
3. **Darkness is unevenly governed.** Wealthier areas may purchase quiet/dark
   retreat while infrastructure corridors and low-income places remain lit or
   exposed to intrusive lighting.
4. **Species have different night geographies.** A lamp can be harmless in one
   place and a migration, nesting or pollination barrier in another.
5. **Cultural astronomy is living knowledge.** Sky visibility can support science,
   navigation, ceremony, seasonal calendars and place identity.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- More lighting can improve route visibility while increasing glare, energy cost,
  sleep intrusion or ecological harm.
- Dimming can reduce energy and ecological exposure while creating real safety or
  accessibility concerns if routes, crossings and maintenance are inadequate.
- LEDs can lower energy use per lamp while expanding hours, coverage or blue-rich
  spectrum and increasing total ecological exposure.
- A dark-sky designation can protect astronomy and tourism while excluding local
  livelihoods or shifting lighting to an unprotected edge.
- Wildlife-friendly lamps can reduce harm for one species or season while failing
  for another; local monitoring is required.
- Satellite nighttime radiance is useful for discovery but cannot establish
  indoor exposure, glare, spectral composition, safety, sleep or cultural use.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. EEA, UNEP, WHO
and UNESCO sources support the biodiversity, health, safety, cultural and
governance mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because public
sources rarely follow the same lighting system and people/species through
exposure, sleep, safety, ecology, culture, energy cost and remedy.
