## Research question

When public and private investment expands infrastructure for AI, energy,
manufacturing, broadband, minerals, and mobility, who receives usable service,
who carries the cost, and which promised benefit is still only an award,
loan, plan, or capacity claim?

Keep the realization chain separate:

`plan/award → construction or deployment → operating service → user access →
customer benefit → cost/risk distribution`

Earlier documented gaps are retained as history. A result is promoted only
when it identifies the atlas source or a distinctive, source-linked public
discussion. Industry enthusiasm, a funding announcement, or a capacity target
is not evidence of delivered consumer or firm benefit.

## Search boundary and limits

Search YouTube, Reddit, LinkedIn, and X for exact source names, distinctive
findings, named projects, and user or firm consequences within the stated
window. Record platform gaps instead of filling them with generic technology
commentary. Public discussion can show expectations, conflict, local burden,
or lived dependence; it cannot by itself prove a line was built, service was
reliable, rates fell, or strategic autonomy was achieved.

This is public-web discovery, not a platform-native Creator/API export. Search
can omit comments, deleted posts, private groups, and non-indexed content.
The batch is not completion of the infrastructure theme or the broader
14-theme atlas.

## Current findings

Six selected rows are augmented:

- Data-center capacity and electricity-tax governance
- National transmission needs and transmission-facilitation realization
- Critical-mineral substitution and recycling
- Autonomous-feature exposure and mobility trust

The data-center discussion contains a counterexample: current policy debate
focuses on local rate design, taxes, congestion, and who pays for grid
buildout, while a relayed causal study reports modestly lower average U.S.
retail rates in an earlier 2015–2024 period. Those findings are not
interchangeable: national average rates can fall while local congestion or
utility-class burdens rise.

The DOE discussion describes real demand and supply-chain constraints for
transformers and power electronics, but anticipated affordability and
reliability gains remain future claims. Critical-mineral discussion shifts the
question from extraction to recovery and processing; policy recognition of
recyclable material is not yet usable industrial substitution. Autonomous
mobility has measurable public exposure, but exposure and trust do not prove
safe, reliable, affordable, or geographically equal service.
