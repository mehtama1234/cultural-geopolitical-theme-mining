# Rotation G: household optionality under cost pressure

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** household economics, substitution, liquidity, care/health trade-offs and protected participation  
**Status:** Tier 2 conversion packet; coping routes are observed across sources, but same-household later outcomes are open

## Research question

When prices, income timing or an unexpected expense compress a household's room,
what alternatives does it actually use, and what does it protect or sacrifice?

```text
price/income/health/repair shock
  -> liquidity and time constraint
  -> cut, delay, borrow, substitute, insure or seek support
  -> food, housing, health, work, care or social-participation consequence
  -> later continuity, debt, recovery, switching or withdrawal
```

This rotation combines three different lenses:

- Fed SHED: reported financial capacity, shocks and coping;
- BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey: observed average spending and income-quintile
  allocation; and
- Census HTOPS: geographically available household conditions, including food,
  housing, usual-expense difficulty and transportation.

They are complementary, not linkable by default.

## New trend interpretation

The sharper trend is not simply “consumers are cautious.” It is:

> Household resilience increasingly means preserving a chosen priority by
> transferring pressure into another domain—time, debt, care, health,
> transportation, quality or participation.

This makes optionality a cultural and political variable. Two households can
report the same income or price shock but experience different freedom because
one has savings, transport, flexible work, nearby providers or family support
and the other has none of those substitutes.

## Cultural and geographical meaning

Household choices are not only economic optimization. They protect meanings:
children's stability, health, family rituals, dignity, religious or community
participation, status, mobility and the ability to say no. A cutback can be a
strategic preservation of identity or an involuntary loss of room.

Geography determines which alternatives are real. A household in a dense city
may substitute transit for a car; a rural household may not. A high-cost region
may convert price pressure into housing crowding; a service-scarce region may
convert it into travel and waiting. National averages can conceal these local
option sets.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Reported emergency capacity is hypothetical and does not prove behavior when
  an actual shock occurs.
- Spending growth can reflect need, price, quality, household composition or
  timing; it is not automatically welfare improvement or decline.
- Income-quintile averages do not follow the same households over time.
- Cutting expenses may be voluntary substitution, prudent planning or forced
  deprivation; the aggregate measure does not identify which.
- Geographic survey estimates do not by themselves identify the local service
  or cultural mechanism producing the difference.
- A household can preserve spending in one domain while accumulating debt or
  care exhaustion elsewhere.

## Promotion decision

Promote the following Tier 2 mechanism:

**Household resilience is the conversion of pressure into a constrained option
set; the critical inequality is not only income, but the availability, quality
and reversibility of substitutes.**

The next qualifying evidence is a panel, transaction/diary record or place-based
study that links a defined shock to a chosen alternative and then to a later
health, work, care, housing, social-participation or recovery outcome.
