# Rotation LT — Subsea cable fault to local continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Lane:** digital geography / island resilience / disaster continuity / geopolitics  
**Status:** Tier 1 named-event boundary plus Tier 2 fault-to-local-continuity question

## Research question

When a remote or island economy depends on a small number of submarine cables,
what converts a fault and a backup technology into continued, affordable and
locally governed service—and who is protected or excluded during repair?

## Named conversion chain

```text
named route and dependent place
  -> fault or hazard
  -> detection, permission, repair vessel and temporary fallback
  -> traffic restoration and service allocation
  -> household, business, public-service and relief continuity
  -> local affordability, agency, trust and future redundancy
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| World Bank Lifelines case on Tonga's 2019 Tonga–Fiji cable break | Anchor-related damage left Tonga without mobile or internet access for 11 days; bandwidth fell by more than 200-fold on the satellite backup; tourism, bookings and remittances were disrupted while essential government services were prioritized. | Named fault, fallback scarcity and differentiated social/economic exposure | Same business/household/service recovery, prices, unequal access and later resilience |
| ITU account of the January 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai eruption | The sole international cable broke in at least two places after the eruption; satellite and collaborative response restored limited connectivity while repair was constrained by the disaster context. | Hazard-to-route failure and emergency restoration mechanism | Which users received usable service, how relief and outer islands recovered, and who governed the backup |
| World Bank disaster-risk country cases | Tonga's 2022 international cable restoration took more than a month and the domestic extension about 18 months; the event caused near-week-long national connectivity loss and hampered relief. | International versus domestic repair asymmetry and public-recovery burden | Long-run household, health, education, business and island-level continuity |
| ITU 2025 disaster-risk synthesis | In Tonga, a 2024 MEO satellite service restored voice, SMS and data about six hours after another cable severance; multi-orbit systems are framed as a resilience layer. | A faster technical fallback in a named country | Capacity, price, licensing, priority allocation, local control and continuity for outer-island and vulnerable users |
| ITU Global Connectivity Report 2025 | Repair can take weeks to months because of permits, customs, cabotage and limited regional repair capacity; remote, island and landlocked economies are especially exposed. | Systemic repair mechanism and geographic asymmetry | Incidence by user, route, service quality, affordability and remedy |
| EU Cable Security Action Plan / Toolbox | The EU risk and funding architecture spans prevention, detection, response, recovery and deterrence, with mapping, stress tests, regional hubs and repair-capacity investment. | Governance and intervention architecture | Whether investments shorten restoration or protect customers and communities in a named incident |

## Bounded finding

> **Backup connectivity is not equivalent to restored local continuity.** A
> satellite terminal, a repaired international trunk, a regional cable hub or a
> resilience plan can close a technical restoration stage while leaving outer
> islands, households, small businesses, public services, remittances,
> affordability, licensing, data sovereignty and local decision power unequal or
> unobserved.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The same outage is not one event for every user. Tonga's international route,
domestic extension, main island, outer islands, government services, tourism
businesses, households and diaspora-linked remittance channels have different
fallback options and different recovery clocks. A resilient network therefore
requires more than route diversity: it requires priority rules, affordable
access, local operating capacity, repair authority and a record of who was able
to communicate while the system was degraded.

The geography of repair is also political. Specialized vessels, permissions,
customs, weather windows, cable ownership and vendor contracts determine who can
act and when. A technically successful international repair may coexist with a
longer domestic-island outage. Conversely, a satellite fallback can reduce harm
rapidly while creating a new dependency on a private operator, licensing regime,
foreign capacity or opaque prioritization.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Tonga's 2024 satellite restoration is a counterexample to the claim that a
  cable cut necessarily creates a prolonged total blackout; it does not prove
  equal service, affordability or durable local control.
- Tonga's 2019 and 2022 events show that a single route can fail through
  unrelated mechanisms; they do not establish a universal failure rate or
  deliberate attribution.
- A national service-restoration timestamp can conceal outer-island or
  household-level non-restoration.
- International repair duration and damage estimates are not the same as a
  measured household welfare loss or causal estimate.
- EU risk architecture is a policy response and investment pathway, not evidence
  that a specific community experienced improved continuity.

## Promotion decision

Promote the named Tonga route/event distinction to Tier 1: fault, repair,
fallback and service priority are separate stages, and international restoration
does not establish domestic or household continuity. Keep the broader
resilience, affordability, sovereignty and legitimacy claims in Tier 2 until a
dated user/service ledger follows at least one main-island and one outer-island
unit through the same event and records a comparator.

## Next acquisition

Build a route-to-user ledger for one Tonga cable event and one comparable island
or remote economy. Record route and ownership, fault time, affected landing and
domestic segments, detection, permissions, repair vessel and milestones,
temporary satellite capacity, licensing and priority rules, ISP performance,
prices, public-service/relief continuity, outer-island versus main-island
experience, complaint/remedy and post-event redundancy. Preserve a service that
continued and a user/place that remained excluded or degraded.
