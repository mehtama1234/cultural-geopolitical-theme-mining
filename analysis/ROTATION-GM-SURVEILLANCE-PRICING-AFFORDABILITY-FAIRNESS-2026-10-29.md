# Rotation GM — surveillance pricing, affordability and fairness

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** consumer, economic, technological, geographical and social systems

## Why this rotation exists

Price variation can reflect supply, demand, timing, scarcity or legitimate
discounts. A different and less visible possibility is a price or promotion
tailored using location, browsing history, demographics, purchase behaviour,
loyalty status or inferred willingness to pay. The market signal is an
algorithmic price or offer; the claimed outcome is affordability and fair
access. Without observing the same product, data pathway, alternative prices
and user circumstances, it is impossible to know whether personalization
expanded access or extracted surplus from people with fewer alternatives.

## Research question

When firms use consumer data and algorithms to set prices or promotions, under
what conditions do individualized offers improve access without undermining
price transparency, privacy, competition, equal treatment, household budgets or
the ability to compare and switch?

## Conversion chain

```text
consumer data / model / market conditions / offer / price
  -> person can see and understand total price and relevant alternatives
  -> purchase reflects affordable and non-discriminatory terms
  -> household budget, access and trust are protected
  -> consumers and rivals can compare, contest and exit pricing dependence
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The FTC's initial surveillance-pricing study found that intermediaries may
   use precise location, browser history, demographics, shopping behaviour and
   even mouse movements or abandoned carts to tailor prices or promotions. The
   findings are an initial document-based perspective, not proof that all firms
   use personalized prices or that every observed practice is unlawful.
2. The FTC reported that the intermediaries examined worked with at least 250
   clients and could show higher-priced products based on search and purchase
   activity in hypothetical examples. This establishes a plausible data-to-
   price mechanism while preserving the study's limits around confidentiality
   and hypothetical scenarios.
3. OECD's 2025 G7 analysis distinguishes dynamic pricing based on market
   conditions from personalized pricing based on consumer data. It notes that
   price discrimination can improve access in some contexts, while opacity,
   increased search costs, vulnerable-customer effects and loss of trust can
   create consumer harm.
4. OECD's 2026 digital-market work says personalization can affect prices,
   offers, rankings and recommendations, while consumers may not know how
   options were tailored. It argues that consumer agency should be incorporated
   into enforcement and remedy design rather than assuming a structural remedy
   automatically restores practical choice.
5. The FTC's 2026 inquiry orders information from airlines, ride-sharing,
   delivery, retail and other firms about whether personal data or loyalty
   participation affects prices or worker compensation. The inquiry is evidence
   of regulatory uncertainty and investigation, not a finding of universal
   discriminatory pricing.
6. EU online-market rules require disclosure where prices are personalized by
   algorithms, illustrating a transparency response. Disclosure may help when
   users can compare and act, but it does not by itself make the price
   affordable, explain the model, prevent sensitive inference or create an
   alternative supplier.

## Bounded proposition

**A dynamic price, personalized offer, loyalty discount, algorithmic price
display, disclosure, competition investigation or anti-discrimination rule is
not by itself affordability, equal treatment, privacy, informed comparison,
household budget security, fair access or consumer power.** A lower price for
one user, a disclosed personalized price or a regulator's inquiry does not
establish that named people understood the basis, received a fair total price,
could obtain a comparable alternative, avoided sensitive inference or could
contest and exit the pricing system.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Price fairness is interpreted through local incomes, norms, trust and the
availability of substitutes. Location-based pricing may reflect delivery cost
or local purchasing power, but it can also burden rural, low-income or
neighbourhood groups that have fewer providers. A migrant, disabled person or
older user may be profiled through proxies that are not visible to them. A
prepaid or cash household may not have the same ability to wait, compare across
devices or use a loyalty programme as a digitally connected higher-income user.
Fairness therefore requires place-level comparison of total prices, substitutes,
data use and actual budget impact—not only an average price or a disclosure.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Dynamic pricing can allocate scarce capacity and discounts can expand access;
  different prices are not automatically exploitative.
- The FTC study's initial findings and hypothetical examples do not establish
  the prevalence, legality or causal welfare effect of personalized pricing.
- A price difference can result from time, inventory, location or service cost
  rather than an individual profile; causal attribution requires model and
  transaction evidence.
- Transparency may be useful but can remain ineffective if users cannot compare,
  switch or afford the alternative.
- Higher prices for some users may coexist with lower prices for others; the
  distributional incidence must be measured by subgroup and geography.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: surveillance pricing is a conversion problem
from data-driven price formation to affordable, comprehensible and contestable
terms. Keep the named surveillance-pricing-to-fair-affordability-and-consumer
power chain as **Tier 2** until transaction-level, subgroup and geographic
evidence closes the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | price variation, personalized offer, inquiry, complaint or policy | strong FTC and OECD evidence |
| Mechanism | data source, inference, model, loyalty, location, timing and ranking | strong bounded mechanism evidence |
| Operation | named user sees price, understands basis, compares and attempts to switch | partial; model and journey data are scarce |
| Material consequence | total cost, affordability, access, budget stress, privacy or rival entry | not closed for a general surveillance-pricing claim |
| Meaning | fairness, dignity, trust, local legitimacy and cultural fit | not closed; requires affected-user evidence |
| Power | bargaining leverage, contestability, data refusal and durable pricing control | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Use mystery-shopping and lawful data-access requests across three places and
five essential or high-frequency sectors: transport, food delivery, lodging,
retail and financial services. Hold product, time and service constant while
varying location, device, account status, loyalty status, browsing history and
language. Record total price, fees, alternatives, consent, explanation,
successful switch, budget impact and complaint outcome. Add interviews with
low-income, rural, disabled, migrant and older users, and distinguish dynamic
from personalized pricing.

## Source boundary

The public record closes the existence of surveillance-pricing inquiries,
algorithmic pricing mechanisms, possible data inputs, transparency concerns and
the possibility of both access-enhancing and exploitative effects. It does not
justify a universal claim that firms charged a named person more, caused budget
harm, discriminated unlawfully or reduced competition. Those later outcomes
remain acquisition targets.
