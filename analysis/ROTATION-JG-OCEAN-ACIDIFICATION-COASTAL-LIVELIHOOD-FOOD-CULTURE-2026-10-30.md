# Rotation JG — Ocean acidification, coastal livelihoods, food and culture

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When ocean monitoring, fisheries science, hatchery adaptation and coastal policy respond to acidification, do they protect seafood security, income, cultural foodways and coastal resilience—or mainly produce pH trends, vulnerability assessments and technical adaptation without securing local access, affordability, jobs or cultural continuity?

## Why this packet matters

Ocean acidification is a chemical and social transition. It can affect shell-building organisms, food webs, reefs and coastal protection, but the consequences are mediated by species, place, fishing rights, aquaculture infrastructure, markets, climate co-stressors and community capacity. A biological signal is not automatically a fishery collapse, and a hatchery adaptation is not automatically an equitable solution for small-scale, subsistence or Indigenous users.

```text
CO2 uptake / warming / local runoff / upwelling
  -> pH, carbonate chemistry, temperature and oxygen change
  -> shellfish, crab, coral, plankton and food-web response
  -> catch, aquaculture, reef protection, price, work and food access
  -> fisher adaptation, management, cultural use, nutrition and migration
  -> durable coastal livelihood and ecosystem resilience
```

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Seafood is not only a commodity. Shellfish, crab, coral reefs and fish can be food, ceremony, identity, employment, ecological infrastructure and intergenerational knowledge. When a stock shifts north or a hatchery changes intake practices, the effects may include longer travel, lost local processing, altered diets, broken seasonal routines and weakened relationships to place—even if national landings remain stable.

Ocean chemistry crosses jurisdictional boundaries, while adaptation capacity does not. A country or port with monitoring, hatchery technology and capital may buffer some losses; a small island, subsistence fishery or Indigenous community may have fewer substitutes and less influence over emissions, coastal pollution or fisheries rules. Coastal adaptation can therefore protect aggregate production while concentrating costs in less mobile communities.

Attribution must remain disciplined. Acidification can be associated with biological decline alongside warming, disease, overfishing, oxygen loss and habitat change. The NOAA red-king-crab evidence is important precisely because it quantifies a relationship in a defined stock and period; it does not justify treating every local fishery change as an acidification effect or every technical intervention as socially adequate.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A pH trend, shellfish sensitivity result, vulnerability map, hatchery retrofit, fishermen workshop, adaptive-management recommendation, fishery closure or modeled catch projection is not by itself stable seafood access, income, food affordability, cultural continuity, reef protection, equitable adaptation or community resilience. A hatchery can buffer water chemistry while excluding small operators; a fishery can maintain aggregate catch while home-port revenue falls; and a decline attributed partly to acidification can still require action on other stressors.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Ocean-acidification monitoring, biological research, hatchery adaptation, fisheries management or coastal planning is not by itself protected seafood security, fair income, cultural food continuity, reef resilience or equitable coastal adaptation.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **ocean-acidification-science-to-coastal-food-livelihood-cultural-continuity-and-equitable-adaptation**. The strongest next acquisition is a named species/fishery/community chain linking chemistry, co-stressors, biology, catch, aquaculture, price, fishing rights, labor, food use, cultural practice, monitoring, adaptation cost, management decision, benefit and long-term resilience.
