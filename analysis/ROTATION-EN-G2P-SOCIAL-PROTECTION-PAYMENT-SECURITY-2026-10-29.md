# Rotation EN — Government-to-person payments, social protection and household security

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** social protection / G2P payments / digital finance / gender / food / children / crisis response / agency

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers food security and benefit access, digital credit, remittances,
automated welfare, legal identity and humanitarian cash. This rotation isolates
the payment rail after eligibility: how a social-protection or humanitarian
entitlement reaches a household, what it costs to receive and use, and whether
it becomes actual security, agency and resilience.

A person can be eligible but not paid, paid but unable to cash out, credited but
unable to use the account, or paid on time while the amount is erased by food
prices, debt, fees, travel, coercive household control or a shock. Digitalization
can reduce leakage and waiting while creating device, connectivity, identity,
privacy, agent, literacy and grievance barriers. The unit is a named recipient’s
complete payment-and-use route, not a budget, enrolment, account or transfer
volume.

## Research question

When a government, humanitarian agency, bank, mobile-money provider or social
registry digitizes cash transfers, pensions, child benefits, disability support,
emergency payments or cash-plus programmes, under what conditions does the
transfer arrive safely, predictably and affordably, remain under the intended
recipient’s control, and improve food, care, health, schooling, debt, housing,
work, resilience and bargaining power without exclusion, surveillance or
transferred costs?

## Conversion chain

```text
poverty / shock / child or disability need / eligibility / crisis
  -> registry, identity, entitlement, payment and G2P design
  -> bank, mobile-money, agent, post-office, cash-out or home-delivery encounter
  -> payment received, usable, timely, private, safe and correctable
  -> food, care, health, schooling, housing, debt, work and shock resilience
  -> household agency, women’s control, trust, voice and remedy
```

The unit must be a named household, recipient, payment, account, agent, service
or crisis period with dates, location, amount, fees, travel, device, identity,
cash-out, use and grievance fields. Enrolment, account ownership, payment
processing, transaction volume or programme reach is an intermediate signal—not
proof of receipt, affordability, agency or household security.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. G2P can be an entry point to financial inclusion, not the end state

World Bank Global Findex 2025 analysis reports that digital G2P payments can
drive account ownership and that women receiving government payments may gain
experience using accounts. It also shows divergence: women’s gains in account
ownership and payment digitalization are not automatic across countries, and
receiving a payment is only a first step toward active use, saving and broader
financial resilience.

The conversion test must therefore separate payment receipt, account use,
withdrawal choice, savings, merchant payment, debt, control and later resilience.

Source: [World Bank analysis of payments and women’s financial inclusion](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/the-power-of-payments--what-s-driving-women-s-financial-inclusio).

### 2. A modern payment architecture requires choice, protection and redress

World Bank G2Px guidance says recipients should be informed about payment
timing, amount, method and costs; be able to access funds; have secure funds and
data privacy; and have clear, quick and responsive grievance redress. It also
calls for recipient-centred design, gender attention and alternatives for people
who face barriers to digital payments.

This closes the service-design mechanism but not actual recipient experience.
The packet must record failed attempts, agents, fees, travel, fraud, account
freezes, privacy concerns, household control and whether complaints resolve the
problem.

Source: [World Bank Next Generation G2P Payments](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099600110202238143/pdf/P173166068e4220430a0ff03279b01c83db.pdf).

### 3. Payment delivery is a social-protection capability during shocks

World Bank evidence on delivery systems describes digital IDs, social registries
and payment platforms as enabling faster crisis response and more integrated
service delivery. It reports examples of expanded electronic payments and
shorter processing times, while the relevant outcome remains whether households
receive usable support at the time and place of need.

Speed and scale can be valuable during drought, conflict, displacement or price
shock, but they can also amplify wrong eligibility records, exclusion and
payment failure. The comparison must include people who were missed or unable
to cash out.

Source: [World Bank delivery systems for social protection](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2025/09/19/digital-delivery-systems).

### 4. Cash-plus links payment to nutrition, care and local services

UNICEF’s Sri Lanka case documents a nutrition-sensitive cash transfer programme
for families with young children during economic crisis, combining monthly cash
with nutrition education and health access. It explicitly examines challenges in
using cash effectively for nutrition and highlights the importance of
community-based care.

Angola’s GRASSP case similarly links cash transfers with birth registration,
early-childhood development, adolescent empowerment and sanitation. These are
useful mechanisms, but they do not prove that the same households retained
nutrition, service access, control or resilience after the programme.

Sources: [UNICEF Sri Lanka cash-plus/nutrition case](https://www.unicef.org/srilanka/reports/building-synergies-between-nutrition-and-cash-transfers)
and [UNICEF Innocenti GRASSP Angola](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/grassp-angola).

### 5. Recipient geography and disability alter what “paid” means

UNICEF’s Yemen payment-cycle evidence describes home outreach for recipients who
could not travel to fixed payment sites, including a wheelchair user. This
illustrates a recurring conversion gap: a payment can be authorized and
available while distance, mobility, insecurity, agent scarcity or care duties
make collection impossible.

The route must include time and cost to receive, safety, accessibility,
substitute collectors, privacy, household gatekeeping and the option of home or
local payment.

Source: [UNICEF Yemen unconditional cash-transfer payment cycle](https://www.unicef.org/yemen/press-releases/unicef-and-partners-launch-19th-payment-cycle-current-unconditional-cash-transfer).

### 6. Digital payment can reduce or reproduce gendered control

World Bank guidance calls for gender-intentional G2P design, recipient education,
multiple payment methods, safeguards and redress. The Findex evidence shows
that women may open accounts for government payments but may also withdraw the
full balance, reflecting the surrounding cash ecosystem and possible preference
or constraint.

Account ownership cannot be treated as women’s economic empowerment. The test
must observe who controls the phone, PIN, account, cash, spending decision,
savings and complaint, as well as risks of intimate-partner coercion or
community surveillance.

### 7. Global scale shows architecture, not equal receipt

The World Bank’s 2026 stocktaking covers 383 social-assistance programmes in
123 countries. It finds digital payments widely used but mixed digital/manual
systems still common, with payment methods, provider choice and account
functionality varying across programmes. This is a valuable comparative map,
but its scale does not join a named recipient to full-value receipt, cash-out,
use, control or remedy.

Source: [World Bank Digitalization of Social Assistance Payments: A Stocktaking](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099100826121526083).

### 8. Digital transition needs layered choice and local access

The World Bank’s 2026 Sahel adaptive-social-protection update reports that
digital payments rose from 20% to 64% of payments between 2021 and 2025, while
describing a Senegal pilot with layered payment options and risk controls for
people with limited phone access, IDs or agent proximity. This closes a named
design response to exclusion; it does not prove that every intended recipient
received, cashed out or retained the intended value.

Source: [World Bank climate-shock-responsive delivery systems](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/sahel-adaptive-social-protection-program-trust-fund/thematic-areas/climate-shock-responsive-delivery-systems).

### 9. Cash-plus can create measurable human-capital results

The World Bank’s 2026 Tanzania PSSN III account reports a productive cash-plus
model combining transfers with savings, child health and education and
self-employment support; it reports a six-percentage-point increase in school
enrollment, improved food security and greater health-service use. These are
important named results, but the account does not close payment-level timing,
fees, control, subgroup variation, grievance, attribution or durability.

Source: [World Bank Tanzania Productive Social Safety Net III](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2026/03/31/the-tanzania-productive-social-safety-net-iii-pssn-iii).

### 10. Payment predictability can matter more than payment modality

World Bank social-protection evidence cautions that cash or digital modality
alone does not automatically produce food security, dietary diversity,
empowerment or financial inclusion. Choice, infrastructure, literacy,
authentication fallbacks and the reliability of reaching cash or services can
matter more than the nominal channel. This is a direct counterexample to
digital-first promotion based on transaction speed alone.

Source: [World Bank payment-mechanism evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099061324164535535/pdf/P176585149bfc00b2199421049786684bc5.pdf).

### 11. Government payment systems are a public infrastructure question

The World Bank’s 2026 payment-systems overview frames government as typically
the largest payer or payee and emphasizes inclusive channels, beneficiary choice
and convenience. That broadens the unit from a programme’s transfer process to
the national payment infrastructure on which social protection, emergency
response and public services depend, while leaving recipient-level reliability
and remedy open.

Source: [World Bank Payment Systems](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/05/15/payment-systems).

## Bounded proposition 145

**A social-protection entitlement, cash transfer, pension, child benefit,
disability payment, humanitarian cash programme, G2P digitization, bank account,
mobile-money wallet, social registry or cash-plus service is not by itself
receipt, affordability, food security, care continuity, schooling, housing
security, women’s agency, financial inclusion, crisis resilience or trust.**
Enrolment, eligibility, budget, account ownership, payment processing, transfer
volume, transaction speed, cash-out availability, programme reach or service
linkage do not establish that a named recipient received the full value on time,
could safely and privately use it, controlled the funds, obtained remedy, or
converted the payment into durable household security.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Payment is a relationship with the state.** Timing, respect, transparency,
   choice and correction shape whether people experience social protection as a
   right or discretionary charity.
2. **The rail is geographically embodied.** Agents, banks, phones, connectivity,
   roads, safety and cash-out liquidity decide who can turn digital value into
   food, care or rent.
3. **Household control is cultural and political.** Who owns the account and
   decides spending can reproduce or redistribute gender, age, disability and
   kinship power.
4. **Cash-plus is a local ecosystem.** A transfer cannot purchase nutrition,
   schooling or care where markets, services, prices or transport are absent.
5. **Digital efficiency can hide exclusion.** Faster processing can make an
   error or suspension faster, broader and harder to contest.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A digital transfer can increase account ownership while recipients immediately
  withdraw because merchants, fees, insecurity or household practice make cash
  more usable.
- A payment can arrive on schedule while inflation, debt, rent or food scarcity
  erases its real value.
- An account-based system can reduce fraud for some while excluding people with
  poor connectivity, inaccessible biometrics, no phone, disability or a record
  mismatch.
- Cash-plus can improve child diets in one setting while local prices, service
  quality or caregiver time prevent the same conversion elsewhere.
- A home-delivery exception can protect a disabled recipient while creating
  privacy, coercion or security risk if the collector is not trusted.
- High programme coverage can hide unpaid fees, failed cash-outs, agent travel,
  account freezes, delayed appeals and recipients who never complete a payment.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. World Bank and
UNICEF evidence supports the G2P, inclusion, cash-plus, gender, accessibility
and shock-response mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because
public sources rarely follow the same recipient from eligibility through payment
receipt, use, household control, material security, resilience and remedy.

## Next test

Follow one recipient in a regular programme, one recipient in a shock response,
and one eligible person who fails at a payment stage. Compare urban/rural,
connected/low-connectivity, woman/man, disability and migrant or displaced
routes. Record at least two payment cycles and one failed, delayed or disputed
transaction.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the G2P, financial-inclusion, recipient-protection,
shock-response, cash-plus, gender and accessibility mechanisms. It does not
close named receipt, full-value use, household control, food/care/schooling/
housing outcomes, resilience, privacy, coercion, fees or remedy. Those remain
acquisition targets.
