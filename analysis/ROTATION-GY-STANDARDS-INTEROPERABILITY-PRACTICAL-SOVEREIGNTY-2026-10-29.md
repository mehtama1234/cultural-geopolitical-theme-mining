# Rotation GY — standards, interoperability and practical sovereignty

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technical standards, interoperability, digital public infrastructure, data sovereignty and inclusive cross-border systems

## Why this rotation exists

Interoperability is often presented as a technical achievement: systems use the
same protocol, data model or interface and can exchange information. In
practice, standards also distribute power. They determine whose terminology is
encoded, which languages and accessibility needs are supported, who can afford
compliance, who controls certification and who can exit a vendor or network.
A system can be compatible on paper while remaining unusable, unaffordable,
unsafe or politically dependent for local institutions, small firms and
communities.

## Research question

When governments, standards bodies, platforms, firms and international
organisations establish technical or legal interoperability, under what
conditions does it create practical choice, durable local capability, secure
service continuity and data sovereignty rather than formal connection,
concentration, exclusion or dependency?

## Conversion chain

```text
standard / protocol / schema / API / certification / data-space rule
  -> systems can connect with shared semantics, rights, security and support
  -> local institution, firm or person can adopt, use, switch and maintain the connection
  -> service, trade, research or public value improves without disproportionate burden
  -> capability, choice, control and sovereignty persist across vendors, borders and shocks
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD's Digital Government Outlook 2026 describes interoperability as a
   governance issue requiring shared rules, semantic standards, technical tools,
   stewardship and operational accountability. It reports that systems exist in
   many OECD countries but use is uneven, especially below central government.
   A connected system is therefore an infrastructure signal, not proof of an
   integrated user journey.
2. OECD reports that data-interoperability systems are present in 30 of 36 OECD
   countries, yet lower usage, institutional silos, regulatory fragmentation and
   maintenance gaps limit practical effect. This directly distinguishes
   availability from adoption and service continuity.
3. OECD technical guidance defines interoperability across semantic, legal and
   technical dimensions and links it to findability, accessibility and
   reusability. A machine-readable interface without rights, provenance,
   language, accessibility or durable maintenance can still fail at the human
   or institutional boundary.
4. The OECD International Data Spaces case shows how standards can combine
   technical, organisational and legal safeguards while preserving data
   providers' control over access rights. This is a positive sovereignty
   mechanism, but a framework does not establish that smaller or less-resourced
   providers can negotiate terms, verify compliance or switch providers.
5. ITU's Global Connectivity Report 2025 identifies affordability, skills,
   devices, security, resilience and national data ecosystems as continuing
   conditions for meaningful connectivity. Interoperability built on unequal
   access can widen the value gap even when the protocol is globally available.
6. WTO's 2025 World Trade Report argues that AI and digital trade can reduce
   costs and expand market access, but infrastructure, skills and capability
   gaps can distribute benefits unevenly. Common digital-trade rules and
   standards require capacity-building and policy choices if developing and
   least-developed economies are to capture value rather than merely comply
   with systems set elsewhere.

## Bounded proposition

**A technical standard, open protocol, API, data schema, interoperability
platform, certification, data space, digital-trade rule or connected system is
not by itself practical choice, affordable access, service continuity, data
sovereignty, local capability, inclusive trade, cultural inclusion or durable
public value.** Systems can exchange data while named people, local agencies or
small firms remain unable to understand, adopt, maintain, secure, correct,
switch or govern the connection, or while the standard embeds an external
language, vendor, jurisdiction or business model.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Standards are cultural and geographical artefacts. A data model decides which
categories, names, identities, calendars, disability needs and languages fit
the system. An international rule may simplify cross-border exchange while
flattening local law, customary authority or informal economic practice. A
municipality may technically adopt a standard but lack staff, broadband,
procurement leverage, cybersecurity capacity or funds to maintain it.

Practical sovereignty is not isolation. It means the ability to participate on
fair terms, inspect and adapt the system, retain lawful control over data,
switch or interoperate without punitive cost, and develop local skills and
institutions. The relevant unit is the user, local agency, small firm or
community attempting a real transaction or service journey—not the standard's
publication or the number of connected systems.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Open standards can reduce lock-in, lower duplication and enable smaller firms
  or public institutions to connect; the packet does not equate standards with
  dependency.
- A single integrated system may be cheaper and easier to secure than many
  systems, while also concentrating failure, data and vendor power.
- Certification can improve trust and quality but impose costs that exclude
  small providers or local-language solutions.
- Data sovereignty can protect rights and autonomy but, if interpreted as total
  localisation, may reduce beneficial research, trade or emergency cooperation.
- Compatibility does not guarantee meaningful use. Test accessibility,
  language, latency, fees, maintenance, security, correction, exit and the
  distribution of benefits separately.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official sources show that
interoperability depends on governance, semantics, rights, maintenance,
capacity and access, and that standards affect cross-border economic and public
value. Promote as **Tier 2 conversion question**:
standards-to-interoperability-and-practical-sovereignty, requiring named
evidence from specification through adoption, user operation, maintenance,
switching, service effect and local control.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | standard, protocol, API, certification, data space, digital-trade rule or connected system | Standards and interoperability expansion are well documented |
| Mechanism | semantics, legal rights, security, governance, cost, skills, maintenance and certification | Conditions are documented; local implementation varies |
| Operation | named agency, firm or person adopts, exchanges, corrects, maintains or switches | Country and sector examples exist; user-level route evidence is limited |
| Material consequence | completed service, lower burden, trade/research value, continuity or avoided failure | Same-user or small-firm outcomes are rarely joined to standards data |
| Meaning | trust, accessibility, cultural fit, dignity, legitimacy and belonging | Design principles are visible; lived meaning is under-measured |
| Power | agenda setting, certification, vendor dependence, exit, data control and local capability | Formal standards governance is visible; realized sovereignty remains open |

## Next test

Select three cases: a public-service interoperability system, an international
data-space or research-data standard, and a cross-border digital-trade or supply-
chain standard. Trace one local agency, small firm and user from specification
and procurement through adoption, language/accessibility, transaction, error,
maintenance, security, correction, switching and benefit. Record who set the
standard, who paid for compliance, who controlled certification and whether a
local alternative could interoperate without punitive cost.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for the governance conditions of interoperability,
uneven implementation, data sovereignty mechanisms, connectivity barriers and
the potential for digital trade and public-service value. It is weaker for
comparable same-unit evidence that standards produced durable local capability,
choice, affordability or sovereignty across geographies. This remains a Tier 2
research opportunity, not a promoted causal finding.
