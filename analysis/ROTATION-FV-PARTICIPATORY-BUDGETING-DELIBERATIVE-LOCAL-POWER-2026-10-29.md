# Rotation FV — participatory budgeting, deliberation and local power

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question
**Research lane:** social, cultural, geographical, economic and governance systems

## Why this rotation exists

Citizen participation is often counted through consultations held, people
invited, ideas submitted, votes cast or dashboards published. Those measures
can hide who participated, whether the agenda was open, whether officials were
obliged to respond, whether resources were actually allocated and whether
under-represented people gained durable power. Participatory budgeting and
deliberative processes make the conversion from voice to allocation unusually
visible.

## Research question

When a government, city, donor or institution creates a participatory budget,
citizen assembly, consultation, social audit or co-design process, under what
conditions does it produce representative agenda power, implemented public
goods, improved services, accountability and durable trust rather than symbolic
participation, elite capture or consultation fatigue?

## Conversion chain

```text
invitation / information / proposal / deliberation / vote / audit
  -> named person or group enters a decision and resource process
  -> priority, allocation, implementation, monitoring or official response
  -> public good, service, burden, agency, inclusion or exclusion outcome
  -> trust, legitimacy, collective capacity or refusal
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD Government at a Glance 2025 records 716 deliberative processes across
   28 OECD countries from 1979 to 2023, involving more than 80,000 randomly
   selected citizens. The scale demonstrates institutional experimentation, not
   that all processes were representative, consequential or durable.
2. OECD's 2025 participation review argues that many people feel unheard despite
   numerous opportunities. It identifies targeted design, lower barriers,
   citizen and official capacity, impact and accountability as conditions for
   meaningful participation.
3. Participatory-budgeting evidence from Tallinn shows a concrete design:
   residents propose and vote on local projects, expert review filters
   feasibility, districts receive allocated funds, and the city evaluates and
   adapts the process. This is a bounded case, not a universal causal result.
4. World Bank evidence describes participatory budgeting as potentially linked
   to pro-poor spending, service access, tax revenue and lower inequality, while
   also emphasizing the need for resources, local fiscal authority and sustained
   institutional support.
5. World Bank participatory-governance material warns that representative voices
   do not automatically emerge organically; participation can reproduce civil-
   society failure, power imbalance or exclusion unless recruitment and support
   are designed deliberately.
6. World Bank and OECD evidence distinguishes information from influence:
   dashboards or transparency may increase awareness and participation for some
   groups without changing perceived ability to influence government or changing
   public-service outcomes.

## Bounded proposition

**A public consultation, citizen assembly, participatory-budgeting platform,
community meeting, open-data dashboard, social audit, public hearing, proposal
vote or co-design workshop is not by itself representative voice, agenda power,
implemented public goods, better services, inclusion, accountability or durable
trust.** Attendance, submissions, votes, transparency or official acceptance do
not establish that named residents shaped the decision, that funds reached the
promised project, that excluded groups gained power, or that services and
relationships improved.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

- **Participation has a place:** transport, safety, timing, childcare, language,
  disability, digital access and local political history shape who can enter.
- **Representation is constructed:** the loudest or most connected participants
  are not automatically the community; recruitment, facilitation and support
  determine whose priorities become legible.
- **Budget power matters:** a forum with no discretionary funds or response
  obligation can be expressive but not allocative.
- **Implementation is cultural:** a project selected by vote may fail if it does
  not fit local maintenance, land, livelihood, language or customary authority.
- **Conflict can be productive or harmful:** deliberation may surface trade-offs
  and build shared understanding, but can also expose minorities to retaliation,
  stigma or majoritarian override.
- **Trust requires feedback:** people need to see reasons, changes, delivery,
  monitoring and remedy—not only a final announcement.

## Counterexamples and cautions

1. High participation can reflect repeated participation by a small, advantaged
   group rather than broad representation.
2. A winning project can be implemented while producing little measurable change
   in service quality, inequality or belonging.
3. Digital voting can expand reach while excluding people without devices,
   connectivity, identity documents or language access.
4. Expert feasibility review may protect public value but can also remove
   community priorities before the ballot without a transparent appeal.
5. Participatory budgeting may improve local projects while leaving major
   revenue, land, debt or procurement decisions outside citizen control.
6. Deliberation can increase legitimacy for a decision people ultimately oppose;
   acceptance is not proof of justice or material benefit.
7. Transparency information may increase participation among people who learn
   their area performs poorly while leaving perceived influence unchanged.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote the Tier 1 distinction that participation must be assessed through
representation, authority, allocation, implementation, feedback and remedy—not
through invitation or attendance alone. Keep the following as Tier 2:
**participatory-governance-to-local-power-and-public-value conversion**. The
next evidence must name participants and excluded groups, the decision rule,
discretionary funds, official response, implementation, service outcome and
later trust or refusal.

## Next test

Build a city or district participatory-budget ledger covering recruitment,
demographics, proposals, deliberation, feasibility exclusions, voting,
allocation, procurement, implementation, maintenance, beneficiary use,
complaints and later budget cycles. Compare winning and non-winning places,
participants and non-participants, and a process with formal response duties.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What is currently supported | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | participation programs, assemblies, consultations, votes and transparency tools | local awareness and perceived relevance |
| Mechanism | information, recruitment, deliberation, voting, allocation, audit and feedback | representation, power imbalance and retaliation |
| Operation | person/group, proposal, forum, budget line, project and decision can be named | repeated participation and implementation pathway |
| Material consequence | allocation, project, service, burden, maintenance and public-good effects are relevant | named receipt, quality, affordability, inclusion and durability |
| Meaning | voice, dignity, recognition, fairness, belonging and legitimacy are central | participant attribution and contested interpretation |
| Power | agenda, budget, veto, reason-giving, monitoring and exit alter leverage | durable local authority, accountability and institutional learning |

## Source boundary

The packet closes discovery and framing for participatory budgeting and
deliberative governance. Public sources support process scale, design
conditions, case architecture, inclusion risks and the information-to-influence
gap. They do not by themselves establish representative voice, changed
allocation, delivered public value, service improvement or durable trust.
