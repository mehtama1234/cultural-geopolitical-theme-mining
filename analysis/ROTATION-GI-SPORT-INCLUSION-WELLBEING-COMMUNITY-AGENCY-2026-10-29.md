# Rotation GI — sport, inclusion, wellbeing and community agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** cultural, social, geographical, health, youth and community systems

## Why this rotation exists

Sport is often credited with health, education, social cohesion, leadership,
peace and economic opportunity. Those possibilities depend on who can enter,
afford the equipment, travel safely, remain free from abuse, shape decisions and
continue participating. A stadium, team, event, grant or participation count
can coexist with gender barriers, disability exclusion, unsafe safeguarding,
commercial pressure, unequal facilities, displacement or the marginalization of
traditional and informal play.

## Research question

When governments, schools, clubs, federations, funders or community groups
invest in sport and physical activity, under what conditions does it produce
repeated, safe and inclusive participation, health, belonging, skills,
livelihood or community agency rather than a facility, event, short-term reach
or elite pathway without durable public benefit?

## Conversion chain

```text
facility / programme / coach / team / event / funding / policy
  -> person can reach, afford, enter and safely remain in sport
  -> repeated participation, relationships, learning, health and voice
  -> wellbeing, inclusion, skills, livelihood or community resilience
  -> belonging, leadership, cultural continuity or collective agency
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. UNESCO's 2026 Sport and Physical Activity Impact Framework for India was
   developed with more than 2,000 learners across eight states and measures
   health and wellbeing, education and lifelong learning, livelihoods and
   economic growth, and equality and social inclusion. Participants reported
   strong perceived benefits, but gender-stereotype barriers remained; perceived
   benefit is not a causal or universal outcome.
2. UNESCO's framework reports that only 58% of respondents said they had tried
   new things despite gender stereotypes and 58% said they spoke up when they
   witnessed discrimination. This illustrates why participation and agency
   should be measured separately.
3. WHO Europe's 2025 guide warns that sport participation does not automatically
   produce better health: injury, exclusion, discrimination and unhealthy
   sponsorship can undermine benefits. It recommends inclusion, mental health,
   injury prevention, context-specific design and member participation in
   decision-making.
4. UNESCO's 2025 safe-sport consultation gathered inputs from 138 athletes,
   whistleblowers, bystanders and advocates across 50 countries and stresses
   that safeguarding policies need accountability capable of preventing and
   redressing harm, not only paper commitments.
5. UNESCO's 2025 emergency case studies across 22 countries show sport used
   for peacebuilding, mental health, gender equity, disability inclusion, social
   cohesion and youth engagement. Case studies demonstrate plausible mechanisms,
   not a general estimate of durable outcomes.
6. UNESCO's International Charter frames inclusive sport as a right and calls
   for non-discriminatory allocation of resources, safe opportunities for women,
   girls, older people, disabled people and Indigenous people, and protection of
   traditional and Indigenous games. A rights standard does not prove local
   access, repeated use or community control.

## Bounded proposition

**A stadium, field, club, team, sports event, participation target, coaching
programme, medal, grant, safeguarding policy or physical-activity campaign is
not by itself inclusive participation, health, wellbeing, belonging, skills,
livelihood, safety or community agency.** A facility opened, participant
registered, event delivered or athlete supported does not establish that named
people could safely return, afford equipment and travel, influence decisions,
avoid discrimination or gain durable individual and collective benefit.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Sport carries cultural meaning through traditional games, neighbourhood play,
school identity, migration, gender norms, disability and national or regional
belonging. A programme imported from another place may not fit local time,
climate, safety, language, religion, family responsibilities or informal play.
Women and girls may face mobility and safety barriers; disabled participants may
need equipment and human support; young people may need voice rather than adult
representation; Indigenous and local games may be treated as heritage without
authority or benefit. Inclusion requires access, safety, leadership and the
right to shape what counts as sport.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Sport can improve health, relationships and skills; the proposition is not
  that investment lacks value.
- Participation counts can hide repeat drop-out, unequal intensity, coercion,
  unsafe conditions or the exclusion of people who never register.
- Elite success or event visibility may inspire participation while diverting
  land, funding and attention from local facilities and informal activity.
- A safeguarding policy may reduce harm when enforced, but can also remain
  symbolic without confidential reporting, survivor support and accountability.
- More activity is not always better health if injury, overtraining, stress,
  unhealthy sponsorship or unsafe environments are present.
- Sport can build cohesion for some while reproducing nationalism, racism,
  gender exclusion, ableism or conflict for others.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: sport investment is a conversion problem from
capacity to safe repeated participation, wellbeing and agency. Keep the named
sport-to-durable-inclusion-and-community-power chain as **Tier 2** until
longitudinal, subgroup and place-specific evidence closes the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | facility, programme, event, funding, participation or policy | strong UNESCO and WHO evidence |
| Mechanism | cost, travel, safety, equipment, coaching, norms, disability access and voice | strong bounded inclusion evidence |
| Operation | named people enter, participate, return, lead and report harm safely | partial; framework and consultation evidence exist |
| Material consequence | health, learning, skills, livelihood, safety or social connection | not closed for a general sport claim |
| Meaning | belonging, identity, dignity, cultural fit and confidence | not closed; requires participant-informed follow-up |
| Power | leadership, resource allocation, safeguarding accountability and community agency | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Follow named participants across one programme for a full season, comparing
gender, age, disability, income, language, migration and neighbourhood. Measure
reach, repeat attendance, cost, travel and safety, injury, discrimination,
voice, leadership, wellbeing, school/work effects and drop-out. Add confidential
safeguarding and community interviews; compare formal sport with informal and
traditional activity rather than assuming facility participation captures local
culture.

## Source boundary

The public record closes sport’s plausible health, education, social-inclusion,
safeguarding and participation mechanisms. It does not justify a universal
claim that sport investment produces durable wellbeing, inclusion, livelihood,
cultural continuity or community agency for named participants. Those later
outcomes remain acquisition targets.
