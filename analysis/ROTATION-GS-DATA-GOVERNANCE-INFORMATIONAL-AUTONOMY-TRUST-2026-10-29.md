# Rotation GS — data governance, informational autonomy and trust

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** data governance, public administration, privacy, trusted intermediaries and cross-border digital power

## Why this rotation exists

Data strategies, interoperability standards, data spaces, trusted intermediaries
and data-sharing laws are increasingly presented as routes to innovation,
better government and trustworthy AI. But a governance framework is not the
same as a governed encounter. Data may remain siloed, inaccurate, inaccessible
or unusable; a “trusted” intermediary may lack accountability; and sharing may
increase institutional or commercial power without giving people meaningful
notice, control, correction or benefit. The central question is whether data
governance creates informational autonomy and public value across places and
groups, rather than merely creating more data circulation.

## Research question

When governments, firms and intermediaries establish data-sharing rules,
standards or consent mechanisms, under what conditions do named people,
communities and public institutions gain safe control, understandable choices,
reliable services and trusted public value rather than extraction, opacity,
exclusion or unaccountable concentration?

## Conversion chain

```text
data collection / registry / sensor / platform / sharing rule / intermediary
  -> notice, purpose, quality, access, consent, security, correction and accountability
  -> authorised institution or person can use data for a bounded legitimate task
  -> service, decision, research or innovation improves without disproportionate harm
  -> people and communities retain agency, remedy, privacy and benefit over time
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD defines data governance as the technical, policy and regulatory
   arrangements managing data across its value cycle, including creation,
   access, sharing and deletion. It identifies trust and conflicting interests
   as reasons that potential social and economic value has not been fully
   realised. This establishes a mechanism and problem statement, not proof of
   public value in a named locality.
2. The OECD Digital Government Outlook 2026 reports widespread public-sector
   data strategies and metadata standards, while identifying a gap between
   formal governance and routine use, quality management, monitoring and
   practical support. The signal is therefore “governance on paper versus
   operational delivery,” not “data strategy equals better service.”
3. OECD's 2025 study of national statistical offices finds that many are
   expanding into trusted data-intermediary roles, using confidentiality
   safeguards, privacy-enhancing technology and secure research environments.
   It also states that oversight, trust-building and adequate resources are
   essential. A trusted institutional label is a mechanism to test, not an
   outcome that can be presumed.
4. The EU Data Governance Act creates rules for protected public-sector data,
   data intermediaries and data altruism. Its design emphasises neutrality,
   transparency, consent, withdrawal and safeguards, while its practical
   operation still depends on competent authorities, registries, technical
   interoperability and user understanding. A legal route to sharing does not
   establish that contributors can exercise meaningful control or that benefits
   return to them.
5. OECD guidance on data governance for trust says that data may be technically
   open but functionally unusable because of poor metadata, non-standard formats
   and weak documentation. It also identifies privacy, IP, commercial interests,
   restricted access, fragmented mandates and unclear accountability as design
   tensions. This is direct evidence that availability and usability are
   separate stages.
6. OECD's AI-era data-sharing guidance recommends stakeholder engagement,
   public-interest objectives, protection of rights and legitimate interests,
   cross-border trust, FAIR data and appropriate incentives. These requirements
   support a rights-and-governance pathway but do not yet close the chain to
   named citizen correction, service completion, local benefit or durable trust.

## Bounded proposition

**A data strategy, registry, interoperability standard, data space, consent
screen, trusted intermediary, privacy-enhancing technology, data-altruism
scheme, data-sharing agreement or AI data pipeline is not by itself
informational autonomy, privacy, trustworthy use, accurate service delivery,
public value, fair treatment, local control or durable trust.** Data collected,
shared, matched or made available does not establish that named people
understood the purpose, could refuse or withdraw, correct the record, obtain a
benefit, challenge a use, prevent harmful inference or see accountable remedy.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Data governance is experienced through institutions and place: a registry
office, clinic, school, welfare counter, bank, employer, research lab or mobile
network. The same rule can produce different autonomy depending on language,
literacy, disability, connectivity, identity documents, legal status, gender,
community membership and the availability of a human fallback. Cross-border
data flows can make a local person subject to decisions made elsewhere, while
local communities may lack bargaining power over data about their land,
health, work or culture.

Informational autonomy is not simply individual consent. It may include
collective governance, community benefit, data minimisation, purpose limits,
local stewardship, meaningful refusal and the ability to contest an inference.
Trust is also not a survey mood alone: it should be connected to observable
quality, clarity, correction, security, service performance and remedy.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Well-designed data sharing can reduce duplication, improve research and
  support faster, more reliable public services; the packet is not anti-sharing.
- A statistical office or other public intermediary may protect confidentiality
  better than direct disclosure, but institutional trust varies by history,
  subgroup and jurisdiction.
- Privacy-enhancing technology can reduce exposure while enabling analysis, but
  it does not answer purpose, power, data quality, model validity or benefit
  distribution questions.
- Consent may be legally recorded but not practically informed, revocable or
  meaningful where services are essential. Conversely, restricted access can
  protect people while limiting research or innovation.
- Data sharing across borders may generate collective value, but jurisdiction,
  enforcement, vendor dependence and geopolitical asymmetry can alter who can
  audit, correct or exit the system.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official frameworks consistently
identify governance, trust, rights, quality, safeguards and operational support
as conditions for data value. Promote as **Tier 2 conversion question**:
data-governance-to-informational-autonomy-and-trust, requiring named evidence
from collection through use, correction, service effect, benefit and remedy.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | strategy, law, registry, intermediary, standard, data space or pipeline | Official evidence is strong for expanding governance activity |
| Mechanism | purpose, quality, metadata, interoperability, consent, privacy, security and accountability | Frameworks name the controls; local implementation varies |
| Operation | named person, institution or community can access, use, correct, withdraw or challenge | Institutional cases exist; comparable person-level operating evidence is incomplete |
| Material consequence | accurate service, faster research, lower burden, safer decision, benefit or avoided harm | Public evidence rarely joins the same record to a later outcome and subgroup effect |
| Meaning | trust, dignity, fairness, recognition and legitimacy | Trust principles and surveys exist; durable trust after an actual data encounter is under-traced |
| Power | agenda setting, refusal, correction, remedy, exit and benefit distribution | Formal rights are visible; realized bargaining power across jurisdictions remains open |

## Next test

Select three contrasting cases: a national statistical-office intermediary, a
public-service data exchange and a data-altruism or community data project. For
each, trace one named data subject or community and one institutional user from
purpose notice to access, quality check, decision, correction/withdrawal,
benefit, complaint and remedy. Record language, accessibility, jurisdiction,
vendor/intermediary role, retention, security, human fallback and whether the
subject could meaningfully refuse or exit.

## Named intermediary, refusal and public-value refresh

### 7. OpenSAFELY: secure analysis supported COVID-19 risk identification without direct researcher access

The UK Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation describes OpenSAFELY as a secure
analytics platform developed by the University of Oxford's DataLab with NHS
electronic-record providers on behalf of NHS England and NHSX. Researchers run
analyses on large volumes of health records without directly observing or
transferring the underlying data. An early project identified people at higher
risk of dying from COVID-19 with higher accuracy than other approaches, helping
organisations target resources to vulnerable groups.

This is a strong privacy-and-public-value mechanism linked to a named health
use. It closes secure analysis and a risk-identification pathway, not patient
comprehension, correction of source records, consent or refusal, language and
disability equity, clinical receipt, false-negative remedy or whether the
benefit was experienced by the identified people. “Higher accuracy” is not the
same as equitable health outcome.

Source: [UK CDEI, data-intermediary case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unlocking-the-value-of-data-exploring-the-role-of-data-intermediaries/unlocking-the-value-of-data-exploring-the-role-of-data-intermediaries).

### 8. Greater Manchester GP Data Trust: opt-out revealed the social licence problem

Greater Manchester's case study describes a General Practice Data Trust pilot
that began after more than one million people opted out of a national plan to
collect primary-care data. The pilot explored why people opted out and whether
a data trust could make sharing more acceptable. It therefore treats refusal,
public understanding and governance acceptability as evidence rather than
assuming that de-identification alone creates trust.

This closes an important negative and participatory mechanism: people can
withdraw or reject a data-sharing design, and that response can trigger a new
governance model. The pilot does not establish that a data trust achieved
representative consent, changed service quality, reduced exclusion, enabled
correction or produced benefits that returned to patients. Opt-out volume is a
signal of legitimacy tension, not proof of what every person wanted.

Source: [Greater Manchester Combined Authority, GP Data Trust case](https://www.greatermanchester-ca.gov.uk/what-we-do/digital/case-studies/case-study-can-a-data-trust-give-people-the-control-they-want-over-how-their-data-is-shared/).

### 9. ONS intermediary for the Troubled Families evaluation: de-identification and power are not identical

The UK government describes a cross-government evaluation involving data from
149 local authorities and four central departments, with an estimated 1.5
million individual records. The Office for National Statistics acted as a
third-party intermediary: it received identifiable matching information,
created pseudonymous identifiers, and returned a de-identified dataset for
analysis. The case also records that the population was vulnerable, individual
informed consent was difficult because of unequal power, and families were
expected to be able to object through local authorities.

This is a useful institutional case because security, matching quality,
intermediary reassurance and meaningful refusal are all visible in the same
design. It does not establish that families understood the use, could exercise
objection without service pressure, corrected records, received a direct
benefit, or saw an accessible remedy when matching was wrong.

Source: [UK CDEI, public-sector data-sharing case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/addressing-trust-in-public-sector-data-use/addressing-trust-in-public-sector-data-use).

### 10. EU data-altruism register: formal trust infrastructure before demonstrated benefit

The European Commission's register lists recognised data-altruism organisations
under the Data Governance Act, including 101 Genomes and the European Brain
Data Hub in Belgium, Datalog in Spain, Elec-tariffs.ie in Ireland and named
organisations in Austria and Italy. Recognition requires a not-for-profit
character, transparency and safeguards for people and companies that voluntarily
make data available without reward for objectives of general interest.

This closes a legal and institutional route for voluntary data contribution and
public scrutiny. The register does not demonstrate contributor comprehension,
withdrawal in practice, data quality, research or service benefit, equitable
distribution, community governance or remedy after misuse. Registration is an
entry condition, not evidence that altruism produced public value.

Source: [European Commission, EU register of recognised data-altruism organisations](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/data-altruism-organisations).

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for governance principles, legal mechanisms,
intermediary designs, privacy safeguards and the persistent gap between formal
frameworks and operational use. It is weaker for comparable same-unit evidence
that data governance produced durable autonomy, equitable service, local
benefit or trust across geographies. This remains a Tier 2 research question,
not a promoted causal claim.
