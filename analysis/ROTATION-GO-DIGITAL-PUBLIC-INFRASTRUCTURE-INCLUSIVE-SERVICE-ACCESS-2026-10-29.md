# Rotation GO — digital public infrastructure, inclusive service access and local agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** digital government, public infrastructure, social protection, geography, culture and rights

## Why this rotation exists

Digital public infrastructure (DPI) is increasingly framed as a foundation for
identity, payments, data exchange and public/private services. Its existence or
technical interoperability is often treated as evidence of inclusion. In lived
terms, however, people still need a device, connectivity, documentation,
language, accessibility, trust, human fallback and a service that actually
resolves the need. Shared infrastructure can reduce fragmentation or amplify
surveillance, exclusion and vendor dependence if governance and remedy are weak.

## Research question

When governments and partners build digital identity, payment, data-exchange
and shared-service infrastructure, under what conditions does it produce
accessible, safe and completed service journeys for named people and firms
rather than a platform, portal, registration record or interoperability claim?

## Conversion chain

```text
identity / payment / data exchange / standards / portal / service platform
  -> person can authenticate, understand, apply, receive and correct safely
  -> benefit, permit, health, education, tax or livelihood service is completed
  -> time, cost and exclusion fall while privacy and human choice are protected
  -> people can contest, opt out where appropriate and shape public systems
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The World Bank's GTMI 2025 measures core government systems and shared
   infrastructure, online service delivery, citizen engagement and GovTech
   enablers across nearly 200 economies. It reports expansion of online
   portals, payments and digital ID, but the index is a maturity and adoption
   measure, not proof of a completed service or equitable outcome.
2. The World Bank reports that 2.9 billion people lack digital IDs for online
   transactions, around 800 million lack official identification, and access
   gaps are especially acute for women, rural populations, people with low
   digital literacy, persons with disabilities and fragile settings. These are
   structural inclusion constraints, not evidence that every DPI project fails.
3. The World Bank's DPI framework defines common building blocks such as
   digital identity, electronic signatures, payments and data sharing, while
   requiring inclusion, openness, modularity, user-centered design,
   privacy-by-design and governance. Principles close the design standard but
   not the individual service journey.
4. The World Bank's Nepal project combines a citizen portal, social registry,
   data exchange, digital locker and wallet with land-administration services,
   cybersecurity and data governance. This is a plausible use-case chain, but
   project components and financing do not prove adoption, correction, benefit
   receipt or equal access across Nepal's diverse geography.
5. The World Bank's Moldova results narrative reports that reengineered and
   digitized services helped increase e-service use from 24% in 2017 to nearly
   54% by 2025, including growth among women and the bottom 40%. This is useful
   outcome evidence for one country and program, but it does not establish
   universal causality or service quality for every group.
6. UNDP describes inclusive and rights-based DPI as requiring interoperability,
   public-purpose governance, privacy and user-centered implementation, while
   the UN Human Development Report notes that causal evidence linking DPI and
   AI remains limited. This supports keeping infrastructure capability separate
   from agency, trust and material service results.

## Bounded proposition

**A digital identity, payment rail, data exchange, interoperability standard,
portal, social registry, digital wallet, service dashboard or DPI investment is
not by itself inclusive service access, completed benefit receipt, privacy,
choice, affordability, public value, trust or local agency.** A person
registered, authenticated, digitally served or included in a dataset does not
establish that they could understand the route, complete the service, correct
an error, receive the intended value, use a human fallback or influence the
rules governing the system.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

DPI is experienced through language, disability, gender, caste or ethnicity,
migration status, rurality, age, device sharing, connectivity and trust in the
state. A digital locker may be empowering for one user and unusable for a
person without a private phone, stable address or documentation. A national
registry can simplify access while imposing culturally inappropriate identity
categories or exposing community data. Local agents, banks, health workers,
schools and civil-society intermediaries often make the difference between a
digital platform and a usable public service. Inclusion therefore means
multi-channel completion, correction and accountability—not only national
coverage or login counts.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Shared standards can lower duplication, improve portability and expand access;
  DPI is not inherently exclusionary or surveillance-oriented.
- Adoption and usage statistics can reflect repeated low-stakes logins rather
  than successful resolution of a high-stakes need.
- A digital route may improve speed while shifting cost, documentation or
  error-correction burdens onto users.
- Human fallback can protect access but may be understaffed, stigmatizing or
  unavailable in remote places; its existence must be observed.
- Integrated data can improve eligibility and continuity while increasing the
  consequences of false matches, breaches, function creep or vendor lock-in.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: DPI is a conversion problem from shared digital
foundations to completed, rights-respecting and locally usable service access.
Keep the named DPI-to-inclusive-service-and-local-agency chain as **Tier 2**
until person-level, subgroup, place-specific and longitudinal evidence closes
the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | ID, payment, data-exchange, portal, registry, wallet or GovTech investment | strong World Bank, UNDP and UN evidence |
| Mechanism | interoperability, standards, device, connectivity, language, privacy, agents and fallback | strong bounded design evidence |
| Operation | named user authenticates, applies, receives, corrects and completes service | partial; adoption is not completion |
| Material consequence | benefit, permit, care, education, tax, livelihood or time/cost result | not closed for a general DPI claim |
| Meaning | dignity, trust, cultural fit, autonomy, fairness and public legitimacy | not closed; requires lived-experience evidence |
| Power | data control, exit, vendor dependence, agenda influence and institutional accountability | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one high-stakes service in three contrasting geographies and trace the
same user journey across digital, assisted-digital and offline channels. Record
identity proofing, device/connectivity, language, disability access, wait,
failed authentication, payment, data correction, human fallback, completion,
cost, privacy concern, complaint and actual benefit or service receipt. Compare
women, rural users, low-income households, migrants, disabled people, older
people and minority-language communities; audit vendor and data-governance
responsibility at each handoff.

## Source boundary

The public record closes DPI's foundational components, adoption and maturity
signals, inclusion principles, selected country use cases and the existence of
large access gaps. It does not justify a universal claim that DPI produced
completed, affordable, private, culturally legible or equitable service access
for named people, nor that it increased local agency. Those later outcomes
remain acquisition targets.
