# Rotation FH — Cloud sovereignty, public-service continuity and exit capability

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 cloud-sovereignty-to-public-service-continuity-and-local-control conversion question  
**Research lane:** cloud infrastructure / public digital infrastructure / vendor concentration / procurement / interoperability / data control / cybersecurity / sovereignty / essential services / public workforce / geography / exit capability

## Why this rotation

The atlas already covers submarine cables, internet shutdowns, platform market
power, AI data-center water, digital identity and public AI. This packet isolates
the cloud operating layer: whether a government or institution can retain
control over data, workloads, costs, security, skills and exit when essential
services depend on concentrated commercial cloud providers.

Cloud adoption, migration, shared infrastructure, a sovereignty framework,
multi-vendor procurement, compliance certification, uptime, cost savings or a
security designation is an important signal. It does not by itself establish
data control, interoperability, affordable exit, local capability, service
continuity, democratic accountability or resilience for residents.

## Research question

When public agencies place essential services in commercial or shared cloud
environments, under what conditions do they preserve operational continuity,
privacy, security, portability, cost visibility, local skills and meaningful
sovereign control across provider failure, legal conflict, cyber incident,
contract exit or geopolitical disruption?

## Conversion chain

```text
cloud adoption / migration / procurement / data placement
  -> architecture, contract, identity, security, skills and dependency
  -> workload operation, cost, uptime, portability and incident response
  -> resident-facing service continuity, privacy and remedy
  -> local capability, provider choice, exit and institutional accountability
  -> digital sovereignty, geopolitical resilience and public trust
```

The minimum unit is a named agency, workload, dataset, provider, contract,
service-level agreement, public service, incident, user group and place over a
defined time period. A provider contract or cloud region is an operating signal,
not proof that a resident can access a continuous service or that an agency can
leave without material loss.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Cloud is becoming part of essential public infrastructure

OECD’s Digital Government Outlook 2026 reports that nearly all surveyed OECD
countries have strategies to promote public-sector cloud adoption, and that
shared cloud storage and computing environments are widespread. It also
describes digital government infrastructure as crucial to continuity of public
operations and essential services. This elevates cloud architecture from an IT
procurement topic to a public-service and sovereignty question.

Source: [OECD, Digital Government Outlook 2026: digital public infrastructure and data governance](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/digital-government-outlook_0496b2bc-en/full-report/strengthening-digital-public-infrastructure-and-data-governance_2c7323c7.html).

### 2. Procurement and multi-vendor interoperability remain unresolved

GAO’s 2026 review found that agencies reported cloud-cost management,
procurement, authorized-solution, workforce and multi-vendor challenges. It
recommends sharing leading practices for multi-vendor cloud solutions,
containerization and platform interoperability. A strategy can therefore be
formally multi-cloud while remaining operationally dependent on one provider’s
interfaces, skills or contract terms.

Source: [GAO, Cloud Computing: Federal Government Needs to Address Procurement Challenges](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107530).

### 3. Vendor lock-in can be financial, technical and institutional

GAO documents restrictive licensing practices that increased conversion costs,
limited provider or architecture choice and, in one case, required an agency to
pay to regain ownership of data at contract end. It also notes that a nominally
multi-provider strategy can still create lock-in when applications are
configured around one provider and migration becomes cost-prohibitive.

Source: [GAO, Cloud Computing: Restrictive Software Licensing Practices](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107114/index.html).

### 4. Concentration risk can become a systemic point of failure

ENISA’s 2025 investment report identifies concentration risk from reliance on a
small number of dominant service providers and the complexity of shared cloud
responsibility. The risk is not only outage duration: organizations may lose
visibility, control or the ability to coordinate security across layered
providers.

Source: [ENISA, NIS Investments 2025](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-02/NIS%20Investments%202025%20-%20Main%20report.pdf).

### 5. Sovereignty frameworks are emerging but are not outcomes

The European Commission’s 2025 cloud-sovereignty initiative frames sovereign
cloud capability as a public-sector market and control objective. A framework,
tender or jurisdictional data requirement can alter procurement and legal
exposure, but does not itself prove portability, service quality, local skills,
affordable exit or resident-facing continuity.

Source: [European Commission, cloud sovereignty tender and framework](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/commission-moves-forward-cloud-sovereignty-eur-180-million-tender-2025-10-10_en).

### 6. Security control is continuous and operational

GAO’s cloud-data-security work describes Cloud Smart expectations around
continuous monitoring, high-value assets and service-level agreements. A
security authorization or compliance label is therefore only one stage; public
value depends on how an agency detects incidents, restores workloads, informs
users, protects data and preserves alternatives.

Source: [GAO, Selected Agencies Need to Better Protect Cloud Data](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108443/index.html).

## Bounded proposition 164

**A cloud migration, hyperscale contract, sovereign-cloud framework, regional data center, multi-vendor strategy, compliance authorization, uptime promise, cost-saving estimate or security control is not by itself public-service continuity, data sovereignty, privacy, interoperability, affordable exit, local capability, accountability or geopolitical resilience.** Workloads migrated, data stored, providers contracted, controls certified, incidents reported or savings forecast do not establish that named residents can receive essential services, that agencies can recover and switch providers, or that public institutions retain meaningful control over cost, law, data, security and service design.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Sovereignty is operational, not rhetorical.** A national cloud label matters only if institutions can control data, contracts, architecture, skills and recovery decisions.
2. **The cloud has a geography.** Data centers, legal jurisdiction, network routes, energy and water, workforce concentration and procurement markets distribute risk unevenly across places.
3. **Public dependence can be invisible to residents.** A benefit, tax, health, education or emergency service may appear local while its continuity depends on distant providers and specialized foreign skills.
4. **Exit capability is a form of institutional agency.** Portability, interoperable standards, tested backups, documentation and multi-vendor skills determine whether public actors can refuse or switch.
5. **Local control can conflict with resilience.** Sovereign hosting may support jurisdictional control but reduce scale, redundancy, specialized security or affordability if not designed as a system.
6. **Continuity has a social distribution.** During an outage or migration, people with language, disability, mobility or low digital access may experience service loss before the agency records a technical failure.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A multi-cloud contract can diversify providers while preserving dependence on one proprietary data format or identity layer.
- A sovereign data location can satisfy jurisdictional requirements while the provider, software, skills or legal control remain external.
- A cloud outage may be brief at system level but cause missed benefits, delayed care, lost applications or unremedied records for particular users.
- Provider-specific capabilities can be strategically valuable; lock-in is not automatically harmful if dependence is understood, priced and paired with a credible exit plan.
- Open standards and containers can improve portability while increasing operational cost, security complexity and staff requirements.
- Redundant regions can improve recovery while expanding data-sharing, energy, water and jurisdictional exposure.
- Compliance certification can reduce known security risk while leaving affordability, accessibility, privacy comprehension and remedy unmeasured.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for cloud adoption, procurement,
concentration, interoperability, data-control, security and sovereignty
mechanisms. Keep the integrated cloud-sovereignty-to-public-service-continuity-
and-local-control outcome at **Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the
same workload and user group through provider failure, recovery, switching,
legal conflict, resident impact and durable institutional learning.

## Next test

Select one essential public service, one workload and one provider contract.
Map the dataset, identity layer, interfaces, service-level agreement, staff
skills, backup, recovery point, exit clause, alternate provider and user
support. Test or reconstruct a provider incident and follow one resident’s
service path through outage, communication, workaround, restoration, correction
and remedy. Compare a single-provider workload with a genuinely portable one.

## Source boundary

The packet closes public-cloud adoption, procurement, interoperability, lock-in,
concentration, security and sovereignty mechanisms. It does not close named
workload recovery, resident service continuity, privacy harm, affordability,
accessible support, portable migration, local skills, legal conflict, exit cost,
remedy or durable public control. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most cloud sources. Contract, architecture and compliance evidence are kept
separate from resident-facing continuity and meaningful sovereignty.
