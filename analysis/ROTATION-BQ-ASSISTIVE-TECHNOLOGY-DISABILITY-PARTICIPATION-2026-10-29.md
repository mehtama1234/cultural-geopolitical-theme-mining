# Rotation BQ — Assistive technology, disability access and functional participation

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** disability inclusion / assistive technology / health and education / digital access / participation / geographic equity  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 device-to-participation question

## Research question

When assistive products and digital technologies are funded or distributed, what
converts availability into a fitted, maintained, affordable and culturally usable
tool that enables a named person to participate in education, work, care,
mobility, communication and community life?

## Conversion chain

```text
functional need and participation barrier
  -> assessment, product selection and financing
  -> fitting, training, repair, maintenance and accessible environment
  -> sustained daily use with user choice and support
  -> education, work, care, mobility, communication and civic participation
  -> agency, dignity, belonging and durable inclusion
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| WHO/UNICEF Global report on assistive technology | More than 2.5 billion people need assistive products, with access gaps greatest in low- and middle-income countries; the report emphasizes systems, workforce, products and enabling environments. | global need, inequality and system architecture | named person receipt, fit, maintenance, use, participation and agency |
| WHO 2025 mobilizing-support brief | Access can be as low as 3% in some settings; assistive technology investment can yield economic returns, but workforce, rehabilitation and system capacity are required. | access gap and investment rationale | product quality, affordability, fit, repair, user choice and outcome |
| WHO/Europe 2025 assistive-technology capacity assessment | Country assessments examine people, policy, products, provision and personnel, showing that product availability is only one part of equitable access. | capacity framework and country diagnostic | user-level completion, continuity, cultural fit and participation |
| UNICEF 2025 children with disabilities report | Assistive technology can enable children’s participation in education, but inclusion depends on wider barriers and support systems. | child participation mechanism and education relevance | classroom use, teacher/family support, learning, belonging and transition |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** assistive technology is being treated as a universal-health,
  education, social-protection and economic-participation issue rather than a
  specialist device market.
- **Mechanism:** assessment, product selection, procurement, affordability,
  fitting, training, repair, replacement, accessible buildings/digital systems,
  personal assistance, language and user choice determine whether a product works
  in daily life.
- **Operation:** a person receives, configures, learns, uses, repairs or replaces
  a wheelchair, hearing aid, prosthesis, communication aid, spectacles, software
  or other assistive product across real settings.
- **Material consequence:** mobility, communication, learning, work, care,
  safety, health, household labor and participation may improve or fail
  independently of distribution counts.
- **Meaning:** an assistive product can create agency and dignity, but a poor fit,
  inaccessible environment or stigma can turn it into a visible reminder of
  institutional dependence or exclusion.
- **Power:** health systems, insurers, schools, procurement agencies, vendors,
  caregivers and designers define eligibility and product choice; users may have
  limited ability to refuse, customize, repair or replace.

## Bounded finding

> **Assistive-technology availability is not functional participation.** A
> product list, procurement award, device distribution, insurance authorization,
> accessibility standard, training program or national capacity assessment does
> not establish that a named person received a suitable product, could afford and
> maintain it, used it safely, or gained education, work, care, mobility,
> communication, agency or belonging.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Assistive technology is relational infrastructure. The product interacts with a
person’s body, household, language, culture, school, workplace, transport,
health system and built environment. The same device can enable independence in
one place and remain unusable in another because of terrain, electricity,
connectivity, stigma, repair supply, climate, customs or the absence of a
trained provider.

Equity therefore requires user voice and continuity, not only coverage. Rural
and low-income settings may have fewer providers and longer repair routes;
children outgrow products; conflict and disaster interrupt supply; and a
technology chosen without the user can create dependence rather than agency.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A device may improve one function while creating burden, pain, stigma or a new
  accessibility barrier elsewhere.
- More products distributed can reflect unmet need and still be progress, but it
  does not prove fit, use or participation.
- A high-cost product is not necessarily more effective; maintenance and local
  repair may matter more than technical sophistication.
- A person may choose not to use a product because of culture, identity, safety,
  comfort or lack of trust; non-use is not automatically failure.
- Participation gains may be real even when a clinical health outcome does not
  change; the units must be kept separate.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: need, eligibility, product, receipt, fit,
training, maintenance, daily use, environment, participation and agency are
separate stages. Keep device-to-participation claims in Tier 2 until a named
person or cohort ledger joins assessment, product, financing, fitting, repair,
use, context and later functional/social outcome.

## Next test

Select one mobility, one communication/hearing and one education-related
assistive pathway across urban, rural and low-income settings. Record need,
assessment, product choice, user voice, funding, delivery, fit, training,
maintenance, failure/repair, daily use, environment, school/work/care/mobility
encounter, stigma, cost, agency and six- or twelve-month retention. Compare
provider-led and user-led selection while preserving voluntary non-use.
