# Rotation DT — Informal food markets, vendors and food security

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** social / cultural / geographical / household / public health / labour

## Why this rotation

The atlas already includes food-benefit access, food environments, food
sovereignty and food-system resilience. This rotation isolates the urban and
peri-urban market as an operating institution: vendors, informal traders,
traditional markets, street food, water and sanitation, municipal regulation,
household affordability, cultural food practice and worker protection.

Formalisation is often presented as an uncomplicated improvement. It can raise
food safety, water access and public order, but it can also increase fees,
remove vendors, break supply relationships, displace women’s livelihoods,
reduce low-cost culturally familiar food, or move trade into less visible and
less safe locations. The right test is not informal versus formal; it is which
combination of infrastructure, rules, voice and accountability produces safe,
affordable and dignified food access.

## Research question

When a city or health authority relocates, licenses, digitizes, upgrades,
formalizes or polices a street market or vendor system, under what conditions
does this improve food safety, affordability, nutrition, cultural continuity and
worker livelihoods—and who loses access, income, public space, voice or
protection?

## Conversion chain

```text
household food need / livelihood need / public-health risk
  -> market, vendor, water, sanitation, licensing and enforcement policy
  -> sourcing, preparation, selling, inspection, payment and household purchase
  -> safe affordable culturally suitable food + income + social connection
  -> nutrition, health, dignity, resilience and local legitimacy
  -> vendor voice, remedy, maintenance, shock continuity and durable governance
```

The unit must be a named market, corridor, vendor group, household or food
stream. Vendor counts, market construction and training attendance are
intermediate evidence, not proof of consumer safety, nutrition or livelihoods.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Street food is a food-security and livelihood system

WHO and FAO report that an estimated 2.5 billion people eat street food daily.
They describe it as inexpensive and convenient food, sometimes nutritious,
with cultural variety and a major income source, particularly for women. They
also identify food-safety risks and call for interventions across government
levels.

This establishes scale and dual function: the same system is a public-health
exposure route and an access/livelihood infrastructure. Neither side can be
discarded in a clean-up or formalisation policy.

Source: [WHO/FAO, Food safety is everyone’s business in street food vending](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-HEP-NFS-AFS-2022.4)
and [WHO/FAO technical leaflet](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/354468/WHO-HEP-NFS-AFS-2022.4-eng.pdf?sequence=1).

### 2. Informal food systems are connected to formal supply chains

FAO’s food-supply indicator notes that informal food production, processing,
transport and retail connect with formal firms and markets. It describes street-
vended food as contributing to food security for producers, processors and
vendors, while warning that unsafe food can damage consumer confidence and the
income of the whole chain. Women are often a large share of owners or workers.

This defeats a binary map of formal versus informal. A vendor may buy from a
formal retailer, sell a culturally important food, pay informal fees and still
need water and waste services from the municipality.

Source: [FAO food-supply and distribution indicator](https://openknowledge.fao.org/3/cb4034en/cb4034en.pdf).

### 3. Food safety is an infrastructure and governance problem

WHO’s traditional-market work frames markets as economic, cultural and social
institutions and uses a One Health approach. Its Five Keys include keeping
clean, avoiding contamination, keeping food safe, reducing contact with
animals and protecting people. A 2024 Indonesia pilot found gaps in water
quality assessment, cleaning protocols, pest control and live-animal handling;
the programme worked with market workers rather than treating them only as
violators.

The conversion implication is that training without water, drainage, storage,
waste collection and clear rules may not change risk. Conversely, upgrading
infrastructure without worker participation may produce rules that are
unaffordable or impractical.

Sources: [WHO traditional food-market risk mitigation](https://www.who.int/westernpacific/activities/mitigating-risks-in-traditional-food-markets)
and [WHO Indonesia pilot](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news/detail/24-06-2024-managing-food-safety-in-traditional-food-markets--who-pilots-five-keys-strategy-to-raise-hygiene-and-sanitation-standards).

### 4. Informal work is culturally visible but institutionally vulnerable

ILO describes street vendors as part of the urban informal economy and notes
their role in jobs, low-cost goods and urban culture. It also records poor
access to services, infrastructure, finance and social security, along with
harassment, eviction, confiscation and bribery risks in some settings. Street
vending varies by time, location, scale, remuneration and whether it is a
survival strategy or a larger business.

This makes “formalisation” a power question. A permit, relocation or digital
registration can create recognition and services, but can also transfer costs
and reduce flexibility. The ledger must follow rent/fees, hours, police contact,
income, safety, social protection and the ability to return after shocks.

Source: [ILO, The informal economy and street vendors](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/%40ed_emp/%40emp_policy/documents/publication/wcms_212689.pdf).

### 5. Market reform can improve safety while creating access trade-offs

The WHO Indonesia pilot illustrates a positive intervention: health and market
actors worked on hygiene and sanitation in traditional markets, with an
estimated 16,000 such markets serving surrounding communities. At the same
time, a safe market may require capital, water, fees, restricted animals or new
operating hours. If those costs are passed to vendors or consumers, food may
become safer but less affordable or culturally available.

The evidence supports a coupled evaluation, not a single safety score:
foodborne-risk reduction, price, vendor income, women’s control, customer
access, cultural food availability and market continuity must be observed
together.

## Bounded proposition 125

**A market relocation, vendor licence, food-safety training, hygiene campaign,
digital-payment system, street clearance, market hall, inspection regime or
formalisation programme is not by itself safe affordable food, nutrition,
cultural continuity, dignified work or resilient livelihoods.** A market built,
vendor registered, inspection completed, training delivered, payment digitized
or street cleared does not establish that named households could still reach
and afford culturally suitable food, vendors retained income and public-space
access, workers obtained water and social protection, foodborne risk declined,
women retained control, or the market remained open and accountable after a
shock or political transition.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Markets are social infrastructure.** They distribute food, information,
   credit, care, identity and social recognition, not only commodities.
2. **Food safety is co-produced.** Vendors need clean water, waste removal,
   storage, affordable equipment and understandable rules; consumer behaviour
   and inspection also matter.
3. **Location is an affordability variable.** Relocation may increase travel,
   time and transport cost even if stalls are cleaner.
4. **Formalisation redistributes voice.** A recognized association or market
   board can improve bargaining, but registration without representation may
   make enforcement more legible without making it fairer.
5. **Cultural food is a living practice.** Standardization may reduce hazard
   while erasing ingredients, preparation, timing or social relationships that
   make food meaningful and acceptable.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A market can be culturally vibrant and affordable while carrying real
  sanitation or foodborne risks.
- A new formal market can improve water, drainage and inspection while raising
  rents, reducing footfall or excluding unlicensed women vendors.
- Vendor training can improve knowledge without changing water, equipment or
  waste conditions.
- Digital payments can reduce cash theft and broaden transactions while
  excluding people without phones, IDs, bank accounts or stable connectivity.
- Street clearance can reduce congestion in one corridor while shifting vendors
  and customers to a less safe or less accessible location.
- A vendor association may genuinely improve bargaining and safety; informality
  should not be treated as proof of exploitation or food risk.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, FAO and ILO
support the dual food-security/health/livelihood mechanism and the need for
infrastructure and worker-sensitive governance. Keep the integrated outcome as
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same market, vendor and
household through formalisation, food safety, affordability, nutrition, income
and post-shock continuity.

## Next test

Select one traditional market, one street-food corridor and one relocated or
formalized market in contrasting geographies. Track:

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | complaint, outbreak, clearance, upgrade, licence or food-price shock |
| Mechanism | water, sanitation, fee, location, inspection, gender, credit or enforcement pathway |
| Operation | named vendor/household/market, products, hours, route, price and customer group |
| Material consequence | food safety, price, diet, income, hours, eviction, illness, waste or continuity |
| Meaning | dignity, cultural fit, trust, stigma, belonging and attribution |

Follow the same market and subgroups before and after intervention and through

unlicensed sellers, safe markets that remained affordable and unsafe markets
that improved after infrastructure support.

## Named outcome and operating-case refresh

### 6. Indonesia risk-based inspection pilot: prioritisation reached 3,681 vendors

Indonesia's Ministry of Health and WHO piloted risk-based food inspection in
five districts and municipalities. The programme assessed 3,681 vendors using a
risk calculator that combined food profile, mitigation, business size and prior
inspection discrepancies. It covered 159 community health centres and produced
different inspection frequencies for high-, medium- and low-risk establishments;
sanitarian food-safety knowledge rose from 60% to 76%.

This is a useful operating case for proportional enforcement: scarce public
health capacity was allocated by risk rather than treating every seller as an
identical compliance problem. It closes inspection architecture and workforce
capability, not consumer illness reduction, vendor cost, affordability, food
quality at point of purchase, appeal, bribery reduction or durable compliance.

Source: [WHO Indonesia, risk-based food inspection pilot](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news-room/item/02-11-2023-breaking-new-ground--piloting-risk-based-food-inspection-in-five-districts-for-better-food-safety).

### 7. Indonesia traditional-market Five Keys pilot: knowledge and cleaning practices moved

WHO supported market communities in three locations across Semarang, Bandung
and Samarinda. The pilot identified missing water-quality assessment, cleaning
protocols, pest control and live-animal handling practices. Across the sites,
vendors' average knowledge of key hygiene measures rose from 84% to 90%, and
the share reporting kiosk and floor cleaning with water and soap or disinfectant
rose from 70% to 80%.

The case supports a market-community intervention that combines communication,
vendor discussion and local authority engagement. It does not show a reduction
in foodborne illness or zoonoses, and it does not report price, income, vendor
retention, women’s control, customer access or maintenance after the programme.
The metric is self-reported or practice-level change, not a completed health
outcome chain.

Source: [WHO Indonesia, Five Keys traditional-market pilot](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news/detail/24-06-2024-managing-food-safety-in-traditional-food-markets--who-pilots-five-keys-strategy-to-raise-hygiene-and-sanitation-standards).

### 8. Accra street-food survey: women vendors sit inside the school-food geography

FAO's interim Central Accra survey covered 1,700 street-food vendors. It found
that vendors were almost all women, that the activity was concentrated in
poverty-pocket areas and near schools, and that almost half operated near a
school. The survey reported overall hygiene and safety conditions as acceptable
in its sample while also documenting the vendors' role in income, food access
and school-child nutrition.

This named case prevents the food-safety lens from erasing gender, location and
child-consumer exposure. It closes a spatial and livelihood mechanism, but the
interim survey does not establish dietary adequacy, child health, vendor profit,
women’s control of earnings, causal proximity effects or what happened after a
licensing, relocation or enforcement change. “Acceptable” must not be converted
into proof of safe food for every consumer or durable livelihood security.

Source: [FAO, street-food vendors in Accra](https://www.fao.org/gender/success-stories/success-stories-detail/FAO-survey-on-street-food-vendors-in-Accra-finds-women-play-a-central-role/en).

### 9. Pasto resilience plan: El Potrerillo market is a climate and food-security node

UN-Habitat's Urban Resilience Action Plan for Pasto identifies the El Potrerillo
market as a critical hub for local livelihoods and food security. The plan joins
the market to watershed, climate and biodiversity action and explicitly includes
local institutions, universities, community organisations, informal workers and
market actors in shaping long-term resilience.

This expands the packet beyond hygiene and licensing: a market can be a
geographical node connecting food flows, livelihoods, flood or climate exposure
and local institutional capacity. The plan is a design and governance signal,
not evidence that resilience investments reduced prices, protected incomes,
maintained food supply during a shock or gave informal workers binding decision
rights. Those outcomes require a named implementation and follow-up ledger.

Source: [UN-Habitat, Urban Resilience Action Plan for Pasto](https://unhabitat.org/urban-resilience-action-plan-urap-for-pasto-colombia-planning-for-climate-urban-and-biodiversity).

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the food-security, public-health, infrastructure and
informal-livelihood mechanisms. It does not close named nutrition, affordability,
foodborne illness, vendor income, women’s control, displacement, cultural
continuity or post-shock governance outcomes. Those remain acquisition targets.
