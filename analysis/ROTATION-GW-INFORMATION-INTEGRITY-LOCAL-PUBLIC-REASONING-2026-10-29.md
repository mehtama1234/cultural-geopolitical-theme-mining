# Rotation GW — information integrity, local public reasoning and civic agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** information ecosystems, media and information literacy, local journalism, access to information, platform governance and civic agency

## Why this rotation exists

Information integrity is often discussed as a problem of false content or a
platform's moderation response. The more useful unit is the local information
encounter: can a person find timely, relevant and multilingual information,
judge its credibility, compare sources, discuss it safely and use it to make a
personal or civic decision? Information can be accurate but inaccessible,
contextless, unavailable in a local language, suppressed by a shutdown or
undermined by a missing local journalist. Conversely, a correction or media-
literacy campaign can exist without reaching the people most affected.

## Research question

When states, platforms, media, schools, libraries, civil society and community
intermediaries act on information integrity, under what conditions do named
people and groups gain reliable, plural, understandable information and the
capacity to act together rather than merely encounter more content,
fact-checking, regulation or “resilience” language?

## Conversion chain

```text
public-interest information / local reporting / platform distribution / literacy support
  -> person can access, understand, compare, verify and discuss information safely
  -> person or group makes an informed household, health, livelihood or civic choice
  -> institutions receive correction, scrutiny or participation and respond
  -> public reasoning, social resilience and civic agency persist across shocks
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The UN Global Principles for Information Integrity define a desired ecosystem
   around societal trust and resilience, healthy incentives, public empowerment,
   independent and pluralistic media, and transparency and research. These
   principles establish a rights-based direction, not proof that people can use
   information in a named place.
2. OECD's information-integrity work reports that misinformation and
   disinformation can undermine trust, elections, public-health initiatives and
   informed decision-making. Its Truth Quest evidence also finds that perceived
   ability to identify false content is not the same as measured ability; across
   21 countries respondents correctly identified claims about 60% of the time.
   Self-reported confidence is therefore not a substitute for observed
   comprehension or action.
3. UNESCO's information-integrity mapping highlights a geographic paradox:
   people may be affected by digital information systems even when they cannot
   participate fully in the digital public sphere. It also frames integrity as
   accuracy, consistency, reliability, pluralism and human-rights-compatible
   access, not simply content removal.
4. UNESCO's Nepal media-and-information-literacy work connects municipal
   education plans, digital rights, access to information and climate/disaster
   preparedness. This is a positive local mechanism: educators and local actors
   can translate abstract integrity principles into practical skills and public
   information routes. The reported training and plans do not yet prove changed
   beliefs, preparedness or civic behavior.
5. The UNESCO-supported Praia Action Plan for West Africa and the Sahel calls
   for multilingual outreach, public records, non-digital access, libraries,
   archives, statistical institutions, independent media and community
   empowerment. It makes the cultural and geographic conditions explicit, while
   remaining a regional action framework rather than outcome evidence.
6. UNESCO's 2026 reporting on access to information and internet shutdowns shows
   how legal guarantees can fail at implementation through weak oversight,
   appeals, record-keeping, budgets or political interference. A right to
   information is therefore an institutional route that must be tested through
   actual requests, response time, accessibility, correction and use.

## Bounded proposition

**An accurate post, fact-check, media-literacy lesson, public-information law,
local news outlet, platform transparency report, content label, election
briefing, correction, internet connection or information-integrity strategy is
not by itself comprehension, informed choice, public reasoning, social
resilience, civic participation, safety or institutional accountability.** A
claim can be debunked, a source labelled or a right enacted without establishing
that named people received the information in an accessible language, trusted
the process, changed a decision, corrected a local record, coordinated with
others or obtained a response from an accountable institution.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Information integrity is experienced through language, connectivity, media
ownership, local journalism, disability access, literacy, migration status,
gender, conflict, trust history and the presence of community intermediaries.
National fact-checking may not address a local rumour, dialect, customary
authority or disaster route. A platform can moderate globally while missing
local context; a state can publish data while excluding people who lack a
device, document, archive or safe request channel.

The relevant unit is a person or group acting in a place, not an aggregate
engagement count. Civic agency requires a path from information to discussion,
choice, collective action, correction and institutional response. It also
requires pluralism: a single authorised narrative can be accurate in parts yet
undermine independent scrutiny and minority voice.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Reliable public information can save lives during emergencies and support
  informed democratic choice; the packet does not treat all information work as
  ineffective.
- Fact-checking can correct a claim, but repetition, identity threat and
  distrust may limit reach or persuasion; correction rate is not outcome proof.
- Media literacy can build capability, but placing the burden on individuals
  should not excuse platform incentives, media concentration, censorship or
  poor public records.
- Local media may improve accountability while facing financial, safety or
  political pressure. Presence is not independence or continuity.
- Information integrity measures can be misused to suppress dissent. Human
  rights, free expression, pluralism and transparent appeal remain separate
  safeguards.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official sources identify public
empowerment, plural media, access to information, literacy, institutional
capacity and transparency as interacting conditions. Promote as **Tier 2
conversion question**: information-integrity-to-local-public-reasoning-and-
civic-agency, requiring named evidence from access through comprehension,
choice, collective action, correction and institutional response.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | misinformation event, literacy programme, law, local-news intervention, platform rule or public-information release | Global risk and policy activity are well documented |
| Mechanism | language, channel, source diversity, media incentives, algorithmic reach, literacy, access rights and institutional response | Mechanisms are documented; local combinations vary |
| Operation | named person/group finds, understands, verifies, discusses and uses information | Survey and programme evidence exists; same-person operating routes are limited |
| Material consequence | household, health, livelihood, safety or civic decision changes; false claim corrected | Outcomes are rarely joined to the information intervention and subgroup context |
| Meaning | trust, identity, dignity, legitimacy, pluralism and belonging | Trust measures exist; lived legitimacy after a specific encounter is under-traced |
| Power | agenda setting, voice, correction, collective action, media independence and institutional accountability | Principles and rights are visible; realized local power remains open |

## Next test

Select three place-bound cases: a local media-and-information-literacy
programme, a public-information/access-to-information route and an election or
disaster information intervention. Trace one named person or group from source
exposure through language/access, verification, discussion, decision, collective
action, correction and institutional response. Record source diversity, channel,
device, disability, trust history, time to correction, offline alternatives and
whether the group could obtain a remedy without retaliation.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for the global information-integrity problem,
rights principles, measured information-evaluation gaps, multilingual and local
capacity mechanisms, and institutional access-to-information barriers. It is
weaker for comparable same-person evidence that integrity interventions produced
durable local reasoning, safer choices, civic action or accountable response
across geographies. This remains a Tier 2 research opportunity, not a promoted
causal finding.
