# Rotation II — Migrant domestic and care workers, rights and portability

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When ageing, disability and care shortages drive recruitment of migrant domestic and care workers, do migration pathways become safe work with fair pay, training, voice, social protection and family security—or do care systems externalize cost and risk onto migrant women and origin-country households?

## Why this packet matters

Care migration is a transnational social infrastructure. The chain is:

```text
care demand / ageing / disability / household need
  -> recruitment, contract and migration status
  -> arrival, housing and work assignment
  -> hours, pay, safety, training and worker voice
  -> care delivered to older/disabled/children
  -> social protection, health and injury coverage
  -> remittances, family care and return/portability
  -> durable care quality and worker agency
```

Domestic work can be hidden inside private homes and excluded from labor law. A worker’s migration status, employer tie, gender, language and recruitment debt affect whether formal rights are usable. A care shortage statistic is not evidence of a safe or sustainable care system.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| II1 | ILO identifies a global care workforce of 381 million workers, 11.5% of employment, while noting many care workers lack adequate social protection and barriers are especially severe for domestic and migrant workers. | Global scale and protection gap. | Worker-level contract, pay, hours, safety, care quality or portability. |
| II2 | ILO estimates 80 million domestic workers worldwide in 2025, with 66.5 million (83.1%) in informal employment without effective labor or social protection. | Domestic-work scale and informality measure. | Migrant subgroup, household conditions, recruitment, enforcement or outcomes. |
| II3 | ILO reports migrant domestic workers are structurally important to care gaps; domestic workers are at least 25% of care workers, and migration corridors show high dependence with historical labor-law exclusion. | Care-chain and legal-exclusion mechanism. | Named worker receipt of rights, employer behavior, family security or care quality. |
| II4 | ILO’s Kenya assessment surveyed 331 domestic workers and 70 employers providing disability care and found long hours, low wages, limited legal protection, little training, social protection or safety; most workers were women. | Named worker/employer evidence and disability-care burden. | Migrant status, worker remedy, disability-user outcome, retention or policy implementation. |
| II5 | ILO reports Malaysia’s PERKESO efforts to extend social protection to foreign workers, including domestic workers and fishers, while noting challenges in increasing coverage. | National portability/coverage route. | Enrollment, contribution, claim, payment, migrant household adequacy or enforcement. |
| II6 | ILO’s South Asia–GCC work identifies migrant domestic workers from Bangladesh, India, Nepal and Sri Lanka as systematically left without adequate social protection as they move across the corridor. | Named regional portability and gendered mobility gap. | Individual claim, bilateral agreement, benefit receipt, injury/health coverage or return security. |
| II7 | ILO reports Thailand may face a shortfall of 250,000 care workers by 2037 and recommends affordable care, decent conditions, fair migration pathways and recognition/upgrading of care skills. | Destination demand and policy response. | Recruitment practice, migrant worker experience, pay, care quality or source-country effects. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Care migration links ageing societies, disability support, private households, gender norms, recruitment agencies, bilateral agreements and remittance-dependent communities. The worker may provide intimate care while being excluded from the social protection system serving the care recipient. Language, live-in housing, employer control, immigration status and family separation shape usable rights. Ethical recruitment and portability are geopolitical infrastructure, not administrative details.

## Counterexample and source boundary

Recruitment into a shortage occupation is not proof of decent work. A social-protection scheme can exist while domestic workers remain outside coverage. Training a caregiver does not prove fair pay, safe hours, or quality care. Public evidence closes workforce scale, informality, legal exclusion, named Kenya conditions, coverage routes, regional portability gaps, destination demand and resource-centre design, but not a comparable worker–care recipient–family chain through rights, care quality and durable security.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Care-worker recruitment, migration, shortage estimates, training, social-protection policy, resource centres or bilateral pathways are not by themselves safe work, fair pay, care quality, portable protection, family security or worker voice.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **care-migration-recruitment-to-decent-portable-work-and-reciprocal-care-security**. The strongest next acquisition is a named corridor/worker cohort linking care demand, recruitment fee/contract, status, housing, hours/pay, training, care recipient outcome, complaint, social-protection contribution/claim, remittance, family care and return/portability.
