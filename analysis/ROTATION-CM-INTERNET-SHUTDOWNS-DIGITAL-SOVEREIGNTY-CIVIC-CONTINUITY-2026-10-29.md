# Rotation CM — Internet shutdowns, digital sovereignty and civic continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** internet shutdowns / mobile and platform blocking / digital sovereignty / emergency communication / protest and elections / livelihoods / humanitarian access / civic space
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 connectivity-control-to-civic-continuity-and-legitimacy question

## Research question

When states, armed actors, regulators, telecom operators or platforms restrict
internet, mobile, social-media or digital-service access, what converts a stated
security, public-order or sovereignty objective into proportionate protection
without cutting people off from emergency information, health, education,
livelihoods, humanitarian assistance, journalism and peaceful participation?

## Conversion chain

```text
security incident, protest, election, conflict, emergency or platform dispute
  -> shutdown, throttling, service block, telecom order, platform restriction or infrastructure disruption
  -> legal authority, scope, duration, geography, notice, alternatives and operator compliance
  -> loss or preservation of communication, payment, information, aid, work, safety and organizing
  -> emergency response, livelihood continuity, civic participation, rights protection and public trust
  -> legitimate and resilient digital public sphere
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access Now/#KeepItOn 2025 report | At least 313 shutdowns occurred in 52 countries in 2025; conflict was the leading trigger and 75 shutdowns persisted into 2026. | global documented-event signal, geography and trigger pattern | causal harm, undercount, affected-user exposure, recovery, remedy and institutional response |
| OHCHR Internet Shutdowns report | Shutdowns can be complete, partial, throttled or service-specific and have wide impacts on rights; blanket measures raise necessity and proportionality concerns. | definition, legal standard and rights-impact architecture | deployment-level legal review, actual safety effect and affected-person remedy |
| OHCHR civic-space technology report | Internet shutdowns are at the extreme end of government tactics and can have severe direct and indirect implications across human rights and civic space. | mechanism and civic-space boundary | local economic, health, gender, disability, humanitarian and participation outcomes |
| OHCHR Myanmar case evidence | Nationwide and service-specific shutdowns during political crisis restricted expression, assembly and access to information, with concerns about lack of legal justification and proportionality. | named case linking shutdown to civic-space restriction | counterfactual security effect, household/service impact, accountability and restoration |
| Access Now STOP methodology | Shutdown counts depend on technical measurement and contextual information; unreported cases and later updates mean the dataset is not complete. | measurement method and uncertainty boundary | comprehensive exposure, severity, causal outcomes and comparability across countries |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** shutdowns and service blocks recur during elections, protests,
  conflict, public emergencies, exams, security incidents and disputes with
  platforms or telecom operators.
- **Mechanism:** authority, operator, scope, duration, geography, bandwidth,
  service dependency, notice, workaround availability, network topology and
  restoration determine who loses what and for how long.
- **Operation:** an order or disruption blocks a network or service; people,
  journalists, clinicians, businesses, schools and aid organizations attempt
  alternate routes; authorities, operators or courts respond; the service is
  restored, partially restored or normalized as unavailable.
- **Material consequence:** emergency calls, health information, payments,
  wages, education, transport, food, documentation, aid, evidence collection,
  protest coordination and household safety may move independently.
- **Meaning:** a shutdown can be experienced as collective punishment, censorship,
  abandonment or occupation, while authorities frame it as sovereignty or
  security. Connectivity can be a lifeline and a political space, not merely a
  consumer service.
- **Power:** governments, militaries, telecom operators, platforms, courts,
  journalists, civil society, businesses and communities decide who can speak,
  organize, receive help, document harm and reconnect.

## Bounded finding

> **Connectivity control or legal authority to restrict a network is not public
> safety, digital sovereignty or legitimate civic governance.** A shutdown,
> throttle, platform block, telecom order, emergency measure, content restriction
> or restoration announcement does not establish that a named community was
> safer, that emergency and humanitarian communication continued, that economic
> and civic harms were proportionate, or that people received notice, remedy and
> durable access.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Shutdowns redistribute risk through geography and social position. A connected
urban professional may switch providers or use a VPN; a rural household,
refugee, disabled person, informal worker, student, patient or person in a
conflict zone may lose the only route to wages, care, identity documents, family
or safety information. Language minorities and people dependent on community
messaging can become invisible to official response. Digital sovereignty is
legitimate only when it protects people’s agency and public goods rather than
making the population unable to observe or contest state power.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A localized service restriction can reduce a specific technical threat while a
  blanket shutdown creates wider and less measurable harm.
- A shutdown may coincide with violence without causing it; timing, actor and
  counterfactual security effects must be recorded separately.
- Restoring connectivity does not restore lost wages, health, evidence, trust or
  family contact.
- A platform block and a full network shutdown differ in scope but can have
  similar consequences for a community whose essential information is on that
  platform.
- Circumvention tools can preserve communication while exposing users to legal,
  malware, surveillance or financial risk.
- Shutdown counts are not complete and cannot alone estimate affected people,
  duration-weighted harm or public-safety benefit.
- Alternative radio, SMS, satellite, mesh or in-person routes may preserve some
  continuity while excluding people without devices, skills, power or safety.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: restriction, scope, duration, exposure,
service dependency, alternative route, emergency communication, livelihood,
participation, restoration, remedy and trust are separate stages. Keep
connectivity-control-to-civic-continuity-and-legitimacy claims in Tier 2 until a
named shutdown ledger joins the order and network event to affected subgroups,
service loss, workaround, safety outcome, economic burden, restoration and
accountability.

## Next test

Select one election-related shutdown, one conflict/emergency shutdown and one
platform or service block. Record legal order, actor, network, scope, duration,
geography, population, services affected, alternatives, emergency/health/aid
communications, payment and livelihood effects, protests or civic action,
restoration, complaint, judicial review and 3/6/12-month trust and continuity
outcomes. Compare connected and disconnected households and include people who
could not report harm.
