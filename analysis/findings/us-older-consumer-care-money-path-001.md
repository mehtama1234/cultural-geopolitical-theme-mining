# When age hides different kinds of need

## Short answer

People over 65 are not one customer group. Two people of the same age may differ more in health, money, mobility, living arrangement, digital access, and need for care than in age itself.

The careful finding is that age alone can hide both buying power and vulnerability. Better sorting may make a product fit, but it can also exclude someone, charge more, or steer a person toward protection they do not need.

## What the sources show

HBS reports that older adults are a large and varied market, with segments differing in health and activity. It describes evidence that some older adults have high buying power, buy repeatedly, and influence younger family members, while companies often rely on age-based stereotypes.

NBER US survey evidence finds that older adults often report greater economic satisfaction as spending declines. Health, widowhood, and changing enjoyment may reduce the desire to spend. Other NBER work finds that health shocks affect consumption and that older households face income and health risks that change how they use resources.

These sources do not follow the same older customer through a product choice, care decision, price, service result, and later independence.

- [HBS: What Brands Get Wrong About the Over-65 Market](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/what-brands-get-wrong-about-the-over-65-market)
- [NBER Working Paper 30460: Explanations for the Decline in Spending at Older Ages](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30460)
- [NBER Working Paper 27348: Old Age Risks, Consumption, and Insurance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27348)

## The connection

```text
age, health, wealth, and living situation
  -> product need, ability, and willingness to pay
  -> firm design, service, and targeting
  -> independence, care burden, or exposure to bad advice
  -> household money and family decisions
```

Lower spending can be a choice, a sign of better fit, a health effect, a loss, or a lack of money. The same number cannot tell those stories apart.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A person weighs simplicity, trust, price, access, health, transport, and whether a product helps remain independent. A family member may also help choose or pay for it.

**Company and sector:** Firms can design for vision, hearing, mobility, memory, caregiving, and different levels of digital comfort. They can also use loyalty and confusion to sell an expensive or unsuitable product.

**Money and finance:** Count income, wealth, health costs, care, insurance, price, fees, and the time a product saves. Buying power is not the same as money that can safely be spent.

**Public power:** Consumer protection, accessible service, insurance rules, fraud prevention, and care support shape whether older people can choose freely. Age-based rules should not replace evidence about need.

## What would change the finding

- Health, disability, mobility, cognition, living arrangement, and care needs.
- Income, wealth, insurance, debt, price, fees, and financial advice.
- Product access, service quality, digital access, and actual use.
- Whether a purchase improved independence, reduced care time, or created risk.
- Family influence, scams, complaints, refunds, and later household outcomes.
- Differences by age, race, gender, place, education, and social support.

## Next test

Follow older customers for two years across a product or care decision. Join health and living conditions to price, service, use, family help, care hours, money, complaints, and independence. Compare age-based targeting with a design based on the person's actual need.

## Reading rule

Do not use age as a shortcut for need. Do not call lower spending a problem or a success until health, money, choice, and independence are visible.
