# When family care becomes paid-work time

## The argument

Childcare is paid for in more than one currency. Some families pay a large cash bill. Some rely on relatives. Some lose work time when care is too expensive, unavailable, or badly timed. These options are not equally available.

Care can make work possible and still consume much of the pay it enables. Unpaid care can be loving support and still depend on a relative’s health, distance, and schedule. The important question is who supplies the hours, what the care costs, and what the family gives up.

## What the sources establish

The Federal Reserve’s 2024 survey found that 24% of parents living with children under 13 used paid childcare in the prior week. The median weekly payment was $240, about $960 a month; among parents using 20 or more hours, the median monthly payment was $1,400. More than half of paid-care users spent at least half as much on childcare as on housing.

The Fed found that 46% of parents with children under 13 used some unpaid care from someone other than the child’s parent. Relatives, especially grandparents, were common providers. Single parents were about as likely as two-working-parent families to use some care, but less likely to pay for it.

A Census working paper estimates that 23.9% of households with children under 13 paid for care in 2024, averaging $10,520 a year, or 5.6% of household income. Among households reporting problems obtaining adequate care, average work time lost was 65.8 days. That is an average among affected households, not a national average or causal estimate.

Sources: [Federal Reserve care and living arrangements](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-care-work-and-living-arrangements.htm) and [Census childcare challenges](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/demo/sehsd-wp2026-08.html).

## Follow the care choice

child needs care → price, supply, schedule, or unpaid-family constraint → cash cost, lost work time, or parent work change → food, housing, debt, health, and benefit choices

The cash cost and time cost can arrive together. A parent may pay for care and still lose a shift because the provider closes early. Another may avoid the bill by asking a relative, who then gives up work or rest.

## What must stay separate

- Paid care and unpaid care.
- Care price and care availability.
- A parent not working and care as the cause.
- Work enabled and work made less valuable.
- Care access and care quality.

## Next test

Use Census SIPP or CPS care questions to compare households with similar children and wages but different care disruptions. Track paid care, unpaid care, work hours, income, commute, subsidies, and household food, health, debt, and housing in the same period.

## Reading rule

Paid care can enable work, and unpaid care can be valuable support. The finding concerns unequal choices and tradeoffs, not a judgment about either form.
