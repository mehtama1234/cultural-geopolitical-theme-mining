# When family care becomes paid-work time

## Short answer

Childcare is paid for in more than one currency. Some families pay a large cash bill. Some rely on relatives. Some lose work time when care is too expensive, unavailable or badly timed. These choices are not evenly available across income and family type.

The careful finding is that childcare can move a household's work and money choices at the same time. It does not follow that paid care is harmful or that unpaid care is a loss. Care can make work possible; the question is who can obtain it and what the alternatives cost.

## What the sources show

### 1. Paid care is a large monthly payment for the families who use it

The Federal Reserve's 2024 household survey found that 24% of parents living with children under 13 used paid childcare in the prior week. The median weekly payment was $240, or about $960 per month. Among parents using 20 or more hours, the median monthly payment was $1,400.

Just over half of parents using paid care spent at least half as much on childcare as on housing. The comparison is not a measure of hardship for every family, but it shows why care belongs in the household budget rather than only in a work discussion.

[Federal Reserve care and living arrangements report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-care-work-and-living-arrangements.htm)

### 2. Families also pay with relatives' time

The Fed found that 46% of parents with children under 13 used some unpaid childcare from someone other than the child's parent. Relatives were common providers, especially grandparents. Single parents were about as likely as two-working-parent families to use some care, but less likely to pay for it.

Unpaid care is not a simple substitute for paid care. It can be welcome support, but it can also depend on a relative's health, schedule, distance and ability to help. The survey does not tell us what the provider gave up.

[Federal Reserve childcare tables](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-care-work-and-living-arrangements.htm)

### 3. Care problems can show up as lost work days

A Census working paper using new questions in the 2025 Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement estimates that 23.9% of households with children under 13 paid for childcare in 2024. Their average annual payment was $10,520, or 5.6% of household income.

Only 3.7% reported problems obtaining adequate care, but those households lost an average of 65.8 days of work time. The figure is an average among affected households, not a national average and not a causal estimate.

[Census: Child Care Challenges and Adaptations](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/demo/sehsd-wp2026-08.html)

### 4. The work effect is not shared equally

The Fed reports that mothers more often said they were usually the primary caretaker when children were home. Among parents living with children under 13, 36% of women were not working for pay compared with 17% of men. Among non-working mothers of younger children, 42% said childcare contributed to that choice, compared with 18% of non-working fathers.

These figures show a difference in reported roles, not proof that childcare alone caused a parent to stop working. Health, wages, schedule fit, partner income and local care supply can also matter.

[Federal Reserve care and work findings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-care-work-and-living-arrangements.htm)

## The connection

```text
child needs care
  -> paid-care price, supply problem, or unpaid family arrangement
  -> money cost, lost work time, or a parent changing work
  -> food, housing, debt, health, status, or public-help choice
```

The sources support the cost, care-arrangement and reported work-time pieces. They do not prove which option a household would have chosen under a different care price or supply. The useful finding is that family time and paid work are linked through an everyday service that households arrange privately.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** The Fed measures paid and unpaid care, payments, caregiver roles and reported work choices. Census measures annual care spending and work days lost among households reporting care problems.

**Inference:** The price of childcare may affect household income twice: through the cash payment and through the work time a parent can or cannot sell.

**Unknown:** We do not know whether a family would pay more, rely on a relative, change shifts, reduce hours, leave work, use public help or move if care changed.

## What would change the finding

- Household records following childcare cost, care quality, work hours and income over time.
- Results separated by single-parent status, wages, schedule, race, disability, family size and place.
- The effect of subsidies, employer care benefits, paid leave and school schedules.
- Care availability and commute time, not only the dollar price.
- Evidence on whether lost work time changes food security, credit use, health care or benefit eligibility.

## Next test

Use Census SIPP or CPS care questions to compare households with similar children and wages but different care disruptions. Track paid care, unpaid care, work hours and household income in the same period. Treat the cash cost and the time cost as separate outcomes.

## Reading rule

Paid care can enable work, and unpaid care can be valuable support. The finding concerns unequal choices and tradeoffs, not a judgment about either form of care.
