# Finding: A startup can depend on relationships built around its founder

**Status:** bounded firm-culture, worker-retention, and organizational-dependence finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

A Census study links U.S. startups to their employees and examines what happens
when a founder dies prematurely. Founder departure significantly increased
employee turnover. The effect was stronger in older and larger startups and was
more visible among employees with longer shared tenure or the same sex as the
founder.

The authors interpret this as evidence that workers and founders can develop
complementarities in skills, relationships, or culture over time. A startup may
therefore have formal jobs and systems while still relying on a personal social
structure that is not easily replaced.

The route is:

```text
founder signals mission and culture
  -> worker joins and builds shared skills/relationships
  -> founder departure
  -> turnover or continued work
  -> [open] replacement leadership, job quality, firm survival, and worker security
```

## What this adds to the atlas

The broader cultural insight is that firm culture can be a real retention force
and a form of organizational dependence. It can help explain why a formal
employment relationship does not tell us whether workers will stay after a
change in leadership.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The study measures turnover, not whether leaving was voluntary, beneficial,
  harmful, or a sign of poor conditions.
- Premature founder death is not the same as resignation, succession, sale, or
  conflict.
- Turnover may reflect grief, uncertainty, financing, strategy, or replacement
  leadership rather than shared culture.
- Longer tenure and same-sex matching may proxy role, hierarchy, occupation, or
  selection rather than cultural alignment.
- The abstract does not observe pay, worker voice, firm survival, household
  effects, or later careers.

## Next decisive test

Follow the same employee and firm through founder departure, replacement
leadership, pay, hours, control, voice, job quality, turnover destination, firm
survival, household effects, and later career. Compare death, planned succession,
acquisition, and conflict while measuring the culture and relationships directly.
