# Four states show why a rent guarantee is not one national product

## The argument

A renter is told that a rent guarantee can replace a large security deposit or a co-signer. That sounds like one simple trade: pay a smaller fee now and get the apartment. The four-state check shows a more complicated deal. The state changes what kind of product may be sold, what paperwork can be found, what the landlord gets after a missed payment, and what the renter may owe afterward.

Imagine two renters with the same lease and the same income. One lives in California; the other lives in Texas. Both pay a provider so the landlord will accept a guarantee. If either renter stops paying, the provider may pay the landlord. That payment is not a gift to the renter. It can open a new collection path against the renter. The first question is therefore not “Was the deposit avoided?” It is “What obligation replaced it, and can the renter understand it before signing?”

The records do not give us a clean national price or a measured household result. They give us four different windows into the deal:

1. California exposes detailed bond terms.
2. New York exposes a regulator’s history of a provider and its rate filing.
3. Texas exposes a tenant rule for a fee used instead of a deposit.
4. Illinois exposes the licensing gate for people selling insurance, but not yet a product-level record.

That difference in visibility matters. A national website can make a product look uniform while the renter’s rights and duties still depend on the state, the contract, and the way a claim is handled.

## Follow the money after the missed payment

The easy version of the product ends when the landlord accepts the guarantee. The important version begins when the renter misses a payment:

```text
state rule and filed form
  -> provider offer and renter fee
  -> landlord accepts lower upfront cash
  -> renter defaults or causes covered damage
  -> provider or surety pays the landlord
  -> provider seeks recovery from the renter
  -> debt, dispute, renewal, credit, or housing result
```

Each arrow is a separate point where the renter can gain or lose protection. A lower move-in cost may help a person get housed today. A repayment demand later may make the next move harder. A claim may be correct, disputed, or based on a contract the renter did not fully understand. A provider’s payment may protect the landlord’s cash flow while leaving the renter with a debt that is harder to see at the start.

This is why a price comparison alone will be weak. The real comparison needs at least five records: the upfront fee, the covered amount, the renter’s duty after payment, the time and cost of a dispute, and the later housing result. The current four-state record has only fragments of that set.

## What connects the four states

The states do not yet show four different renter outcomes. They show four different levels of public visibility. California gives us contract detail without outcome data. New York gives us oversight history without a current quote. Texas gives us a clear legal boundary without provider practice. Illinois gives us a gate for sellers without a product record.

That leads to a useful research rule: legal permission, product availability, quote price, claim behavior, and household result must be tracked as separate fields. Combining them into one label such as “available in the state” would hide the part of the deal that matters most after a claim.

## The household question

For the renter, the decision is not simply deposit versus fee. It is:

- How much cash is saved at move-in?
- What event lets the provider make a claim?
- Does the provider ask the renter to repay everything it paid?
- Can the renter challenge the claim and see the records?
- Does the product help at the next renewal, or make the renter harder to house?

For the landlord, the question is different: does the product reduce screening friction and protect rent collection without changing tenant behavior? For the provider, the question is whether fees cover claims, collection, legal work, and capital. For the state, the question is whether access is being widened or whether a new debt product is being placed in front of renters with weak bargaining power.

## What would change the finding

The next pass should collect the same fields for the same lease and renter profile in all four states:

1. provider and carrier;
2. filed policy or bond form;
3. quoted fee and covered amount;
4. required disclosures;
5. claim trigger and notice process;
6. renter repayment, dispute, and collection terms;
7. renewal, move-out, credit, and later-housing result.

Until those fields are filled, the safe conclusion is narrow: states shape the product’s legal path, but the public record does not yet show whether the fee improves a renter’s life over the full lease.

## Reading rule

Do not call a state covered because a provider has a national website. Find the state rule, the actual policy or bond form, the quote, and the renter’s duty after the provider pays.
