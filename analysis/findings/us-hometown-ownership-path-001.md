# When business ownership moves away from home

## The argument

A business owner does not have to be born in a place to serve it well, and a local-born owner does not automatically protect it. The useful issue is not birthplace. It is whether a place keeps durable firms, useful services, good jobs, local wealth, and a voice in decisions.

An owner may move because customers, workers, credit, housing, or a better market are elsewhere. The move can bring a stronger business to the new place and leave a service gap in the old one. A firm can also look local while its profits, key decisions, and ownership sit elsewhere. Those are different facts and need different records.

The deeper finding is that “local control” is a bundle, not a feeling. The location of a storefront tells us where a customer buys. It does not tell us where the owner lives, where profits go, who chooses prices, where jobs are created, or whether the firm will stay during a hard year. A place can lose hometown owners and still gain useful businesses, or keep local owners while losing services and jobs.

## Follow one business move

A founder leaves a small town for a larger market. The new firm gains customers and survives. The old town loses a possible employer, but another owner may fill the space. If the founder’s company supplied a needed service, the gap may show up as longer travel, higher prices, or fewer choices. If the business was marginal, the move may have changed little for residents.

The customer result depends on what the firm did, not only on who owned it. The worker result depends on wages, stability, and opportunity. The civic result depends on where decisions and profits flow.

```text
capital, skills, housing, and local networks → where an owner can live and start
→ who owns firms and controls services → jobs, prices, profits, and place identity
→ housing, migration, and political demands
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Place attachment | An HBS account of Census research reports the extra chance of staying in one’s birth state among self-employed White men fell from 8 percentage points in 1970 to 2 points in 2018; the hometown advantage was below zero for all Americans in the latest sample. | Birthplace has become a weaker guide to where self-employed people live. |
| Business entry | NBER finds home equity can help some people start longer-lived businesses, while much house-price-linked entry is small or tied to construction and real estate. | Housing wealth can affect who can start and what kind of firm appears. |
| Local formation | Another NBER study links county business formation to long-run local traits. | Place conditions shape entry, but this does not show customer benefit or local control. |
| Missing result | The sources do not show whether residents gained from local ownership or whether mobile owners served them worse. | Ownership location is not the same as service quality. |

Sources: [HBS hometown entrepreneur](https://www2.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/charting-the-decline-of-the-hometown-entrepreneur), [NBER Working Paper 21458](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21458), and [NBER Working Paper 28606](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28606).

## The deeper finding

The real local asset may be the ability to make and keep decisions, not the owner’s birthplace. A town is more exposed when an essential service, employer, or property decision can leave without a local replacement. But a mobile owner can also bring capital, skill, and a better service. The test is whether the place keeps choice, work, and value after ownership changes.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show that local ownership was lost, that a mobile owner harmed customers, or that an owner move caused a local service gap. It does not follow firms through survival, prices, wages, profits, taxes, and civic participation. Outcomes may differ by industry, town size, housing cost, and access to credit.

## Next test

Link owner moves to firm records and local customer outcomes over five years. Compare places where owners leave with similar places where they stay. Track firm survival, jobs, wages, prices, service gaps, housing costs, profits, taxes, and local participation.

## Reading rule

Birthplace is not the same as commitment. Follow where decisions, profits, jobs, and services end up.
