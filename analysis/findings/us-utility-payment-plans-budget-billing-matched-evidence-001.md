# A smoother bill can move the problem rather than end it

## The argument

Budget billing changes when a household pays, not necessarily how much it owes.

A utility can estimate the cost of a season, divide it into average monthly payments, and later adjust the account for the difference between estimated and actual use. This can be valuable for a household whose income is steady but whose energy use rises sharply in winter or summer. A predictable payment is easier to plan than a large surprise bill.

But the smoother number can hide the account's running balance. If use is higher than expected, the household may reach the end of the plan owing a true-up. If the payment is taken automatically, the withdrawal can also arrive before a paycheck or alongside rent, food, and medicine. The plan may reduce the shock this month while creating a less visible shock later.

The fair question is therefore not whether the monthly payment is lower. It is whether the plan leaves the household with a manageable total cost and a clear path through the next adjustment.

## Follow the account, not just the payment

Imagine a family enrolling after a difficult winter. Its monthly payment falls from the recent peak. The family feels relief and stops cutting other expenses. Through the spring, use falls, but the utility's estimate may not be updated quickly. In the next winter, use rises again. The family now has a monthly payment and a growing balance it may not see until the true-up.

Another family may benefit greatly: its income arrives evenly, its home is efficient, and the estimate is accurate. The same plan can work for one household and fail for another because the issue is not only the plan's design. It is the fit between the plan, the home, the weather, and the household's cash calendar.

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate | CFPB describes a home-fuel budget plan that bills an average monthly amount based on expected seasonal cost. | The plan smooths timing. |
| True-up | The plan adjusts a final payment for the difference between estimated and actual cost. | A later balance can remain. |
| Withdrawal | CFPB notes that utility payments may be fixed or variable automatic withdrawals. | The payment can meet the bank-account calendar, not the household's cash reality. |
| Help | DOE and CFPB point households toward assistance and payment plans. | Plans are one response among several. |
| Risk | EIA counts disconnect notices and unusable heating or cooling equipment. | Poorly fitted plans can end at a service crisis. |

Sources: [CFPB budget-plan rule](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/2023-01-01/interp-3/), [CFPB automatic-payment guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-automatic-payments-from-a-bank-account-work-en-2021/), [DOE energy-help resources](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/wap/how-apply-weatherization-assistance), [CFPB bill-help guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/housing/housing-insecurity/help-for-renters/get-help-paying-rent-and-bills/), and [EIA 2024 energy-insecurity data](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf).

## The argument has limits

The sources establish how a budget plan works. They do not show how many customers reach a true-up, how large those balances are, or whether budget billing lowers shutoffs. They also do not tell us whether customers understand that a smaller monthly payment is not a discount.

That missing information matters. A payment plan can be good cash-flow design without being good debt design. It can also be bad cash-flow design if the estimate is wrong or the withdrawal date is fixed at the wrong point in the month.

## What this changes

Every plan should show four numbers together: the monthly payment, actual use, running balance, and likely adjustment. It should also show what happens if the customer leaves, misses a payment, or cannot pay the true-up. Without those numbers, the customer is being asked to manage a future obligation with present-day information.

The best comparison is not between “budget billing” and “no budget billing.” It is between budget billing, flexible due dates, arrears plans, direct aid, and repairs. The right option depends on whether the problem is timing, total cost, a bad home, or income that does not cover the bill.

## What remains unproven

The next test is a customer-level record of enrollment, use, payment, balance, notice, true-up, arrears, and service status at 30, 90, and 180 days. We need to know whether smoothing prevents a missed payment or simply moves it to a more dangerous month.

## Reading rule

Do not call a payment plan affordable because the monthly number is smaller. Follow the balance, the true-up, and the next bill.
