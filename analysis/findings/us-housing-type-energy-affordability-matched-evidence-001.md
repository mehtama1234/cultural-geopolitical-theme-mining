# The same energy bill means different things in different homes

## The argument

Energy burden is usually presented as a percentage: energy spending divided by household income. That is useful, but incomplete. Two households can spend the same share of income and have very different ways to lower the bill.

A renter may pay the monthly bill but need a landlord to replace a window, furnace, or air conditioner. A manufactured-home resident may face a small, older structure with costly heating needs and fewer repair or financing choices. A homeowner may control the repair but lack the cash or credit to make it. The bill is visible; the power to change it is not.

That difference matters when a shock arrives. The renter can ask for a repair but cannot order one. The manufactured-home resident may pay more per square foot because the building or equipment is less efficient. The homeowner can choose an upgrade, but that choice may mean debt, a second mortgage, or postponing another need.

The point is not that one housing type is always worse. It is that “energy burden” describes pressure, not the route out of pressure.

The percentage can also hide the fixed amount left after energy. A household with a lower income may spend the same share as a higher-income household but have much less money for food, medicine, transport, and repairs. Two homes with the same percentage can therefore have different safety margins. The burden measure needs a remaining-cash measure beside it.

## Three homes, three recovery paths

Imagine three households with similar incomes and the same winter bill.

The renter waits for a landlord to approve a repair. The bill keeps arriving while the request moves through a property manager. The manufactured-home resident may own the home but face an expensive heating or insulation problem that is hard to finance. The homeowner can call a contractor immediately, but the repair competes with mortgage payments, food, medicine, or other debt.

All three may report energy insecurity. Their next action is not the same. One needs a landlord decision, one needs a building or equipment solution, and one needs affordable capital or public help. A bill-payment program may protect all three for a month but solve none of the underlying control problems.

The home also changes the value of moving. A renter may leave an inefficient unit but face a higher deposit or rent elsewhere. A manufactured-home resident may own the structure but rent the land and risk losing the site. A homeowner may want to move but be unable to sell or refinance. “Just move” is not a neutral energy plan; it has housing, transport, school, and care costs.

## The control problem

The renter split is the most important connection. If the tenant pays the energy bill and the landlord controls the building, a higher bill is not an ordinary shopping decision. The tenant cannot simply buy the better window or replace the furnace. The household can reduce use, tolerate an unsafe temperature, move, or ask someone with control to act.

That is why a lower average burden after an upgrade is not enough. We need to know who paid for the work, who approved it, who received the savings, and whether the tenant's rent changed. Otherwise, the same intervention can look successful in the building's energy data while leaving the resident with the same choices.

There is a split in the savings as well. A repair may lower the utility bill but raise rent, fees, or the owner’s claimed value of the property. A tenant may receive the comfort but not the cash saving. A homeowner may receive the saving but have less cash because the upgrade was financed. The outcome is not “energy fell”; it is who gained safe use and who carried the cost.

## The manufactured-home case

Manufactured homes should not be treated as a footnote to “owners.” Ownership can mean control, but it does not guarantee a good building, affordable repair, or a valuable asset to borrow against. GAO's older review found higher energy spending per square foot in older manufactured homes than in detached homes, and examined the relationship between those costs and LIHEAP help.

The evidence is old, so it cannot describe today's prices. It does show why tenure alone is too blunt. An owner in a manufactured home can have more control than a renter and fewer practical ways to use that control.

The asset may not provide the borrowing power that standard homeownership assumptions imply. Land-lease terms, age, title, insurance, and repair condition can limit refinancing or resale. A policy that offers a loan may therefore reach a homeowner on paper while failing to provide usable capital.

## What remains unproven

The sources establish different measures of burden, the renter split, a housing-type distinction, and reported energy-insecurity outcomes. They do not yet show one household's bill, repair request, landlord decision, upgrade, temperature, health, and later stability.

They also do not show whether a lower bill changes rent, whether repairs last, or whether the household can remain in the home. An improvement in energy use is not automatically an improvement in household security.

The next test should follow those records together and compare the time and money needed to reach a safe home—not just the annual energy share.

Build a home-level ledger for renters, homeowners, and manufactured-home residents: housing payment, energy bill, equipment, insulation, repair authority, application, financing, landlord response, indoor temperature, moving cost, transport, food or medicine tradeoff, and next-season result. Measure remaining cash, safe hours at home, control over the fix, total housing-and-energy cost, and ability to stay separately. Compare bill aid, repair, weatherization, rent protections, and low-cost capital by the problem each one can actually solve.

## Reading rule

Do not compare homes only by energy burden. Ask who pays the bill, who controls the fix, and who can leave.
