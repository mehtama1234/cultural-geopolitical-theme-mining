# The first price shapes the choice before the full price appears

## The argument

A shopper cannot compare a cost that is hidden until late in the purchase. The first number on a listing or ticket attracts attention, starts the search, and can make the shopper spend time choosing. If taxes, service charges, shipping, or late fees appear only after that commitment, the customer is no longer comparing the same products from the start.

That does not make every separate fee wrong. Some costs are real and may be useful to show separately. The problem is timing. A customer who sees the full mandatory price early can leave or compare. A customer who sees it late may accept a worse deal because backing out now costs time, a deadline, a trip, or a needed service.

The deeper finding is that price display is part of the market, not decoration around it. The first number changes who enters the comparison and how much effort the customer has already spent before the final number arrives.

## Follow the price through the purchase

A traveler compares rooms by the opening price. After choosing dates and entering payment details, a mandatory fee appears. The traveler can abandon the booking, but alternatives may now cost more or be unavailable. The first price helped create a commitment.

The same pattern can appear in credit. A low opening cost attracts a customer while over-limit, late, or other fees arrive later. The final cost is harder to compare because the decision was made using incomplete information.

```text
opening price
  -> attention and search
  -> time, data, and commitment spent
  -> late fee or full mandatory price appears
  -> stay, exit, or accept a worse comparison
  -> final cost, quality, and trust change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NBER experiments find full-price display changes purchase, exit, quantity, and quality choices. | Information timing changes behavior. | Changed behavior is not automatically a welfare gain or loss. |
| NBER research examines costs that arrive later in credit products. | The first number may not describe the product’s life. | It does not measure every fee or customer. |
| HBS research links price coherence with consumer decisions and value judgments. | Comparable prices help customers judge value. | A judgment is not the final household outcome. |
| FTC rules require total prices in defined ticket and lodging markets. | Disclosure can change comparison. | The coverage is limited. |

Sources: [NBER price salience](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186), [NBER tax salience](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13330), [NBER credit-card fees](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22360), [FTC fee rule](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/05/ftc-rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-take-effect-may-12-2025), and [HBS price coherence](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/its-called-price-coherence-and-its-surprisingly-bad-for-consumers).

## The deeper finding

Keep four questions apart: was the fee mandatory, was it visible early, did it change the choice, and what was finally paid and received? A customer may see a fee and still choose because the total is worth it. Another may leave. The source-backed point is narrower: late information changes the conditions under which comparison occurs.

This is a customer-power issue. Early full pricing preserves the right to walk away before time and attention are sunk. Late disclosure makes the customer pay a search cost before learning what is being compared.

## What remains unknown

The experiments show that price display changes behavior. They do not show that disclosure alone improves quality, trust, or household finances, or what purchase the customer made instead.

## Next test

Join first price, final price, fee timing, quality, cancellation, refund, and what the purchase displaced. Compare customers who saw full prices early with those who saw them late.

## Reading rule

Do not call a fee hidden because it is separate. Ask whether the full mandatory cost was clear before the customer committed time, attention, or money.
