# When protection becomes part of the price of a home

## The argument

A home can become harder to keep even when the mortgage payment has not changed. Disaster risk can raise the insurance bill. A weaker credit record may raise it too. The higher bill can affect the owner’s monthly room, the home’s value, and the choice to stay, sell, repair, change coverage, or move.

Insurance is often paid through mortgage escrow, so a premium increase can look like a sudden mortgage increase even when the loan’s interest rate and principal are unchanged. An owner may cut repairs, borrow, accept less coverage, or go without insurance. Each choice creates a different risk. A premium increase is not automatically a forced move, but it can make a home less secure and harder to finance.

The deeper finding is that protection is becoming part of the home’s financial structure. The same disaster risk can reach a household through the premium, the escrow account, the mortgage, the property value, and the ability to sell or refinance. The owner’s credit record can become part of that price as well.

## Follow one home through renewal

An owner in a disaster-exposed area receives a renewal notice that is $300 higher per month. The owner can pay it, reduce coverage, shop for another insurer, borrow, delay a repair, or sell. Shopping may fail if insurers have left the area. Reducing coverage may lower the bill while leaving the owner unable to rebuild after a loss.

The household’s result depends on income, savings, credit, local insurer supply, home condition, and the cost of leaving—not on the premium alone.

```text
disaster exposure or weaker credit record → higher insurance price
→ larger escrow payment or less coverage → repairs, borrowing, sale, or move choice
→ future housing and credit access
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Premium | Using more than 74 million mortgage-linked insurance observations, NBER finds premiums rose sharply between 2020 and 2024. | Protection has become a larger household cost in the studied data. |
| Risk price | The relationship between local disaster risk and premiums grew stronger; in the most exposed ZIP codes, higher premiums were linked to more than $40,000 less home-price growth. | Insurance and property value can move together. |
| Credit price | A newer NBER study using about 70 million policies linked to mortgages and property risk reports that credit scores affect homeowners-insurance premiums alongside disaster risk. | The financial record may enter the protection price. |
| Household gap | The Federal Reserve reports 6% of homeowners went without insurance; the share was 12% in low- or moderate-income neighborhoods and roughly 2 in 10 for owners with income below $50,000. | Coverage is harder to keep where money is tighter. |

Sources: [NBER property insurance and disaster risk](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579), [NBER pricing protection](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34848), and [Federal Reserve 2025 housing findings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-housing.htm).

## The deeper finding

Insurance is a condition of housing security, not just another bill. A home can remain occupied and still become less protected, less valuable, or harder to finance. A premium policy may prevent a large future loss while creating a payment problem today.

## What remains unproven

The studies do not show how many owners lose coverage, miss escrow payments, cut repairs, sell, move, or borrow because of the increase. They do not follow one owner through premium, coverage, credit, property value, mortgage trouble, and housing outcome. The survey figures are reported conditions, not proof that disaster risk caused every gap.

## Next test

Join mortgage escrow, insurance, credit, property, and nonrenewal records. Start with exposed ZIP codes and compare owners with similar homes but different credit paths. Keep price, coverage, payment trouble, repairs, sale, and moving separate.

## Reading rule

Do not call a premium increase a forced move. First show whether the owner paid, changed coverage, borrowed, sold, or stayed.
