# When protection becomes part of the price of a home

## Short answer

A home can become harder to keep even when the mortgage payment has not changed. Disaster risk can raise the insurance bill. A weaker credit record may raise it too. The higher bill can then affect the home's value, the owner's monthly room, and the choice to stay, sell, repair or move.

The careful finding is not that insurance prices cause every move or default. It is that protection is becoming a measurable part of the home's financial burden.

## What the sources show

### 1. Disaster risk is reaching the monthly bill

Using more than 74 million mortgage-linked insurance observations, NBER finds that premiums rose sharply between 2020 and 2024. The relationship between local disaster risk and premiums also grew stronger. In the most exposed ZIP codes, higher premiums were linked to more than $40,000 less home-price growth.

[NBER property insurance and disaster risk study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579)

### 2. The financial record may also affect the price

A newer NBER study uses about 70 million policies linked to mortgages and property risk. It reports that credit scores affect homeowners-insurance premiums alongside disaster risk.

[NBER pricing protection study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34848)

### 3. The home cost can be larger than the mortgage line

Insurance is often paid through the mortgage escrow account. A premium increase can therefore raise the monthly payment even when the loan's interest rate and principal have not changed. An owner who cannot absorb the increase may reduce repairs, change coverage, borrow, sell or move.

This last sentence is a household path to test, not a result shown by the premium studies.

### 4. The effect can reach future housing access

Credit records already shape access to borrowing and housing in the atlas. If credit affects insurance pricing, a payment problem and a protection problem may reinforce each other. The current sources do not show that full loop for one owner.

### 5. The gap is wider where money is tighter

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey reports that 6% of homeowners went without homeowners insurance. The share was 12% in low- or moderate-income neighborhoods and 5% in high-income neighborhoods. Roughly 2 in 10 homeowners with income below $50,000 went without coverage, and 43% of uninsured homeowners said they could not afford it. Among insured owners with income below $50,000, nearly 30% said they struggled to afford premiums.

These are reported household conditions, not proof that disaster risk caused the gap. They do show that insurance is not only a market price; for some owners it is a choice between protection and other bills.

[Federal Reserve 2025 housing findings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-housing.htm)

## The connection

```text
disaster exposure or weaker credit record
  -> higher insurance price
  -> larger escrow payment or less coverage
  -> home value, borrowing, repairs, sale or move choice
  -> future housing and credit access
```

The first two links are supported by NBER evidence. The household decisions at the end remain open.

## What is fact, interpretation, and unknown

**Fact:** Insurance premiums rose unevenly. Disaster risk is more strongly reflected in prices than before. Credit scores are reported to affect homeowners-insurance premiums. Exposed ZIP codes saw slower home-price growth.

**Interpretation:** Global reinsurance prices and local credit records can reach a household through one ordinary monthly bill.

**Unknown:** We do not know how many owners lose coverage, miss escrow payments, cut repairs, sell, move or borrow because of the increase.

## What would change the finding

- Premium, coverage, credit and escrow records for the same owner.
- Nonrenewal and insurer-withdrawal records by place and household type.
- A before-and-after comparison of premium changes with mortgage trouble, repairs and moves.
- Evidence on renters who face higher insurance costs through rent or building supply.
- Results by income, race, credit score, disaster exposure and state rules.

## Next test

Join mortgage escrow, insurance, credit and property records. Start with exposed ZIP codes and compare owners with similar homes but different credit paths. Keep price, coverage, payment trouble and moving as separate outcomes.

## Reading rule

Do not call a premium increase a forced move. First show whether the owner paid, changed coverage, borrowed, sold or stayed.
