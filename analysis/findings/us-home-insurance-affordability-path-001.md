# When protection becomes part of the price of a home

## The argument

A home can become harder to keep even when the mortgage payment has not changed. Disaster risk can raise the insurance bill. A weaker credit record may raise it too. Because insurance is often paid through mortgage escrow, a premium increase can feel like a mortgage increase even when the loan's principal and interest are unchanged.

The owner then faces several choices: pay more, reduce coverage, shop for another insurer, borrow, delay a repair, sell, or go without insurance. Each solves one immediate problem and creates another risk. A premium increase is not automatically a forced move, but it can make a home less secure and harder to finance.

## Follow one home through renewal

An owner in a disaster-exposed area receives a renewal notice $300 higher per month. Shopping fails because insurers have left the area. The owner accepts less coverage and delays a roof repair. The monthly bill falls, but the home is less able to withstand the next storm. Another owner pays the increase and cuts food or savings. A third sells before the next renewal.

```text
disaster exposure or weaker credit record → higher insurance price → larger escrow payment or less coverage
→ repairs, borrowing, sale, or move choice → future housing and credit access
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Premium | Using more than 74 million mortgage-linked insurance observations, NBER finds premiums rose sharply between 2020 and 2024. | Protection became a larger household cost in the studied data. |
| Risk price | The relationship between local disaster risk and premiums grew stronger; in the most exposed ZIP codes, higher premiums were linked to more than $40,000 less home-price growth. | Insurance and property value can move together. |
| Credit price | A newer NBER study using about 70 million policies linked to mortgages and property risk reports that credit scores affect homeowners-insurance premiums alongside disaster risk. | The financial record may enter the protection price. |
| Coverage gap | The Federal Reserve reports 6% of homeowners went without insurance; the share was 12% in low- or moderate-income neighborhoods and roughly 2 in 10 for owners below $50,000 income. | Coverage is harder to keep when cash is tight. |
| Limits | The studies do not show the same owner through premium, coverage, payment trouble, repair, sale, and move. | A premium result is not automatically a displacement result. |

Sources: [NBER property insurance and disaster risk](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579), [NBER pricing protection](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34848), and [Federal Reserve housing findings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-housing.htm).

## The deeper finding

Insurance is a condition of housing security, not just another bill. A home can remain occupied and still become less protected, less valuable, or harder to finance. The policy can prevent a large future loss while creating a payment problem today. The real issue is whether the owner has a way to absorb both.

## What remains unproven

The studies do not show how many owners lose coverage, miss escrow payments, cut repairs, sell, move, or borrow because of premium increases. The survey figures are reported conditions, not proof that disaster risk caused every gap.

## Next test

Join mortgage escrow, insurance, credit, property, and nonrenewal records. Start with exposed ZIP codes and compare owners with similar homes but different credit paths. Keep price, coverage, payment trouble, repairs, sale, and moving separate.

## Reading rule

Do not call a premium increase a forced move. First show whether the owner paid, changed coverage, borrowed, sold, or stayed.
