# Unpaid eldercare can become a second job before it becomes a bill

## The argument

The US often pays for eldercare with family time before it pays for it with money. A worker drives a parent to appointments, manages medicines, prepares food, handles forms, or stays nearby in case something goes wrong. The care is real, but the cost may show up as fewer work hours, a job exit, lower savings, or exhaustion rather than as a care invoice.

The burden is not evenly shared. Women are more represented among unpaid providers in the BLS data, and NBER finds an earnings and employment decline after caregiving begins for women. This does not mean every caregiver has the same result. It means the system assigns measurable economic risk to a group already carrying much of the work.

The deeper finding is that unpaid care is a transfer from one person’s future to another person’s present. It can protect an older person and avoid a paid bill while narrowing the caregiver’s job, savings, health, and housing choices.

## Follow one working caregiver

An employee’s parent needs help several times a week. The employee uses leave, changes shifts, turns down overtime, or leaves a job with a long commute. The family saves the price of paid care, but the worker loses earnings and may reduce future retirement savings.

The care arrangement may work for a month and fail when the parent’s health worsens. A family that looked able to manage can then face a sudden paid-care bill, a move, or a job loss. The hidden cost is the lack of a second option.

```text
need for care
  -> unpaid family time or paid care
  -> work, earnings, health, savings, or housing change
  -> family and employer response
  -> public support or greater dependence on relatives
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| BLS reports 38.2 million unpaid eldercare providers in 2023–24. | Family care is a large part of the system. | The count does not price the care. |
| 52% provided care at least several times a week; care days averaged 3.9 hours. | The work is repeated and time-consuming. | An average hides the heaviest cases. |
| NBER finds caregiving precedes drops in earnings and employment for women. | Care can reduce paid work. | It does not show every caregiver’s cause or choice. |
| BLS reports women made up 55% of unpaid eldercare providers. | The burden is distributed unevenly. | The statistic does not measure family wealth or support. |
| Five states had enacted caregiver tax-credit programs by April 2025. | Policy is beginning to recognize the work. | A credit does not provide time, respite, or safe care by itself. |

Sources: [BLS unpaid eldercare](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/elcare.htm), [NBER caregiving and labor supply](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31450), and [HHS caregiver tax-credit review](https://aspe.hhs.gov/reports/caregiver-tax-credit).

## The deeper finding

The relevant measure is not only hours given. It is the options lost: a shift, promotion, job change, retirement contribution, rest, or move. A caregiver tax credit may offset some cash cost while leaving the time and coordination problem untouched.

Employers also see only part of the chain. A missed shift may look like attendance trouble when the underlying issue is a care system with no reliable replacement. The worker and older person are connected to the same service gap.

## What remains unknown

BLS shows scale and timing, and NBER links caregiving to work outcomes. Neither gives the complete balance of paid care avoided, wages lost, health effects, savings, and the older person’s result for the same family.

## Next test

Link caregiver and recipient records around the same event. Measure tasks, hours, distance, schedule, paid replacement cost, leave, earnings, health, savings, housing, and recipient outcomes. Test tax credits, paid leave, respite care, and home services.

## Reading rule

Do not call unpaid care free. State who provides the time, what work or money changes, and what care the older person receives.
