# When cash changes the rest of the household

## The argument

Cash paid to one person can change what other people in the home do. Someone may enter training, change work, spend differently, or finally come into contact with health care. That spillover can be a family gain. A report of forgone care can also reveal an unmet need rather than show that the payment made health worse.

The payment therefore has to be followed member by member. Who receives it is not always who benefits first. One person may gain time, another may take on care, and a third may hold the new debt. A household average hides the bargaining and tradeoffs inside the home.

The deeper question is not only whether the payment raised consumption or reduced stress. It is **who gained control, what work moved between family members, which need became visible, and what remained when the payment stopped.**

## Follow one payment through a family

A recipient uses regular cash to reduce a bill and take a training course. A partner changes work hours to cover care. A child gets a needed appointment. The family gains time and a possible path forward, but it may also spend more, carry debt, or discover a health need that had been delayed because there was no money, time, or way to reach care.

The recipient may control the payment, or the family may pool it. Either arrangement can work for one household and create conflict in another. A payment can increase a person's ability to refuse unsafe work while shifting more unpaid care to a partner. It can let a young adult study while a parent pays another bill. The same dollar has different effects depending on who can decide where it goes.

When payments end, each member may face a different return to the old constraint. Training may continue to pay off, but debt may remain. A newly identified health need may still require care. A household may keep a new work pattern or return to the old one.

Regularity changes the value of cash. A predictable payment can let a family plan a bill, accept a shift with less fear, or schedule care before it becomes urgent. An irregular payment may still help but be used to patch the latest crisis. The same total amount can therefore produce different results depending on whether the household knows when it will arrive and whether it can make a commitment around it.

The payment can also alter who has the power to say no. A recipient may leave an unsafe job or refuse a bad housing arrangement. That is a gain in control, but another household member may take more paid work or unpaid care to keep the budget balanced. The effect is not captured by total income. It sits in the decisions each person can make and the cost another person bears.

```text
one household member receives cash → money, time, and decision power move inside the home
→ another member trains, works, receives care, or changes spending
→ family gains room or finds an unmet need → work, health, debt, and roles change
→ payment ends → gains hold, weaken, or return to the earlier constraint
```

## What the sources actually establish

|---|---|---|---|
| Spillover | In a randomized guaranteed-income experiment, NBER finds other household members were more likely to take part in education or training. | A payment can affect people beyond the named recipient. | It does not show that training led to better work or income. |
| Health report | Recipients were more likely to report that someone in the household had forgone health care; the authors say this may reflect greater engagement with care and needs already present. | A cash program can change what a household notices and reports about care. | It is not proof that cash caused worse health or less care. |
| Consumption and balance sheet | Another US experiment with $1,000 or $50 monthly payments found higher short-term consumption and near-zero average change in net worth because debt also rose. | Cash can meet a need while debt absorbs part of the gain. | It does not identify every within-household decision or long-term security. |
| Control | HBS reports that money can give people more control over daily transport, food, and care problems and reduce stress. | Cash can buy choice before income changes. | It does not test the guaranteed-income experiment or household spillovers. |

## The deeper finding

Cash changes the household's internal clock. One person can use it to buy time for training, another can handle care, and the family can address a problem before it becomes an emergency. But flexibility is not free. It can change who controls spending, who takes unpaid work, whose career pauses, and which need becomes visible first.

The health result shows why simple before-and-after labels fail. More reported forgone care could mean a person became aware of a problem that had been hidden, tried to obtain care but could not afford it, or interpreted a past delay differently after joining a study. The result may contain both better detection and unmet need. We need care use, diagnosis, cost, health, and timing to tell them apart.

The household also has a power question. A payment in one person's name may increase that person's say, or it may be pooled under an existing arrangement. A family may use the money to protect a child's future or an older person's care. It may also use the money to keep the main earner working while another member absorbs more unpaid labor. The result should not be described as a household gain without showing who made the sacrifice.

The end of the payment is a test of whether it built capacity or only filled a gap. A family may have paid down a bill and become safer, or may have used the cash for food and be no worse off except that the shortage returns. Training may create a later wage path, but it may also leave tuition debt and no time for care. A short program should therefore report what remains after the transfer, not only what changed while it was arriving.

There is a public design choice in targeting. Paying one member can recognize an individual right and shift bargaining power. Paying the household may simplify administration but hide unequal control. A broad payment may reach people with need before they can prove it, while a narrow one may direct more money to a specific problem and miss people who cannot complete the process. The amount, recipient, timing, and conditions are separate parts of the intervention.

## Four views of the same payment

**Recipient:** What did the person control, spend, save, study, refuse, or choose differently?

**Other household members:** Who changed work, care, health use, transport, debt, or education? Who gained time and who lost it?

**Household balance sheet:** Did cash, debt, savings, housing, health costs, and food security change together?

**Public power:** Is the payment meant to provide income, choice, care access, or a path out of work risk? What happens when it ends?

## What remains unproven

The studies do not show who controlled the payment, how money was shared, whether training led to better work, or whether the family became safer. They do not show whether reported forgone care was newly created, newly detected, or both. They do not follow the same family's roles, debt, health, and security after payments stop.

## Next test

Follow every household member before, during, and two years after payments. Join payment, work, training, care, health, spending, debt, housing, time use, and decision-making records. Ask who decided how the money was used and whether a care report reflects new unmet need, better detection, or both.

Compare predictable monthly payments with the same total delivered irregularly, and compare individual control with household pooling where ethically and legally appropriate. Measure food, rent, debt, savings, work hours, job quality, training completion, care use, health, stress, conflict, and each member's ability to refuse a bad option. Recheck outcomes six, twelve, and twenty-four months after payments end to see whether the gain became durable room or returned as a new shortage.

## Reading rule

Do not treat a household result as only the recipient's result. Do not treat reported forgone care as proof that cash harmed health.
