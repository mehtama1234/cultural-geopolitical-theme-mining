# When a trusted service becomes a dispute

## Short answer

Fraud is not only a theft story. It is also a test of whether a person can get an answer, stop the loss and recover enough money to keep going. The same names people trust—banks, agencies, employers and brands—can be copied by scammers, while the recovery rules differ by payment type.

The careful finding is that a financial loss can become a customer-service and trust problem. The sources do not yet show that a person stopped using a bank, changed a political view or lost trust after a specific response.

## What the sources show

### 1. Fraud reaches a large share of households

The Federal Reserve's 2024 household survey found that 21% of adults experienced financial fraud or a scam involving their money. Credit-card fraud was the most common type. Eight percent experienced fraud not related to a credit card.

The survey measures reported experience, not all attempts or incidents. Still, it shows that fraud is common enough to be part of ordinary financial life rather than an edge case.

[Federal Reserve financial fraud findings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-banking-and-credit.htm)

### 2. The payment type changes who carries the loss

Among adults who experienced non-credit-card fraud, 63% lost money and 32% said at least some of it was not recovered. The Fed estimates $84 billion in total losses before recovery and $63 billion in net losses borne directly by consumers.

Credit-card fraud usually does not leave the cardholder carrying the full direct loss. Bank transfers, investment accounts and other payment routes may have less automatic protection. “Fraud happened” therefore does not describe the household result by itself.

[Federal Reserve household well-being report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-banking-and-credit.htm)

### 3. Reported losses are rising in the public record

The FTC says consumers reported more than $12.5 billion lost to fraud in 2024, a 25% increase from 2023. Imposter scams were among the largest categories. In 2025, reported losses to imposter scams reached $3.5 billion, and total reported fraud losses reached about $16 billion.

FTC figures are reports, not a count of every loss. Reporting can change when people learn where to complain, when platforms change their forms, or when shame keeps someone silent.

[FTC 2024 fraud data](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/03/new-ftc-data-show-big-jump-reported-losses-fraud-125-billion-2024)

[FTC 2025 imposter-scam data](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025)

### 4. The next problem is getting a response

The CFPB's 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report records complaints across credit reporting, debt collection, credit cards, checking accounts and money transfers. Complaint counts show where consumers seek help, but they are not unique people and do not prove that a firm broke the law.

This is the service link: after money moves, the consumer must explain what happened, wait for a decision and sometimes challenge the decision. Time and clarity are part of the recovery experience.

[CFPB 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/2025-consumer-response-annual-report/)

### 5. Information can help and also create exposure

Research on consumer surveillance and financial fraud provides a mechanism to examine how data practices may affect fraud risk. The current opening pass has not reviewed the full paper closely enough to make a stronger claim.

This limit matters. More data can help a firm spot a strange transaction, but more data can also create more paths for misuse. The answer cannot be inferred from “more monitoring” alone.

[NBER: Consumer Surveillance and Financial Fraud](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31692)

## The connection

```text
trusted name, account, platform, or payment route
  -> scam or unauthorized transaction
  -> money loss, recovery attempt, time cost, or account lock
  -> trust, usage, credit, or household budget change
  -> firm response, regulator action, or political demand
```

The sources support household exposure, different loss protection, reported losses and the existence of a complaint path. They do not show the full sequence for one consumer.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** The Fed measures fraud exposure and unrecovered losses. FTC measures reported fraud and losses. CFPB records consumer complaints by product.

**Inference:** A fraud event can become a trust problem when recovery is slow, unclear or shifted onto the consumer. The same event can have different effects depending on the payment route.

**Unknown:** We do not know whether the person stopped using the service, borrowed to replace the loss, changed account behavior, or changed a view of the firm or government.

## What would change the finding

- A panel following the same consumer from fraud report to recovery, account use and later financial condition.
- Recovery rates and time by payment type, bank, platform and loss size.
- Results by age, disability, language, income and digital access.
- Complaint response records showing whether the consumer received money, an explanation or a workable appeal.
- Evidence on whether stronger monitoring reduces losses without creating new privacy or access costs.

## Next test

Join FTC reported-loss categories with CFPB complaint outcomes for the same product and period, then compare with the Fed household loss measure. Keep four outcomes separate: attempt, reported loss, unrecovered loss and trust or usage change.

## Reading rule

A complaint is not proof of a violation, a scam attempt is not a loss, and a reported loss is not always an unrecovered loss. The distinctions are the finding.
