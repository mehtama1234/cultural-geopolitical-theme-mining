# When a trusted service becomes a dispute

## The argument

Fraud does not end when money leaves an account. The household then has to stop the loss, prove what happened, wait for a decision, and replace whatever the money was meant to buy. A bank, platform, agency, or employer may have been copied by the scammer, but the customer still has to navigate the real institution’s recovery rules.

The payment route changes the result. A credit-card charge may have a familiar dispute path. A bank transfer, investment payment, or other route may leave the consumer carrying more of the loss. Two people can suffer the same false identity scam and end up with different cash, time, and trust because the payment system assigns the loss differently.

The deeper finding is narrow: a financial loss can become a customer-service and trust problem. We do not yet know whether a specific response made a person stop using a bank, change a political view, or lose trust in government.

## What the sources establish

| Evidence | What it tells us | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| The Federal Reserve found that 21% of adults experienced financial fraud or a money scam in 2024. | Fraud is part of ordinary financial life, not a rare edge case. | How many attempts were never reported and which response prevented loss. |
| Among adults with non-credit-card fraud, 63% lost money and 32% said some was not recovered. The Fed estimates $84 billion in losses before recovery and $63 billion borne directly by consumers. | Payment type changes how much of the loss stays with the household. | Recovery time and outcome by bank, platform, loss size, and payment route. |
| FTC reports show large reported losses: more than $12.5 billion in 2024 and about $16 billion in 2025. | Public reports show rising reported harm and the scale of imposter scams. | Reports are not every incident; reporting and silence change the totals. |
| CFPB complaint records cover credit reporting, debt collection, cards, checking, and transfers. | After a loss, consumers seek an explanation or remedy through a formal channel. | Complaint counts do not prove a violation or show whether money was returned. |
| NBER studies consumer surveillance and financial fraud as a mechanism. | More monitoring may help spot unusual activity. | Whether more data lowers loss without creating new misuse, privacy, or access costs. |

## Follow the household after the scam

trusted name or account → scam or unauthorized transaction → loss, account lock, recovery attempt, and time cost → money replaced or not → trust, usage, credit, and later budget

The first arrow may be outside the customer’s control. The later arrows are shaped by the institution’s rules and the customer’s ability to reach a person, provide records, and wait. A loss can therefore become a second loss: unpaid rent, borrowed money, missed work, or a frozen account while the case is reviewed.

## What must stay separate

- An attempted scam is not a financial loss.
- A reported loss is not always an unrecovered loss.
- A complaint is not proof that a firm broke the law.
- A returned dollar is not the same as restored time or trust.
- More monitoring is not automatically safer if it creates new exposure.

## Next test

Link fraud type, payment route, report date, recovery amount, recovery time, complaint response, account use, borrowing, and later household condition. Compare card payments, bank transfers, and investment routes. Split results by age, disability, language, income, and digital access.

## Reading rule

Ask who carried the loss, who carried the recovery work, and whether the customer could still use the account while waiting.
