# When the power fails, payment becomes access

## The argument

A household can have money and still be unable to use it when electricity, communications, or a merchant terminal fails. In an outage, payment is not an abstract financial service. It helps decide whether food, medicine, fuel, or a needed trip can be completed.

Before a storm, people may stock up and use credit. During the outage, a card reader or network may stop working. Cash may keep one purchase moving, but only if the household has cash, the store is open, shelves are stocked, and the person can travel safely. A shift toward cash can show a useful backup, a failed preferred payment, or both.

The deeper finding is that payment resilience is part of basic access. The failure is not only “the card did not work.” It may become a missed prescription, a longer trip for fuel, expensive credit, or lost work time. The household account balance can remain unchanged while the household’s usable options shrink.

## Follow one essential purchase

A family loses power after a storm and needs medicine. The pharmacy can open but its terminal cannot connect. The family has some cash, but not enough for the full prescription, and the nearest working pharmacy is 20 miles away. A second card may work, or the family may delay treatment. Each backup has a different cost.

The payment channel, merchant, supply, transport, and household buffer all have to work together. A backup that works for a restaurant may not work for a pharmacy or fuel station.

```text
storm or infrastructure failure → electronic payment unavailable
→ cash, another card, offline tool, or no transaction
→ essential purchase completed or missed → household cost, health, work, and recovery change
```

## What the source actually establishes

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | NBER combines outage, weather, transaction, scanner, and survey data and finds spending rises before disasters, often using credit. | Households try to prepare before access becomes harder. |
| Channel failure | After outages, digital payments fall and cash use rises. | Payment methods change when networks fail. |
| Choice loss | Nearly half of surveyed consumers could not use their preferred electronic payment during an outage. | A preferred method may not be a usable method. |
| Backup | Greater cash access reduced the decline in completed purchases. | Cash can help, but does not solve supply, safety, or transport limits. |

Source: [NBER Working Paper 35115](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35115).

## The deeper finding

Payment is part of infrastructure because it connects stored money to a real purchase. Each link has a different owner: banks, networks, merchants, utilities, fuel providers, and households. A failure at one link can stop a purchase that the others could have served.

## What remains unproven

The study does not show which households missed food, medicine, fuel, or work access, or which backup design works best in every outage. It does not prove that every cash user was in hardship. Cash can be lost or stolen, and a long outage can empty shelves and stop fuel pumps.

## Next test

Follow households through a real outage for three months. Join payment attempts, cash access, merchant acceptance, offline tools, essential purchases, credit, work, care, health, transport, and recovery. Compare households with different backups but similar needs.

## Reading rule

Keep payment availability, purchase completion, essential need, and later loss separate. Having money is not the same as being able to use it.
