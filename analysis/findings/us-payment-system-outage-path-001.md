# When the power fails, payment becomes access

## The argument

A household can have money and still be unable to use it when electricity, communications, or a merchant terminal fails. In an outage, payment is not an abstract financial service. It decides whether food, medicine, fuel, or a needed trip can be completed.

Before a storm, people may stock up and use credit. During the outage, a card reader or network may stop working. Cash may keep one purchase moving, but only if the household has cash, the store is open, shelves are stocked, and the person can travel safely. A shift toward cash can show a useful backup, a failed preferred payment, or both.

The deeper finding is that payment resilience is part of basic access. The NBER evidence shows the transaction change; it does not yet show which households missed which essential needs or which backup design works best in every outage.

## What the source establishes

NBER combines outage, weather, transaction, scanner, and survey data. Spending rises before disasters as households stock up, often using credit. After outages, digital payments fall and cash use rises. Nearly half of surveyed consumers could not use their preferred electronic payment during an outage, while greater cash access reduced the decline in completed purchases.

That supports a clear mechanism: the failure of a payment channel can reduce completed purchases even when the account balance is unchanged. It does not prove that every cash user was in hardship, or that cash is a complete solution. Cash can be lost or stolen; a long outage can empty shelves and stop fuel pumps.

Source: [NBER Working Paper 35115](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35115).

## Follow the purchase

storm or infrastructure failure → electronic payment unavailable → cash, another card, offline tool, or no transaction → essential purchase completed or missed → household cost, health, work, and recovery change

Each link has a different owner. Banks, networks, merchants, utilities, and fuel providers may each control one part. A failure at one link can stop a purchase that all the other links could have served.

## Four questions that should stay separate

- **Availability:** Could the person reach a working payment channel?
- **Purchase:** Was food, medicine, fuel, care, or another essential actually obtained?
- **Cost:** Did the household pay a fee, use expensive credit, lose work, or travel farther?
- **Recovery:** How long until normal payment and supply returned?

## Next test

Follow households through a real outage for three months. Join payment attempts, cash access, merchant acceptance, offline tools, essential purchases, credit, work, care, health, and recovery. Compare households with different backups but similar needs, and split results by income, age, disability, race, broadband, transport, and place.

## Reading rule

Keep payment availability, purchase completion, essential need, and later loss separate. Having money is not the same as being able to use it.
