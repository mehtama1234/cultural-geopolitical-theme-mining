# When household choices become a judgment about the country

## Short answer

People can feel pressure in their own budget and still judge the national economy much more harshly. That gap matters because it is where prices, expectations, party identity, media and public action can meet.

The careful finding is not that higher prices cause distrust or a vote. It is that households are already changing what they buy, save, borrow and delay, while their personal and national economic judgments remain different measures. Any explanation of political feeling must follow both.

## What the sources show

### 1. Most adults changed something after higher prices

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey says 77% of adults took at least one action after prices rose. The most common were switching to cheaper products, using less or stopping use, delaying a major purchase, reducing savings, increasing borrowing or working more.

These answers allow more than one action. They show adjustment, not the size of the loss or which household need gave way.

[Federal Reserve income and expenses](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-income-and-expenses.htm)

### 2. Personal and national views do not line up

In the same survey, 73% of adults said they were doing okay financially or living comfortably. But only about one-fourth rated the national economy good or excellent. Price increases were still the most common financial concern.

This is not a contradiction that needs to be smoothed away. A person can be managing their own bills while believing that the country is moving in the wrong direction, or can blame national conditions for a future risk that has not yet reached their own home.

[Federal Reserve 2025 household report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-executive-summary.htm)

### 3. Trust is low, but party identity is a strong filter

Pew reports that 17% of Americans trusted the federal government to do what is right most or all of the time in September 2025. The result was 9% among Democrats and Democratic leaners and 26% among Republicans and Republican leaners.

The long trend shows low trust, but the party split warns us not to turn a price story into a trust story too quickly. People may read the same bill through different views of who governs, who is to blame and what information they believe.

[Pew public trust in government, 1958–2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/)

### 4. A benefit change may affect economic feeling, but that is a separate test

An NBER working paper studies whether losing part of the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit changed consumer sentiment. It is useful because it tests a material change against a reported belief, but it does not by itself prove a change in voting, party identity or trust in government.

[NBER: The Child Tax Credit and Consumer Sentiment](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35059)

### 5. Lost buying power may reach the vote differently than inflation

An NBER county study of the United States from 2021–2024 reports that falling real wages predicted Republican electoral gains, while inflation remained related to presidential vote share after accounting for real wages. This points to a useful distinction: people may respond to what their pay can buy, not only to the rate at which prices are rising.

The study compares counties and does not identify each voter's price basket, blame, turnout or reason for voting. It is evidence about a political pattern, not proof that one household's lost buying power caused its vote.

[NBER Working Paper 35301](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301)

## The connection

```text
price, bill, wage, or benefit change
  -> household switches, delays, saves less, borrows, or works more
  -> personal financial judgment
  -> national economic judgment filtered through party, place, media, and expectation
  -> demand for relief, blame, regulation, or political change
```

The first two steps are visible in the Fed survey. The county voting result adds a political pattern, but the trust and household-to-vote steps still need a design that can separate material change from party identity and the national information environment.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** Many adults report changing behavior after price increases. Personal financial well-being and national economic ratings differ. Trust in the federal government is low and varies by party.

**Inference:** A household may experience economic pressure as a national judgment before it reaches a clear political action. The gap between managing personally and fearing nationally is a useful place to look for deeper change.

**Unknown:** We do not know whether a specific price change caused a change in trust, blame, turnout, vote or policy demand. We also do not know how much local prices, media, party identity or expected future costs explain the gap.

## What would change the finding

- A panel following the same household's prices, actions, financial view and political view over time.
- Local price and service measures matched to survey respondents' places.
- Tests that compare people with similar party identity but different material exposure.
- Evidence that a benefit or price change altered behavior without altering economic or political views.
- A serious counterexample where large household pressure produced no change in public judgment.

## Next test

Use the Fed SHED files to cross-tab price actions with personal and national economic views by income, age, race, disability and place. Add the NBER benefit-loss design as a separate causal test. Do not combine trust, sentiment and voting into one outcome.

## Reading rule

A changed shopping choice is not a changed political belief. A low trust number is not proof that prices caused it. Keep the household action, the judgment and the political response as three separate steps.
