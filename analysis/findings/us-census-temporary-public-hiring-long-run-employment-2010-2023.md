# Finding: A temporary public job can be a bridge—but not for everyone in the same way

**Status:** bounded public-hiring, labor-attachment, and unequal-conversion finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census study looks at the more than 850,000 temporary jobs created for the
2010 Decennial Census. It compares applicants who were very close to the test
score needed to receive a job. For these marginal applicants, getting the
Census job sharply increased the chance of having W-2 employment in 2010—by
about 61 percentage points.

The difference did not disappear immediately. By 2023, the marginal applicants
who got the Census job were about 28 percentage points more likely to have a
W-2 job than comparable applicants who were not hired. Their later wages, among
people who were working, were not significantly different; the main difference
was whether they were working at all. The long-term effect was concentrated
among men.

But the bridge has a cost and a boundary. Across the broader applicant pool,
the short-term employment gain was smaller and faded by 2011. Low-score hires
were also more than twice as likely to be terminated for misconduct or poor
performance. The public job created opportunity for some people while placing
management and work-quality burdens on the agency.

```text
public hiring test and local job opening
  -> temporary government job, income, training, and résumé signal
  -> later employer attachment or no lasting change
  -> wages, household room, work identity, and public trust [open]
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a public-capacity version of the atlas's support-versus-security
tension. Government does not only transfer money or regulate a labor market;
it can temporarily become an employer. For people near the edge of employment,
the job may provide a bridge into later work even when the position itself ends.

The result also shows why averages can mislead. Most applicants who received a
Census job would probably have worked anyway, while the marginal group gained
much more. A public-hiring program can therefore look modest overall and still
be consequential for people with few alternatives. The same program can have
different effects by sex and test-score position.

There is a signal mechanism here as well as a wage mechanism. Passing a hiring
test, background check, training, and a trust-sensitive public assignment may
give a worker a credential that private employers recognize. The study cannot
separate those channels, but it keeps “temporary pay” distinct from “later
employer attachment.”

## Limits and counterexamples

- The strongest estimate is local: it applies to non-veteran applicants just
  around the Census hiring-test threshold, not to everyone who wants a public
  job.
- The 2010 Census hired during and after the Great Recession, with a specific
  test, background check, training process, work schedule, and trust-sensitive
  enumeration role. Effects may differ in a stronger labor market or another
  public program.
- W-2 employment and wages do not measure job quality, hours, control, health,
  care, debt, housing, or household security.
- The long-run gain was concentrated among male marginal applicants; the study
  does not establish equal benefits for women or other groups.
- A short-term public job can displace private employment for people who had
  better outside options. The broader applicant effect was much smaller and
  faded by 2011.
- The study observes some termination outcomes but not the full cost of
  management time, replacement, work quality, or worker experience.

## Next test

Compare temporary public jobs by task, training, supervisor, schedule,
location, hiring threshold, and labor-market conditions. Follow applicants from
application through hiring, hours, termination or completion, next employer,
pay, benefits, job quality, household room, and later public-sector trust.
Measure whether the bridge works through income, training, a résumé signal,
new contacts, or simply a temporary period of labor-market attachment.
