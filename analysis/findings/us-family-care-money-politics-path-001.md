# From family time to money and economic views

## Short answer

Family care is work, but it is often counted first as private time. When a person helps an older relative, the change may show up in work hours, earnings, savings, or the need for paid help before it appears in an economic report.

The careful finding is that care creates a possible money and public-life path. The sources show how common unpaid eldercare is and connect reported care to long-run earnings records. They do not show that care alone changes borrowing, trust, or a vote.

## What the sources show

BLS reports that 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24. Fifty-five percent were women, and care providers spent an average of 3.9 hours on days when they provided care. Among providers who were also parents of children at home, 86% were employed and 72% worked full time.

An NBER study links reported family caregiving to administrative earnings records over 25 years and finds different labor effects by gender. This is evidence that care and work can be connected over time, not proof that every caregiver loses earnings or leaves work.

The sources do not measure the same person's full care cost, savings, debt, health, housing, trust, and political action.

## The connection

```text
need for family care
  -> unpaid time, paid care, or both
  -> work hours and earnings change
  -> savings, debt, health, or housing room changes
  -> view of employers and public support changes
  -> demand for a different care rule
```

Care can be a burden, but it can also be chosen, loving, and useful. The question is who can absorb the time and who must give up work or pay for help.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A family may trade work time for care, ask another relative for help, or buy care. The same care need has a different money effect depending on wages, savings, distance, and health.

**Company and sector:** Employers set schedules, leave, remote-work options, and benefits. Care support may keep a worker in a job, but a benefit that cannot be used at the needed time may have little value.

**Money and finance:** The missing account is the full one: lost earnings, paid care, transport, debt, savings, medical costs, and the value of the care provided. A simple wage measure misses the trade.

**Public power:** Tax credits, leave, care supply, health coverage, and work rules shape whether care stays inside the family or becomes a paid or public need. A policy claim needs evidence of take-up and later household change.

## What would change the finding

- Care start and end dates for the same person and family.
- Work hours, earnings, job changes, leave, and promotion over time.
- Paid care, travel, health, debt, savings, and housing costs.
- Employer support, public benefits, caregiver tax credits, and actual use.
- Differences by gender, income, race, age, disability, family structure, and place.
- Trust, complaints, local policy, and political action after a care shock.

## Next test

Follow families for two years from the start of a regular care need. Record who provides the hours, what work changes, what money is spent or lost, which support is available, and whether the family asks for a public or employer response. Keep the value of care separate from the cost of carrying it.

## Reading rule

Do not call family care an economic loss without measuring what it provided. Do not call it free without measuring whose time, income, and future choices carried it.
