# Finding: Bigger hospitals do not necessarily mean easier local care

**Status:** bounded hospital-infrastructure, place-access, and regional-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census studied hospitals with at least 50 employees from 1987 through 2019.
The number of hospital establishments fell by more than 20% from its late-1980s
peak, while hospital employment rose by more than 40%. Average hospital size
grew by about 75%.

At the same time, more counties had no hospital meeting the study definition.
In the decade beginning in 2009, 26.9% of Southern counties and 19.7% of
counties elsewhere had no such hospital. The gap was larger in rural places:
39.2% of rural Southern counties and 30.1% of rural counties elsewhere had no
study-defined hospital.

The pattern is therefore not simply “health care shrank.” It is closer to:
fewer and larger anchors, with access becoming more dependent on distance,
transport, neighboring places, insurance, emergency capacity, and digital
infrastructure.

## What this adds to the broader theme atlas

This is an infrastructure version of the atlas’s capacity-versus-usability
theme:

```text
hospital consolidation and employment growth
  -> fewer local establishments and larger catchment areas
  -> travel, transport, broadband, insurance, and provider constraints
  -> local care reached, delayed, substituted, or missed
  -> health, household cost, work time, trust, and political response [open]
```

An industry can employ more people and still become less locally reachable.
The same project can look efficient from an establishment or employment count
while feeling like loss of local protection to a rural resident.

## Limits and counterexamples

- A “hospital” means a NAICS 622 establishment with at least 50 employees; this
  is not every clinic, emergency site, outpatient provider, or telehealth option.
- A county with no hospital may still have nearby care across a county line.
- The study does not measure patient travel, appointment availability, quality,
  price, insurance acceptance, emergency transport, or health outcomes.
- Fewer establishments and more employment suggest consolidation but do not
  directly document every merger, ownership change, or causal closure mechanism.
- Telehealth may offset some distance, but the paper does not measure whether
  patients had usable broadband, devices, privacy, or services that could be
  substituted virtually.
- Policy discussion about future broadband or Medicaid effects is conjectural,
  not an estimate of outcomes under those policies.

The next decisive artifact would connect one county and patient episode through
hospital opening/closure, travel time, transport, insurance, appointment,
emergency use, telehealth access, care quality, cost, work time, and health
outcome.
