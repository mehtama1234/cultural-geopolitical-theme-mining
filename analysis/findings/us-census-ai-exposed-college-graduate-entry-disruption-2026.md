# Finding: AI can change the first job before it changes the whole career

**Status:** bounded technology, early-career, and unequal-opportunity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census study compares new bachelor's graduates from majors with different
levels of exposure to AI-related tasks. It finds that the most exposed group
had a weaker start after ChatGPT became widely available: employment just after
graduation fell by about 5 percentage points relative to the least-exposed
comparison group, and full-quarter earnings fell by about 13%.

This was not only a lower paycheck in the same kind of job. Roughly half of the
earnings decline came from a shift away from higher-paying Information and
Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services toward lower-paying Retail and
Accommodation and Food Services. The effect became smaller as graduates moved
away from the entry point, but the most exposed majors still showed lower
employment and earnings estimates around two years later.

The plain social meaning is that education can still produce a credential while
the first available work changes underneath it. A person can remain attached to
the labor market and still enter through a weaker door, with less pay, a lower
sector, and less room to build early experience.

```text
college major and prior skill investment
  -> exposure to changing AI-related labor demand
  -> first job, delay, or lower-paying sector
  -> earnings, job switching, training, and skill accumulation
  -> later career, household room, and political meaning [open]
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This adds a generational version of the program's recurring capacity-versus-
optionality tension. A technology can raise productive capacity while making
the entry route narrower for the people who prepared for the affected work.
The first job matters because it is where graduates begin to accumulate
experience, contacts, earnings, and a record that later employers can read.

It also sharpens a distinction between displacement and downgrading. The study
does not show that exposed graduates simply disappear from work. Many find work
in another sector, sometimes later switching jobs. That adjustment can conceal
the cost of the initial mismatch if evaluation records only eventual
employment and not the path taken to reach it.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The filtered sample contains about 6.7 million graduate records, roughly 29%
  of bachelor's degrees, from 27 data partners in 24 states. Participating
  institutions are not the whole higher-education system.
- The LEHD outcome is observed UI-covered employment and earnings; it does not
  measure informal work, job quality, learning, health, debt, housing, or
  household security.
- AI exposure is a pre-period major-level assignment based on occupation-task
  measures and survey occupation distributions. It is not a record of whether
  a particular firm adopted AI or whether a particular graduate used it.
- The event-time evidence is consistent with an AI-related shock, but the paper
  cannot identify one mechanism or eliminate every coincident field, industry,
  macroeconomic, or post-pandemic change.
- Effects attenuate for many exposure groups over one to two years, and the
  short post-ChatGPT period cannot establish whether the top-exposure group
  eventually catches up.

## Next test

Follow graduates from major and institution through application, interview,
offer, delay, first employer, industry, pay, hours, training, job switching,
debt, housing, and later earnings. Compare actual employer AI adoption and
entry-level task changes with the pre-period exposure measure. Preserve
graduates who do not appear in UI-covered employment and distinguish a later
job from a restored career path.
