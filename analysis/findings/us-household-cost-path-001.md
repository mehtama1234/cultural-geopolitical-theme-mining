# When household protection becomes a public feeling

## Short answer

Three separate studies point to three different ways a household can feel a cost: a higher bill to protect a home, a payment system that rewards some shoppers more than others, and the loss of a child benefit. Together they suggest a useful line of inquiry: household pressure may be felt through both money paid now and money no longer arriving.

This is a connected reading, not a proven chain. The sources do not follow the same households from insurance payments to card use to economic views.

## What the sources show

### 1. Protection can raise the monthly cost of keeping a home

Keys and Mulder study more than 74 million insurance premiums inferred from mortgage escrow payments from 2014 through 2024. They estimate that rising premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes.

This means the cost can appear twice: as a larger bill and as slower growth in the value of the home. The estimate is for the most exposed ZIP codes; it is not a $40,000 loss for every homeowner.

[NBER Working Paper 32579](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579)

### 2. The checkout price can hide who funds rewards

Egan, Matvos, Seru, Wang and Yao use merchant payment data to estimate that interchange fees move about $30 billion each year from cash and debit users to credit-card users. Different shoppers and different merchant fees reduce the estimated transfer by about 25%, but do not remove it.

The result is an estimate about the payment system, not a direct bill sent to each cash or debit user. It depends on how merchants pass fees into prices and on where different shoppers buy things.

[NBER Working Paper 35067](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067)

### 3. Losing a benefit can change how people judge the economy

Bastian and Harvey estimate that every $1,000 lost when the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit expired lowered consumer sentiment by 1.7 points. The effect lasted nearly two years and was largest among lower-income families with several children.

This gives us evidence that a change in money arriving at home can affect economic views. The study does not show that the change altered votes.

[NBER Working Paper 35059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35059)

## The careful connection

The three findings fit one question: what does a household experience when the money available for protection, purchases or children changes? A home-insurance bill reaches the monthly budget and the value of an asset. Payment rules shape the price paid by people using different methods. A lost benefit changes the income a family expected to receive.

The sources support those separate statements. They do not prove that insurance costs caused a person to change payment method, or that either caused a political response. The useful finding at this stage is that “cost of living” has more than one clock: bills can rise, assets can grow more slowly, and expected support can disappear.

## What would change the finding

- Same-household data linking insurance costs, payment method, income and economic views.
- A comparison of households that lost different amounts of protection or public support.
- Evidence on whether people cut care, borrowing, saving or housing quality first.
- State and local records showing whether these pressures became complaints, policy demands or votes.

## Next test

Pair the Federal Reserve’s household survey measures with insurance and payment records by state and income group. Treat this as a comparison of related pressures until a dataset follows the same people over time.
