# The person paying the bill may not control the fix

## Short answer

Renters often pay for energy while landlords control major building and appliance upgrades. DOE describes this as a split incentive: the cost falls on one party and the savings on another. EIA finds that energy-insecure households paid more per square foot, with low-income renters paying more than comparable owners in the cited data. On-bill financing is one possible bridge because the cost can follow the property and the bill. The deeper finding is that energy efficiency can fail as a household solution when the person carrying the monthly burden cannot choose the repair or upgrade.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Layer | What the evidence says | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Bill | EIA finds higher energy bills per square foot among energy-insecure households and higher cited bills for very low-income renters than owners. | Whether the difference comes from building condition, behavior, rates, or control. |
| Control | DOE says landlords may lack a direct financial reason to fund upgrades when tenants receive the bill savings. | Which owners delay which upgrades and why. |
| Access | Renters can use some low-cost measures and may qualify for incentives. | Whether landlords permit, fund, or maintain the improvement. |
| Bridge | Inclusive utility investment can attach upgrade cost to the property and bill. | Actual savings, comfort, rent, and move outcomes. |
| Household result | Lower bills can improve comfort and free money for food, care, or debt. | Same-renter bill and housing path before and after an upgrade. |

Sources: [DOE LEAD split-incentive guidance](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=56640), [DOE renter guidance](https://www.energy.gov/save/renters), and [ENERGY STAR inclusive utility investment](https://www.energystar.gov/products/inclusive_utility_investment).

## The connection

```text
renter pays energy bill
  → landlord controls building upgrade
  → upgrade is delayed, funded, or shared through a bill tool
  → bill, comfort, rent, and property value change
  → energy burden and housing stability change
```

The sources support the control problem and possible financing bridge. They do not yet measure a common renter outcome across upgraded and unchanged homes.

## The useful surprise

The person with the strongest reason to save energy may have the least power to change the home. A renter can pay a high bill every month and still be unable to replace a window, appliance, heating system, or insulation.

## Four views

**Household:** Count bill, comfort, repair requests, rent, move cost, and money left for other needs.

**Customer and resident:** Record who controls the equipment, who pays, who receives the savings, and whether the tenant can stay during work.

**Money and finance:** Separate upgrade cost, rebate, on-bill charge, rent change, bill savings, and debt.

**Public power:** Test rental standards, incentives, disclosure, utility programs, landlord duties, and tenant protection from rent pass-through.

## What would change the finding

- Same-home energy bills before and after an upgrade, linked to rent and tenure.
- Landlord investment, rebate, financing, maintenance, and rent records.
- Comfort, shutoff, health, move, and housing-stability outcomes.
- Results by income, race, disability, building type, climate, and utility.
- Comparison of direct aid, landlord incentives, on-bill finance, and minimum standards.

## Reading rule

Do not say renters received an efficiency benefit until you show who paid for the fix, who controlled it, and who kept the savings.
