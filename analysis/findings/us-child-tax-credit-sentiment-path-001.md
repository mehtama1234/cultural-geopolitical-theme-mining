# When public help changes the story of the economy

## Short answer

A benefit can change how a family sees its own finances and the country's economy, not only how much money it has today. When the benefit ends, the loss may remain in the family's economic mood for a long time.

Sentiment is its own result. It is not automatically a change in spending, trust, or voting.

## What the sources show

Using monthly University of Michigan consumer-sentiment data and differences in likely benefit loss, NBER estimates that each $1,000 lost from the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit reduced sentiment by 1.7 points. The effect was larger among lower-income families with multiple children and persisted nearly two years.

Other NBER research links the monthly credit to less back-owed rent or mortgage and changes in living arrangements. HBS reports that more money can give people more control over daily problems and reduce stress.

The sentiment study does not show the same family's spending, saving, policy support, trust, or vote after the benefit changed.

- [NBER Working Paper 35059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35059)
- [NBER Working Paper 31339](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31339)
- [HBS: More Proof That Money Can Buy Happiness](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/more-proof-that-money-can-buy-happiness)

## The connection

```text
benefit starts or ends
  -> household cash and housing room change
  -> people judge their own financial position differently
  -> consumer sentiment changes
  -> spending, trust, blame, or voting may change
```

Families also judge the economy from prices, jobs, and news. A benefit can change sentiment without changing long-run finances or political behavior.

## The four outside views

**Household:** Cash can reduce a missed payment and make daily problems easier. Losing it can make the future feel smaller before every bill changes.

**Company and sector:** Retailers, landlords, lenders, employers, and care providers may see spending or payment changes. Their response can shape the household's next choices.

**Money and finance:** The real effect includes cash, debt, rent, food, care, savings, and sentiment. A lower mood can coexist with stable work or housing.

**Public power:** Benefit size, timing, eligibility, renewal, public messaging, and program end dates shape both the money and the story people tell about government.

## What would change the finding

- Actual benefit use, income, spending, saving, debt, food, housing, and care.
- Personal versus national economic belief and the prices each family faces.
- News, party identity, local jobs, policy support, trust, turnout, and vote.
- Household sentiment before, during, and after benefit loss.
- Effects by income, family size, race, state, and housing condition.

## Next test

Follow families before, during, and two years after a benefit starts or ends. Join benefit records, cash, spending, housing, debt, sentiment, trust, policy support, and voting where permitted.

## Reading rule

Keep benefit exposure, money, sentiment, policy support, and voting separate. A lower sentiment score is not by itself a political action.
