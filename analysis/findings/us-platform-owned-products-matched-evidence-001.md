# A useful product can still sit inside a power problem

## The argument

A platform’s own product can help a customer by fitting a need and reducing search time. The same platform can control the shelf: search placement, seller access, advertising, fulfillment, and customer data. The current customer gain may be real while the future market becomes more dependent on one gatekeeper.

The right question is not whether a platform-owned product is always good or bad. It is whether today’s convenience is bought with tomorrow’s higher price, weaker seller entry, poorer information, or fewer choices. Those are separate outcomes and need separate evidence.

Ownership alone does not reveal the problem. A platform may make a product because outside sellers do not offer the right price, size, or delivery. The concern begins when the firm can use the same data to learn what sells, decide which rivals appear, and make its own item look like the neutral answer. A customer can receive a good product and still be inside a market where the rules are difficult for others to challenge.

## Follow one search result

A shopper searches for a common product. The platform’s own item appears first, fits the need, and arrives quickly. Hiding it could make the shopper worse off today. But if the platform also controls rival sellers’ data and visibility, its position may affect which sellers survive and what customers can choose later.

The conflict is not proved by ownership alone. It appears when the same firm can use information from the shelf to shape the shelf, then make the customer believe the resulting list is a neutral market.

The customer sees one screen, but the platform is making several decisions at once: what to show, what to call sponsored, which seller can use fulfillment, which offer wins the purchase button, and what data is available after the sale. Those choices can improve matching or steer demand. They should not be treated as one act called “ranking.” Each can change the seller's cost and the buyer's belief.

Rivals may also depend on the platform to reach customers. A seller can keep its own website and still lose access to the buyers who search on the platform, or pay for ads and fulfillment to make its offer usable. A platform-owned item may therefore compete against rivals using a road that the platform controls. The important comparison is not only the product price; it is the terms under which a rival can reach the same customer.

The effect can differ by category. In a commodity category, a platform product may discipline price and improve delivery. In a specialty category, customer reviews, repeat purchases, and data about demand may give the platform a stronger advantage. A single market-wide claim can miss these different paths.

```text
platform-owned product + search control
  -> useful choice and less search today
  -> seller access, data, and visibility change
  -> entry and competition change
  -> future price, quality, and choice
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| An NBER field experiment found a short-run customer loss when some Amazon-owned brands were hidden; estimated welfare fell 5.5% in the tested setting. | Removing a useful product can hurt today. | One experiment is not the whole market. |
| Moving the brands lower did not raise estimated welfare in the study. | The answer is not simply “push it down.” | It does not test every ranking design. |
| Amazon reports revenue from outside-seller services, advertising, fulfillment, and its own retail business. | One platform has several interests at once. | A filing does not prove how each rule affects sellers. |
| The FTC and states allege links among seller prices, data, advertising, Featured Offers, Prime, and promotion. | Platform rules may shape rival access and customer choice. | Allegations are not final findings. |
| NBER models data neutrality and competition. | Data access can matter to future market structure. | A model is not an observed long-run result. |

Sources: [NBER field experiment](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34135), [Amazon 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000004/amzn-20251231.htm), [FTC Amazon case record](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/1910129-1910130-amazoncom-inc-amazon-ecommerce), and [NBER data-neutrality model](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35159).

## The deeper finding

A platform-owned product changes two time horizons. It may improve the current purchase while changing the conditions under which future sellers enter, compete, and reach customers. The customer may save search time today and lose a rival tomorrow.

That does not justify removing every own-brand item. It does require a two-part test. Measure the current purchase—fit, price, delivery, quality, and search time. Then measure the market path—seller entry, exit, dependence, variety, price, and the ability to reach customers without buying visibility from the same gatekeeper.

The deeper issue is information asymmetry. The platform can observe searches, conversion, returns, complaints, and seller performance across the whole shelf. Each rival sees only its own slice. If the platform's product gets an early advantage, the resulting data can make that advantage self-reinforcing. This is an inference to test, not a conclusion from ownership alone.

There is also a customer-protection tradeoff. The platform may be able to trace, refund, and fulfill its own product more reliably than an unknown seller. A rule that limits its reach could push customers toward less accountable sellers. A rule that gives it unlimited preference could leave customers with fewer independent choices later. The useful design may depend on clear labeling, equal access to core services, portable seller data, and a remedy that does not require the platform to be the only seller.

The political question is who controls the conditions of ordinary buying. When one firm controls discovery, payment, fulfillment, reviews, and a competing product, a customer is not only choosing an item. The customer is using a private market with its own rules. Public oversight has to ask whether those rules preserve a real path for rivals and a real remedy for buyers.

## What remains unknown

The experiment measures a short-run customer effect in one setting. Company filings, enforcement allegations, and models do not yet follow one customer or seller through years of ranking, data access, price, quality, entry, and exit.

They also do not show whether the platform's own product was better because of scale, better because of preferential placement, or both. We do not know how much seller data was used in each decision, whether rivals could reach customers on equal terms, or whether a customer would choose the product if the listing were clearly labeled and the alternatives were equally visible.

## Next test

Track seller entry, exit, prices, quality, product variety, search placement, sponsored placement, and customer outcomes before and after platform-owned products appear. Separate small and large sellers, and distinguish a useful product from preferential treatment that blocks a substitute.

Preserve the search page, labels, offers, delivery promise, review set, and seller terms seen by each customer. Compare the platform product with matched rivals on fit, total price, delivery, returns, quality, and customer time. Track how rival traffic, margin, entry, exit, ad spending, and access to fulfillment change. Test equal placement, clear ownership labels, portable data, and a neutral purchase button where feasible. Measure both the customer's immediate outcome and whether a capable rival can still reach the customer next year.

## Reading rule

Do not call platform-owned products harmful because the platform controls the shelf, or helpful because some customers prefer them. Measure today’s gain and tomorrow’s market power separately.
