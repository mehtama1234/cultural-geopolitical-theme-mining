# When local buying power reaches the ballot

## Short answer

People may respond to what their pay can buy, not simply to the inflation rate in a national report. If local prices rise faster than local pay, a household can feel less room even while another household in the same county feels better off.

The careful finding is a county-level link between falling real wages and election results. It does not show how each person voted, which price mattered, who they blamed, or whether a price change caused the result.

## What the source shows

An NBER study uses US county-level family-budget costs, nominal income, and presidential and congressional results from 2021 to 2024. It reports that a decline in real wages—not higher inflation alone—predicted Republican gains. Inflation still had an association with presidential vote share beyond a simple economic-voting measure.

Separate NBER survey work finds that people dislike inflation mainly when they believe wages do not keep up with prices, and that this belief is linked to reported shopping and saving choices. The county and survey evidence describe related parts of a path, but they do not follow the same household from prices to a vote.

- [NBER Working Paper 35301: Do Voters Punish Inflation or Pay Cuts?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301)
- [NBER Working Paper 32300: Why Do We Dislike Inflation?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32300)

## The connection

```text
local prices and wages
  -> real buying power and household room
  -> buying less, saving less, borrowing, or delaying a need
  -> blame, trust, and view of the economy
  -> turnout, vote choice, or support for change
```

People vote on more than money. Party identity, immigration, abortion, safety, age, media, and local candidates can outweigh economic conditions. County averages also hide large household differences.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A family compares rent, food, fuel, care, debt, and wages. A national price number may matter less than the bill that rose this month and the paycheck that did not.

**Company and sector:** Employers, stores, landlords, and local service firms shape prices, pay, hours, and product choice. A local wage can rise while basic costs rise faster.

**Money and finance:** Measure real income after the household's actual basket, debt, saving, and credit cost. Borrowing can hide a loss for a time and create a later bill.

**Public power:** A government may be rewarded or punished for local economic conditions it did not fully control. Clear records of policy timing, local exposure, and alternative causes are needed before assigning blame.

## What would change the finding

- Individual prices, wages, debt, savings, and household budget over time.
- Local exposure to housing, food, fuel, care, and transport costs.
- The policy, employer, industry, or supply change behind the price or wage movement.
- What voters knew, which source they trusted, and who they blamed.
- Turnout, vote choice, party identity, age, race, income, and place.
- Results after real wages recover: trust, spending, turnout, and the next vote.

## Next test

Join local prices and wages to a household panel for two election cycles. Follow each family's spending, saving, borrowing, media, policy knowledge, trust, turnout, and vote choice. Compare a fall in real pay with a rise in inflation that leaves real pay unchanged.

## Reading rule

Do not say voters punish inflation when the evidence may show a loss of real pay. Do not turn a county pattern into a story about every voter.
