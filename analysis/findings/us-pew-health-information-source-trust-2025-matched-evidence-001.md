# Americans use many health-information sources, but deciding what to trust remains hard

## The deeper finding

Pew surveyed 5,111 U.S. adults in October 2025. Health care providers were
still the most common source: 85% said they get health information from
providers at least sometimes, and 65% of provider users rated that information
extremely or very accurate.

But the health-information system is plural. Sixty-six percent get information
from people with a similar health issue, 36% from social media, and 22% from AI
chatbots at least sometimes. Seventy-six percent encounter conflicting health
information at least sometimes; 54% say it is at least somewhat difficult to
know which source to trust when that happens. Social-media users were much less
likely to rate that information highly accurate: 7% did so, while 47% rated it
not too or not at all accurate.

```text
health question or concern
  -> clinician, website, peer, news, agency, social media, or AI source
  -> convenience, familiarity, accuracy judgment, conflicting claims
  -> [open] advice, care-seeking, treatment, adherence, health outcome
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This adds a cultural-legitimacy layer to the health and digital lanes. The
public is not simply abandoning experts for platforms. People still turn most
often to clinicians, while also using peers and newer sources that may be more
available, familiar, or easy to ask. The unsettled point is the translation
from information abundance to a trusted decision.

It also qualifies “trust” as a practical resource. Provider information can be
trusted and still be hard to reach because of insurance, cost, time, or a lack
of a regular clinician. Conversely, a source can be convenient without being
seen as accurate. Cultural meaning emerges at that junction: people decide whom
to believe while managing the limits of institutions, platforms, and their own
time.

## What the report does not establish

- The results are self-reported perceptions, not clinical fact-checking of the
  claims respondents encountered.
- Source use does not show that a person followed the advice or received care.
- The survey does not measure treatment quality, adherence, health outcomes, or
  misinformation prevalence.
- Conditional accuracy ratings use different denominators because only users of
  each source rate it.
- Provider trust does not prove provider access, affordability, or continuity.

## Next test

Follow a protected health question from source exposure through claim
verification, clinician contact, care-seeking, treatment, cost, adherence, and
health outcome. Record whether the source was institutional, peer, platform, or
AI, and whether the person had insurance, time, money, and a usable alternative.

## Reading rule

Do not treat source use as belief, belief as accuracy, or accuracy judgment as
health success. Ask what happened after the information was encountered.
