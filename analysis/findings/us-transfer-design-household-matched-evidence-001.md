# The same dollar can do different work depending on how it arrives

## Short answer

A dollar of public aid is not experienced as the same dollar when it is food or cash, or when it arrives once or every month. NBER finds larger short-run food-store spending responses from in-kind and recurring aid. A separate US cash experiment found more spending on housing, food, and cars, but little average change in net worth.

The deeper finding is about timing and choice. A label may protect food spending. Cash may cover rent, medicine, transport, or a repair. Recurring aid may help planning. None of these short-run responses alone proves lasting household security.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Food aid | NBER 35698 finds food-store spending responses of 0.38 for recurring in-kind aid and 0.18 for one-time in-kind aid. | Better food security or health for every family. |
| Cash aid | The same study finds food-store responses of 0.20 for recurring cash and 0.06 for one-time cash. | That cash is worse; it may pay a different urgent bill. |
| Basic needs | NBER 32784 finds monthly cash raised spending, especially on housing, food, and cars, and increased mobility. | Which need mattered most for one household. |
| Balance sheet | The cash experiment found higher debt offset modest asset gains, leaving near-zero average net-worth change. | That households gained no useful room or choice. |
| Design choice | The evidence is consistent with labels, timing, and forward-looking planning changing use. | The best design for every need, place, or family. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 35698](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35698), [NBER Working Paper 32784](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32784), [NBER Working Paper 30379](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30379).

## The connection

```text
public need
  -> aid amount, form, and timing
  -> what the household can buy or postpone now
  -> food, housing, transport, debt, care, or savings choice
  -> short-run relief or lasting security
```

The policy does not only choose how much money arrives. It also sets what the money points toward and when the family can count on it.

## Four views

**Household:** Ask what bill is due, what food is needed, and what a program label prevents or permits.

**Customer and worker:** Spending can reach groceries, rent, cars, care, or local shops; the category is not the same as the household's full need.

**Money and finance:** Measure cash, debt, assets, missed bills, credit, and the cost of waiting.

**Public power:** Judge a design by food security, housing, health, choice, take-up, cost, and trust—not only by the first purchases.

## What would change the finding

- The same household's aid form, timing, spending, food security, skipped bills, debt, care, health, mobility, savings, and trust.
- Results outside pandemic-era programs and outside food-store data.
- Effects by income, family size, race, place, disability, housing, and access to stores or banks.
- Administrative cost, take-up, stigma, and the work required to use each design.
- Two-year evidence showing whether early spending becomes safer housing, food, health, or financial room.

## Next test

Compare cash and food aid with the same value and timing for two years. Track what the family bought, what it postponed, what debt changed, and whether its next shock was easier to handle.

## Reading rule

Do not call a larger short-run purchase response a better policy. Ask what the family needed, what the aid let it protect, and what risk remained.
