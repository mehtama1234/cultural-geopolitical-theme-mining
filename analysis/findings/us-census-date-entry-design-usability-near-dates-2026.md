# Finding: A form can feel easy while different designs produce different results

**Status:** bounded public-interface, usability, and administrative-access finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census tested three ways to enter a date: typing it, choosing from
dropdowns, or using a calendar. About 1,000 people completed near-date tasks.

The calendar worked better than typing for getting the answer right, and it
was faster than both typing and dropdowns for most tasks. The difference was
especially useful when people had to know which day of the week a date fell on.

Yet participants did not say that one design felt harder. Most rated all three
designs as extremely easy.

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a small but important version of the program's access theme:

```text
public form design
  -> memory, navigation, and device demands
  -> correct and timely completion
  -> record accepted or task abandoned
  -> access to a public service, benefit, or voice [open]
```

People's stated feeling that a form is easy can coexist with measurable
differences in accuracy and speed. So “the form is available” and “people can
successfully use it” are different claims.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is a usability experiment, not a population estimate of government
  access.
- It tests near dates; distant dates may produce different results.
- The abstract does not provide full results by disability, language, device,
  literacy, age, or digital skill.
- Better task performance does not prove lower burden, greater trust, or a
  better later outcome.
- The study does not follow a person from form completion to benefit receipt,
  correction, or political participation.

The next decisive artifact is an accessibility-tested public-task ledger linking
interface, device, language, disability accommodation, completion, correction,
dropout, processing time, and the actual service or record outcome.
