# Outsourcing an initial review does not outsource final safety responsibility

## The deeper finding

FDA lets accredited outside organizations conduct initial reviews of some 510(k) medical-device submissions, but FDA makes the final decision about whether a device may be marketed. GAO reports that third parties reviewed 617 submissions from fiscal years 2018 through 2025—about 2% of CDRH’s annual 510(k) reviews. FDA ultimately found 526 of those submissions substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate device.

The route increases review capacity, but it also creates an oversight clock. FDA conducted 25 periodic audits of third parties from 2000 through 2026. GAO found that audit policies lacked target times for completing audits and communicating findings; some recent deficiency audits took more than six months to close. Two third parties supplied 90% of the third-party reviews, and nine had active accreditations as of May 2026.

```text
device sponsor
  -> accredited outside initial review
  -> FDA review of recommendation
  -> final market decision
  -> periodic audit and correction
  -> [open] device use, safety, clinical benefit, and patient outcome
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a health-infrastructure version of a recurring pattern: capacity can be expanded through delegation while responsibility remains centralized. The outside reviewer may reduce FDA review time, but the public safety route still depends on FDA’s final review, accreditation standards, audit timing, and correction process.

The concentration matters as a resilience question, not automatically as a competition claim. If two organizations provide most reviews, interruption, loss of expertise, or a delayed audit could affect a large share of the delegated route. The report does not establish that this happened; it identifies a dependency worth tracking.

## What the report does not establish

- A final “substantially equivalent” determination is not proof of clinical effectiveness or patient safety.
- The 617 submissions are not all devices and represent only a small share of CDRH reviews.
- An audit delay does not prove that a defective device reached patients.
- Concentration among two organizations does not by itself show market power or poor quality.
- The report does not link a named device to audit timing, market exposure, adverse events, patient use, or correction.

## Next test

Build a device-level ledger linking sponsor, reviewer, accreditation status, review time, FDA final decision, audit status, deficiency, correction, post-market safety signal, price, access, and patient outcome. Preserve the difference between review capacity, regulatory authorization, actual use, and clinical benefit.

## Reading rule

Do not call a device safe because it was reviewed, accredited, or found substantially equivalent. Follow the device through final authorization, use, safety signals, correction, and patient outcome.
