# The fare is only one part of the ride

## The argument

Rideshare competition looks healthy when two apps show two prices. But the customer can benefit from that difference only if they can see it, compare it, and wait long enough to act. The real purchase is not a fare. It is a bundle of fare, pickup time, chance of cancellation, account history, safety features, and the effort of checking another app.

That changes the meaning of a price gap. A rider going to dinner may compare. A nurse ending a late shift, a parent approaching a child-care closing time, or a person carrying groceries may pay the first acceptable quote because delay has a cost. The platform that becomes the default screen can keep customer attention even when it is not the cheapest option.

The deeper finding is not simply that apps charge different prices. It is that search friction can weaken competition. When the cost of comparison is measured in minutes, uncertainty, and missed obligations, “choice” exists on paper but may be hard to use in the moment.

## Follow one ride

A worker finishes late. One app quotes $28 and another quotes $23. The second app is not free to check: it takes time to open, locate the trip, enter a destination, and judge whether the cheaper car will arrive soon enough. The first app may also warn that the quote can change. The worker accepts $28. That is not proof of exploitation. It is a decision made under a deadline.

Now repeat the decision twice a week. The extra $5 becomes about $520 a year before tips. If the worker has no record of the cheaper quote, the cost disappears into many small decisions. If enough riders behave this way, a platform can benefit from being the place people start, even when its fare is higher.

The opposite case matters too. If the cheaper app has a long wait, poor wheelchair access, weak safety support, or a history of cancellation, the higher quote may buy a real service difference. A fair test must therefore compare the whole trip, not just the number shown before booking.

```text
need to travel now
  -> apps show fare, wait, and service signals
  -> rider weighs money against time and uncertainty
  -> default choice is made
  -> repeated choices shape platform power and household cost
```

## What the source actually establishes

| Finding | What it means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute price gap of about $3.50, around 14% of the average fare. | The same-looking trip can produce materially different quotes. | Every rider could have saved that amount. |
| Only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other. | Live comparison is not common. | We do not know whether people lacked time, trusted one app, or had another reason. |
| Neither app was always cheaper. | There is no simple rule that solves search. | The study does not measure the better pickup, driver, or safety outcome. |

Source: [NBER Working Paper 34441](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34441).

## The deeper finding

The market has two prices: the posted fare and the price of finding a better fare. The second price is paid in attention and risk. It falls for people with time, multiple accounts, good phone access, and flexible plans. It rises for people who are late, caring for someone, unfamiliar with the area, or unable to tolerate a failed pickup.

This is why convenience can become a form of market power. A platform does not need to win every comparison. It can win by making comparison feel impractical at the exact moment the customer needs the service.

## What remains unknown

The study does not show why each rider chose an app, whether the chosen trip was safer or faster, or how fare gaps affect budgets over time. It covers one city and a limited period. It also does not test whether a comparison tool would save money without increasing cancellation, wait, or privacy costs.

## Next test

Track matched trips for six months. Record both quotes, pickup time, wait, cancellation, completed-trip time, tip, service quality, trip purpose, income, and whether the rider checked both apps. Report two outcomes separately: dollars saved and obligations protected, such as arriving at work or collecting a child on time.

## Reading rule

A fare gap is evidence of a possible saving, not proof of customer harm. Show what the rider paid for besides the fare.
