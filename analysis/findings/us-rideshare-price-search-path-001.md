# When app convenience leaves money on the trip

## The argument

Two apps can show different prices for the same ride while most riders check only one. That does not automatically mean riders are careless. Checking takes time, attention, battery, data, and a willingness to risk losing the ride while comparing. A rider may also trust one app, hold a discount there, prefer its safety tools, or expect a shorter wait.

The fare gap matters only beside the effort of comparison and the value of arriving on time. Someone carrying a child, starting a shift, or traveling somewhere unfamiliar may rationally pay more to reduce uncertainty. Another rider with time and little cash may check both apps and wait. The same price difference can therefore be a lost saving for one rider and a purchased service feature for another.

The deeper finding is that the market does not offer “the price” in isolation. It offers a bundle of fare, wait, reliability, safety, account history, and comparison effort. A platform can gain power when it becomes the default place to look, even if a competitor is sometimes cheaper. Customer power depends on whether the rider can see and act on the alternative before the trip is gone.

## Follow one ride

A worker finishes late and needs to reach a child-care pickup before closing. Uber shows $28 and Lyft shows $23. Opening the second app takes a minute, but the first app warns that the fare may change and the driver may cancel. The worker chooses the first ride. The $5 difference is not enough to show a bad decision; it is evidence of a trade between money, time, and uncertainty.

If this happens twice a week, however, the small choice becomes a household cost. If the cheaper app often waits longer or cancels, switching may not help. If the rider never sees the cheaper option, the platform has less reason to keep the fare close. Repeated default choices can shape competition even when each single choice looks reasonable.

```text
two platforms show different prices → checking takes effort and attention
→ rider chooses an app → fare, wait, reliability, and safety are exchanged
→ money and time remain → repeated defaults shape platform competition
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Price | An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute gap of about $3.50, roughly 14% of the average fare. | The same-looking trip can carry different displayed prices. |
| Search | Device data show only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other. | Many riders do not make a live comparison. |
| Direction | Neither app was consistently cheaper. | A rider cannot rely on a simple “always choose X” rule. |
| Value | The study does not measure each rider’s income, trip purpose, wait, service quality, or reason for choosing an app. | A price gap is not yet a complete measure of customer loss. |

Source: [NBER Working Paper 34441](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34441).

## The deeper finding

Convenience can be a market advantage because it removes the moment when a customer might compare. The platform does not need to be cheapest on every trip; it may only need to be familiar, trusted, and quick enough that checking feels too costly. That makes attention part of the price system.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show why each rider chose an app, whether the chosen ride was better, or how repeated fare gaps affect household budgets. It covers one city and a short price window. It does not show whether comparison tools, fare disclosure, or automatic switching would improve outcomes without making rides slower or less reliable.

## Next test

Follow riders and matched trips for six months. Join both app prices, comparison time, wait, cancellations, reliability, trip purpose, income, safety, total cost, and switching. Measure not only the fare saved, but also missed work, late care, and time recovered.

## Reading rule

A price gap is not automatically a customer loss. Show what the rider paid for besides the fare.
