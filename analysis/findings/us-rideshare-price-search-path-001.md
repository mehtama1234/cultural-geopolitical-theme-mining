# When app convenience leaves money on the trip

## Short answer

Two apps can show different prices for the same ride while most riders check only one. The reason may be habit, time, trust, discounts, or a better service—not only carelessness.

The fare gap matters only alongside comparison effort, wait, reliability, and the choices a rider could actually make.

## What the source shows

An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute price gap of about $3.50, or roughly 14% of the average fare. Device data show that only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other, even though neither app was consistently cheaper.

The study covers one city and a short price window. It does not show each rider's income, reason for choosing an app, service quality, final cost, or later switching.

[NBER Working Paper 34441](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34441)

## The connection

```text
two platforms show different prices
  -> effort and habit of checking
  -> app chosen
  -> fare paid and money left
  -> customer power and platform competition
```

People may value wait time, reliability, discounts, account history, or habit more than the lowest displayed fare. A higher price can buy a better service or save time.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A rider may need to arrive on time, carry a child, reach work, or feel safe. The cheapest fare is not always the lowest total cost.

**Company and sector:** Platforms benefit when an app becomes the default and comparison takes effort. Prices, rewards, maps, wait estimates, and account history shape the choice.

**Money and finance:** The ride costs the fare, tip, time, and any missed work or care. Repeated small gaps can matter to a low-cash household.

**Public power:** Data access, platform design, fee disclosure, transport alternatives, and competition rules decide whether riders can compare and switch.

## What would change the finding

- Rider income, time value, trip purpose, disability, safety, and alternatives.
- Posted fare, final fare, tip, discount, wait, cancellation, reliability, and quality.
- App opening, comparison time, booking, repeat use, and switching.
- Platform rules that limit comparison or make one app the default.
- Results outside New York City and over a longer period.

## Next test

Follow riders and matched trips for six months. Join both app prices, comparison, wait, cancellations, quality, trip purpose, income, total cost, and switching. Measure what money and time remain after repeated rides.

## Reading rule

Keep posted price, comparison effort, service quality, chosen app, and total customer value separate. A price gap is not automatically a customer loss.
