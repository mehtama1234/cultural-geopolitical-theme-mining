# The vote may respond more to lost buying power than to inflation alone

## The argument

“Inflation hurt the party in power” is too blunt. A local price rise and a loss of buying power are not the same thing.

Prices can rise in two counties at the same rate. In one, pay rises faster. In the other, pay falls behind while rent, food, transport, and debt take more of the budget. The second household has less left after ordinary life, even though the headline inflation rate looks similar.

That is the political route worth testing. The voter may not respond to the rate itself. The voter may respond to what remains after local prices meet local pay. But a county vote is not a household diary, and a household's economic pain is not automatically the reason it voted.

## Follow the local pay gap

Imagine a county where prices rise six percent. A worker receives a seven percent raise and has slightly more room. In another county, pay rises two percent while rent and food rise six percent. The second worker cuts purchases, uses savings, borrows, or gives up care.

If the second county shifts toward one party, several explanations are possible: lower real pay, party identity, local industry, news, immigration, social issues, or anger about government. The price number alone cannot choose among them.

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Local prices | NBER separates local price changes from the rest of the economic picture. | Place matters. |
| Real pay | A county study finds falling real wages predict Republican gains more clearly than higher inflation alone for 2021–2024. | Buying power may matter more than the rate. |
| Turnout | NBER research links wages and participation in elections. | Work and pay can relate to who votes. |
| Blame | Research on the 2022 elections links reported personal and community inflation burdens with lower Democratic support. | People can connect price pressure to political judgment. |
| Limits | County vote share and turnout do not identify individual reasons. | The personal path remains open. |

Sources: [NBER county study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301), [NBER wages and turnout study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17270), [inflation blame in the 2022 elections](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9ACD009459C2C11C02BB89DA68B6B929/S0007123424000930a.pdf/inflation-blame-attribution-and-the-2022-us-congressional-elections.pdf), and [HBS on elections and economic prospects](https://www2.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/what-elections-can-reveal-about-countrys-economic-prospects).

## The important split

Keep five measures apart: local price growth, nominal pay, real pay, vote share, and turnout. Each answers a different question.

Real pay is closer to household room than inflation alone, but it still does not show rent, debt, family help, savings, or the products actually bought. Vote share shows a place's result, not which voters changed their minds. Turnout shows participation, not satisfaction. A person can feel poorer and vote on another issue—or not vote at all.

That is why the strongest claim is narrow: the recent county evidence makes lost buying power a better candidate for political study than inflation by itself. It does not prove a single cause of a vote.

## What remains unproven

The next test is individual or household data joining local prices, pay, rent, debt, purchases, repeated economic views, party identity, policy knowledge, blame, and turnout. It should show whether a household's room actually fell and what political choice followed.

## Reading rule

Say “real pay fell,” “local prices rose,” or “the county vote changed.” Do not collapse all three into “voters punished inflation.”
