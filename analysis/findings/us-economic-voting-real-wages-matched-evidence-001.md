# The vote may respond more to lost buying power than to inflation alone

## Short answer

“Inflation hurt the party in power” is too blunt. A new NBER county study separates the rise in local prices from whether local pay kept up. For 2021–2024, it finds that falling real wages—not higher inflation by itself—predict Republican gains more clearly. Inflation still has a separate association with presidential vote share. That points to two different experiences: the cost went up, and the household had less left after paying it.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Measure | What it captures | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | How quickly a local basket became more expensive. | That every household bought that basket. |
| Nominal pay | Dollars earned before removing price changes. | Whether those dollars bought more. |
| Real pay | Pay after accounting for local budget costs. | Why a person voted. |
| Vote share | The county's result for a candidate or party. | Which voters changed their minds. |
| Turnout | Who participated. | Whether nonvoters were satisfied, angry, busy, or unable to vote. |

Sources: [NBER county study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301), [NBER wages and turnout study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17270), [study of inflation blame in the 2022 elections](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9ACD009459C2C11C02BB89DA68B6B929/S0007123424000930a.pdf/inflation-blame-attribution-and-the-2022-us-congressional-elections.pdf), and [HBS on elections and economic prospects](https://www2.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/what-elections-can-reveal-about-countrys-economic-prospects).

## The connection

```text
local price and pay gap → less room after ordinary bills
                        → personal burden or national worry
                        → blame, identity, or hope for change
                        → vote choice or turnout
```

The county evidence supports the first and last observations together, but not the full personal story. A county can move toward one party while many people inside it do not experience the same loss. A person can feel poorer but vote mainly on another issue.

## Why the distinction matters

The same 6% price rise can mean different things in two places. In one place, pay rises faster. In another, pay falls behind, rent takes more room, and the household gives up more choices. If the political response follows the second condition, the useful measure is not inflation alone. It is what remains after local costs meet local pay.

The next distinction is blame. A household may know prices rose but not know which policy, firm, or outside event mattered. The 2022 election research connects reported personal and community inflation burdens with lower Democratic support, but that does not establish that a particular price caused a vote. The result could include party identity, news, local conditions, and a general view of the economy.

## Four views

**Household:** Start with pay after rent, food, transport, health, and debt—not a national average.

**Customer:** Record what was delayed, dropped, replaced, or bought on credit.

**Money and finance:** Separate price growth, real pay, debt service, savings, and local housing costs.

**Public power:** Test knowledge and blame before saying hardship changed a vote.

## What would change the finding

- Individual or household data joining local prices, pay, rent, debt, and purchases.
- Survey answers collected before and after major price or wage changes.
- A measure of whether respondents knew the relevant policy and assigned blame to it.
- Results split by income, race, age, party identity, county type, and election level.
- A turnout test that does not treat a vote shift and a participation shift as the same thing.

## Reading rule

Say “real pay fell,” “local prices rose,” or “the county vote changed.” Do not collapse all three into “voters punished inflation.”
