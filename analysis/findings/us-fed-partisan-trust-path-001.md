# When the price message depends on who says it

## The argument

An economic message does not enter an empty room. People hear it while comparing the message with rent, food, wages, debt, and their view of the institution speaking. If the Federal Reserve appears aligned with one party, the same message may receive more attention from that party's supporters and more doubt from the other side.

That matters because a message can change an inflation expectation before it changes a budget. A person may distrust the speaker and still follow the rate. Another may trust the speaker and still be unable to change spending because the bill is due. A third may update an expectation and vote for a different reason.

## Follow one message through a household

A family sees its credit-card rate rise and hears that tighter policy will bring prices down. If it trusts the Fed, it may delay a purchase. If it sees the Fed as partisan, it may reject the explanation. But if rent and medicine are due, neither reaction changes the next payment. The message can alter blame without changing the budget.

```text
price experience and political identity → perceived institutional alignment → trust and message receipt
→ inflation belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Trust and receipt | In a survey experiment with 5,205 US consumers, NBER finds people who saw the Fed as aligned with their party reported more trust and were more likely to receive or pay for Fed communications. | Perceived alignment affects whether the message gets through. |
| Belief | Those respondents gave the messages more weight when updating inflation expectations. | Trust can change how information is used. |
| Partisan pattern | Another NBER study finds trust in the Fed is often higher among people of the same party as the president. | Institutional trust can move with political identity. |
| Separation | Trust and inflation beliefs do not always move together. | One trust score cannot stand in for later outcomes. |
| Limits | The studies measure reported trust, communication, and beliefs, not actual household spending, borrowing, or voting. | Message credibility is not proven behavior. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 33071](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33071) and [NBER Working Paper 33684](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33684).

## The deeper finding

Public communication is part of economic policy because people must decide whether a signal deserves weight. But credibility is built against a household's own bills, party identity, media path, and ability to respond. A trusted message can still be unusable when the family has no short-term choice.

## What remains unproven

The studies do not show actual spending, saving, borrowing, credit balances, or lasting communication effects. They do not establish that a partisan trust gap changes a vote or that a different message changes a household's inflation.

## Next test

Follow households from a real Fed communication for six months. Join exposure, source, party cues, belief, prices, income, spending, saving, borrowing, credit, turnout, and policy views. Record whether the household had room to act.

## Reading rule

Keep alignment, trust, message receipt, belief, behavior, and political judgment separate.
