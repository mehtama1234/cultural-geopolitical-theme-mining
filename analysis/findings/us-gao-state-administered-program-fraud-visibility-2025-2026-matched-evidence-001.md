# Federal aid can become harder to see as it passes through more hands

## The deeper finding

GAO reviewed 20 large programs in which federal money is administered by
states or other nonfederal entities. Together they represented about $1.1
trillion in FY2025 obligations and nearly 90% of obligations among the 203
qualifying programs. The money can pass through states to local governments,
Tribes, subrecipients, contractors, and subcontractors.

That arrangement can make services more locally responsive. It also creates
more handoffs where eligibility, payment, records, and oversight can become
less visible to the federal agency. Only 5 of the 20 selected programs had
documented evidence consistent with identifying and assessing fraud risks to
prioritize action; 15 did not. GAO listed 22 open recommendations.

```text
federal money
  -> state or other administrator
  -> local/subrecipient/contractor delivery
  -> benefit, grant, or project
  -> [open] risk detection, correction, recovery, service continuity, trust
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a public-system version of the atlas's capacity-versus-control
tension. Decentralization can increase reach and fit local needs, but it can
also move the point of practical control away from the institution that is
ultimately accountable for the money. A program may therefore be formally
funded and locally delivered while federal visibility into risk, contractor
capacity, or the final recipient remains incomplete.

The cultural and political meaning is not simply “fraud is high.” It is that
people encounter government through a chain of institutions. When something
goes wrong, the person may not know which level can correct it, while each
level may hold only part of the record. That makes public trust depend on the
visibility and repair of the delivery chain, not only on the size of the
appropriation.

## What the report does not establish

- The 20 programs were selected for size, not randomly.
- The $1.1 trillion is an obligation total, not household receipt or usable
  benefit value.
- The government-wide $233–$521 billion annual fraud estimate is not a loss
  estimate for these 20 programs.
- Missing documented risk assessments do not prove realized fraud.
- Open recommendations do not prove that a particular beneficiary lost a
  benefit or that a service failed.
- The report does not follow a person or household through exposure, report,
  correction, recovery, service continuity, or later trust.

## Next test

Follow one named program and one protected recipient or award through federal
funding, state allocation, subrecipient or contractor delivery, eligibility or
payment, anomaly detection, complaint, investigation, correction, recovery,
and later service use. Publish the handoff points and the records each level
can see. Compare whether more local discretion improves delivery without
creating an unrepairable visibility gap.

## Reading rule

Do not treat a large federal obligation or a state delivery contract as proof
that the intended service reached people safely. Ask who handled the money,
which records crossed each handoff, who could correct an error, and whether the
recipient's service continued.
