# When the news selects the economic mood

## Short answer

The prices people hear about can shape what they expect next. High-price news may raise inflation expectations more than lower-price news lowers them, because bad price news is more likely to be noticed and reported.

An expectation can change attention or a plan without changing what a household actually buys or how it votes.

## What the sources show

A household-panel NBER study finds that exposure to higher-price news raises inflation expectations by 0.4 percentage point, while lower-price news has no significant effect. Another NBER study finds that daily US television coverage raises expectations, with stronger responses to positive CPI surprises and effects that fade after about ten days.

NBER research on inflation beliefs provides a separate test of how party and news source shape perceived causes and policy preferences.

The studies measure news exposure and expectations. They do not show each household's full media diet, price basket, spending, or political response.

- [NBER Working Paper 33837](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33837)
- [NBER Working Paper 34088](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34088)
- [NBER Working Paper 32497](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32497)

## The connection

```text
price movement
  -> selected news and repetition
  -> inflation expectation and cause belief
  -> spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment
```

Bad price news can be useful because it reports real harm. Lower-price news may be less common or less relevant to a household still facing a high price level.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A family weighs a headline against rent, food, gas, wages, and debt. Repeated news may make a future bill feel urgent before it arrives.

**Company and sector:** Media, retailers, lenders, employers, and firms choose what price change to show and explain. Their messages can change shopping and borrowing plans.

**Money and finance:** Expectations can affect saving, borrowing, and a large purchase, but the actual result depends on income, rates, prices, and credit.

**Public power:** Data release, central-bank communication, media rules, and public information shape which price story reaches people and how long it stays with them.

## What would change the finding

- Household price basket, income, debt, and local prices.
- Complete media exposure, source, trust, repetition, and timing.
- Price news, inflation expectation, cause belief, and policy preference.
- Spending, saving, borrowing, turnout, voting, and public demand.
- Differences by party, income, age, race, place, and education.

## Next test

Follow households from a measured price story through three months. Join media exposure, personal prices, wages, expectations, trust, spending, saving, borrowing, and political action.

## Reading rule

Keep news exposure, expectation, cause belief, household action, and political action separate. A headline can change what people expect without changing what they do.
