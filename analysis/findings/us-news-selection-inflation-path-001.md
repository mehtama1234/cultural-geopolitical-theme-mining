# When the news selects the economic mood

## The argument

The price stories people hear can shape what they expect next. A headline about a sharp increase may raise inflation expectations more than a report about a decrease lowers them, because bad price news is more likely to be noticed, repeated, and treated as a warning. That expectation can change a shopping plan, a saving decision, or a judgment about who is responsible before the household's own bill arrives.

But a news effect is not the same as a spending effect. A family may hear that inflation is easing and still face a rent renewal, a grocery price that never came back down, or a high credit-card rate. The story and the lived basket can move in different directions.

## Follow one price story through a household

Suppose a household hears repeated reports of a higher Consumer Price Index. The family starts looking for a deal, delays a large purchase, or borrows sooner because it expects prices to rise again. Ten days later the effect of the headline may fade, but the rent, food, and fuel prices remain. Another household hears the same report through a trusted source and blames a different institution for it.

The news can change the frame around a real cost. It cannot tell us, by itself, what the household bought or who it blamed.

```text
price movement → selected news and repetition → inflation expectation and cause belief
→ spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Household news | An NBER household-panel study finds exposure to higher-price news raises inflation expectations by 0.4 percentage point, while lower-price news has no significant effect. | Bad price news can move expectations more than good price news in the study. |
| Broadcast news | Another NBER study finds daily US television coverage raises expectations, with stronger responses to positive CPI surprises and effects that fade after about ten days. | Repetition and timing can shape a short-term economic frame. |
| Cause belief | NBER research on inflation beliefs provides a separate test of how party and news source shape perceived causes and policy preferences. | People can disagree about both the price path and who caused it. |
| Household reality | Rent, food, gas, wages, debt, and local prices determine what a family actually faces. | A national headline may fit one basket and miss another. |
| Limits | The studies do not show each household's full media diet, price basket, spending, or political response in one record. | Expectation evidence does not prove changed behavior or voting. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 33837](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33837), [NBER Working Paper 34088](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34088), and [NBER Working Paper 32497](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32497).

## The deeper finding

Inflation is lived twice: first as a price in the household's basket, then as a public story about what that price means. The second layer matters because it can change when people buy, how much cash they hold, and which institution they blame. Yet a calmer headline does not undo a higher price level. Political judgment may therefore track accumulated loss even when the rate of increase has slowed.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether higher expectations caused more spending, borrowing, or political action for the same families. It does not fully separate media exposure from party identity, trust, local prices, or personal financial stress. Nor does it show whether better information makes people more accurate or merely changes which explanation they prefer.

## Next test

Follow households from a measured price story through three months. Join complete media exposure, personal prices, wages, debt, expectations, trust, spending, saving, borrowing, and political action. Compare the national story with the household's own basket and ask which one predicts the next choice.

## Reading rule

Keep news exposure, expectation, cause belief, household action, and political action separate. A headline can change what people expect without changing what they do.
