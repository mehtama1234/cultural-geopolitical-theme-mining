# An energy bill can take food, medicine, or safe temperature first

## Short answer

Energy burden is not only the share of income spent on power and fuel. The 2024 EIA survey records a more concrete cost: 32.89 million US primary homes reported reducing or forgoing food or medicine to pay energy costs. It also records disconnect or delivery-stop notices, unhealthy home temperatures, and homes unable to use heating or air conditioning. The deeper pattern is a contest over control: the household may pay the bill, while the renter or low-income owner cannot quickly fix the building that creates it.

A ratio can miss the moment of harm. A household may have a modest annual burden but face a winter bill before payday, a deposit after moving, or a repair during a medical crisis. Another may spend a large share on energy because it chooses a warmer home and can afford it. The percentage identifies pressure; it does not say whether the home was safe or which other need lost the contest.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Layer | What the record shows | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Burden | DOE reports about 6% average energy burden for low-income households and about 2% for others. | The actual bill and income path for one household. |
| Sacrifice | EIA reports 32.89 million homes reducing or forgoing food or medicine for energy costs. | Which need was cut first and for how long. |

## The connection

```text
home condition + energy price → bill pressure
                              → food, medicine, rent, debt, or comfort tradeoff
                              → notice, shutoff, aid, or weatherization
                              → health, work, housing, and trust
```

The EIA table measures the pressure and some coping actions. DOE and GAO show why the housing and payment rules matter. The final health, work, and trust links still need a joined household record.

## The control problem

An owner may be able to replace equipment or improve insulation, though not always able to afford it. A renter may pay the monthly energy bill but have no right or money to change windows, insulation, or the heating system. DOE calls this a split between who pays and who can make the improvement. A bill-help program addresses the immediate payment. Weatherization addresses the building. They are not the same intervention, and they should not be credited with the same outcome.

The same distinction appears in assisted housing. GAO found that the utility allowance used by a housing agency can leave actual utility costs outside the rent calculation. A home can therefore be called affordable under one allowance while the household is still rent-burdened after paying the real bill.

The coping choice is often hidden. A family may lower the thermostat, cool only one room, use an unsafe heater, borrow from a relative, or put the bill on a card. A shutoff notice is visible in utility records, but the avoided shutoff may have been paid for with food, medicine, sleep, or debt. Service remaining on is not proof that the household was secure.

The burden can also move between people. A tenant pays the bill while a landlord controls the windows and heating equipment. An adult child may pay a parent's utility account. A family may move to a cheaper home with higher energy use or remain in a costly home to stay near care and work. The account holder is not always the person who controls the cause or bears the full social cost.

The same dollar may protect several systems at once. Paying the utility can keep a refrigerator cold, a medical device running, a child able to sleep, or a worker able to stay in the home. Cutting use may reduce the bill while reducing those services. Energy burden should therefore be measured as the cost of maintaining a safe, usable home, not simply the cost of kilowatt-hours or fuel.

## Four views

**Household:** Record what was cut—food, medicine, temperature, rent, debt, or work travel.

**Customer:** Separate a bill, a notice, a shutoff, a payment plan, and restored service.

**Money and finance:** Keep burden percentage, arrears, assistance, and building investment separate.

**Public power:** Ask who sets the rate, who controls the building, who qualifies for help, and who bears the unpaid balance.

**Health and care:** Record temperature, equipment, sleep, medicine storage, respiratory or heat symptoms, care visits, and the time spent managing the bill.

## What would change the finding

- Utility records joined to household income, rent, housing quality, and assistance.
- Dates for notice, payment plan, shutoff, restoration, and arrears repayment.
- Health, school, work, and housing outcomes after an energy insecurity event.
- Renter-owner comparisons that include who pays and who can improve the building.
- A test of whether weatherization lowers later sacrifice, not only energy use.
- Evidence on whether flexible billing, faster aid, repair, weatherization, or housing support changes the specific sacrifice made by the household.

## Reading rule

Do not call a lower energy bill a full success until we know whether the household kept food, medicine, safe temperature, housing, and work access.

Follow the same household for two heating and cooling seasons. Join bills, pay and benefit dates, rates, arrears, notices, shutoffs, equipment, home condition, landlord action, temperature, food and medicine choices, debt, work, care, and moves. Compare bill aid, repair, weatherization, allowance changes, and flexible due dates by the first cause of the crisis. Measure months of safe service, sacrificed essentials, debt, health, missed work, and cash left after energy—not only the burden ratio.
