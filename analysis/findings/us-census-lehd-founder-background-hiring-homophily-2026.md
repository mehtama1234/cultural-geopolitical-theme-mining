# Finding: New firms can reproduce their founder's social background in hiring

**Status:** bounded firm-culture, labor-opportunity, and unequal-access finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census study asks whether the people who start new firms shape who gets
hired into them. In the main LEHD analysis, founder and employee backgrounds
were compared after accounting for industry, metro area, and founding cohort.

The differences were large. Female-founded firms had about a 36 percentage-point
higher female employee share. The corresponding differences were about 51
points for Black workers, 37 points for Hispanic workers, and 11 points for
workers without a college degree. The pattern remained visible as firms aged
and persisted to at least 20 years in the study's longer-run analysis.

The wage analysis points more toward firm demand than worker supply: workers
who shared a founder's background received higher relative firm wage effects,
ranging from about 2.9% to 12.9% across the demographic dimensions studied.

## What this adds to the broader theme atlas

This suggests a firm-level opportunity pathway:

```text
unequal access to entrepreneurship
  -> founder background and firm formation
  -> hiring demand, networks, and early organizational culture
  -> who enters, stays, and receives relative wages
  -> career mobility, household resources, and social representation [open]
```

The broader cultural point is that a firm's “culture” may become a labor-market
allocation mechanism. New firms can create jobs while also concentrating access
among people socially similar to the founder. That is not automatically bad:
shared background may improve communication, trust, or task fit. But it means
aggregate job creation does not tell us who received the opportunity.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The primary LEHD sample covers 30 states and D.C., not the entire country.
- The balanced sample requires firms to have non-founder employees observed for
  years 0-5, which selects against some small, young, or short-lived firms.
- LEHD founder identity is proxied using the top three earners in a firm's first
  year; that is not a direct founder survey.
- The LinkedIn/Crunchbase comparison has different coverage and selection,
  including self-selection into LinkedIn and a college-educated, venture-backed
  tilt.
- The results are not a direct measure of discrimination, worker preference,
  workplace quality, or worker harm.
- The wage decomposition favors a labor-demand explanation but does not prove
  one mechanism for every demographic group.

The next decisive artifact would follow a protected founder-firm-worker panel
through founder access, recruiting channel, applicant pool, hiring, pay,
promotion, retention, worker voice, firm survival, and later household or
community outcomes.
