# When earning more leaves a family with less room

## The argument

A raise can increase wages and still leave a household with less usable support. Food aid, health coverage, child care, transport help, taxes, debt, and the time spent proving eligibility all sit beside the paycheck. If several supports fall at once, the next dollar of work may buy less than expected.

The shape of the rule matters. A gradual phase-out can leave a family better off while making each new dollar worth less. A hard cutoff can create a sudden loss. A work rule can reduce program participation without increasing employment. Calling all of these a “benefit cliff” hides the choice the policy creates and the cost the family must carry.

## Follow the next dollar

A parent is offered extra hours. The paycheck rises, but the hours require paid child care and a longer commute. Food help falls at the same time. The parent may be better off, worse off, or unable to tell until the next monthly notice. If a report is late, the benefit may stop even though earnings did not change.

```text
extra work → more wages → taxes, care, travel, and benefit change → net household resources rise, fall, or become uncertain
→ work, food, debt, health, and trust change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Work rule | NBER studies find some SNAP work requirements reduced participation without increasing work. | Losing aid does not prove more employment. |
| Program shape | HHS distinguishes phase-outs from cutoffs and shows how several programs can create high effective rates as earnings rise. | Rule design changes the payoff from the next dollar. |
| Care | HBS research shows caregiver support affects whether workers can remain and perform at work. | Work choice depends on care, not pay alone. |
| Total resources | Wages can arrive with lost food, coverage, care, or transport value. | Cash income is not the full household result. |
| Limits | The studies support separate links between rules, care, and resources but do not show one family's complete monthly budget. | We cannot call an exit progress or a raise a gain without the full account. |

Sources: [NBER SNAP work requirements](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32441), [NBER earlier SNAP study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28877), [HHS benefit-cliff analysis](https://aspe.hhs.gov/topics/poverty-economic-mobility/marginal-tax-rate-series), and [HBS caregiver-support research](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-to-support-your-best-employees-who-are-probably-caregivers).

## The deeper finding

The right unit is usable room after the change. That includes cash, food, health coverage, care, transport, taxes, debt, and the time needed to keep the benefit. A family can work more and have less room, or work less because the rule makes extra hours too costly. The public system may call this movement toward independence while the household experiences a tighter month.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether an exit made a particular family safer, poorer, or more independent. It does not show which support fell first, whether the family understood the change, or whether the new work was stable enough to replace the lost help.

## Next test

Build a monthly record of wages, taxes, benefits, child care, health coverage, transport, work hours, food, debt, savings, notices, and the stated reason for exit. Compare gradual phase-outs, hard cutoffs, and work rules using the same household outcomes.

## Reading rule

Do not call a program exit progress or a wage increase a gain until you count the help lost and the new costs required to earn it.
