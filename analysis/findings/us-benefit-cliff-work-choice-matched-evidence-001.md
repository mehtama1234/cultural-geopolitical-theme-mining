# When earning more leaves a family with less room

## The argument

A raise can increase wages while reducing food, health, child-care, transport, or cash help. The family may still be better off, but the gain can be smaller than the wage number suggests. Losing one program is not proof that total household resources fell.

The shape of the rule matters. A gradual phase-out can leave a family better off while making each new dollar worth less. A hard cutoff can create a sudden loss. A work rule can reduce program participation without increasing employment. Calling all of these a “benefit cliff” hides the choice and cost the family must carry.

The deeper finding is that a benefit rule changes the price of moving forward. It can reward work, make work possible, or make the next step too fragile. The wage is only one input into the decision.

The loss can be hard to see because programs recalculate on different schedules. A paycheck may rise today, food help may fall next month, Medicaid may change later, and child-care costs may begin when the new shift starts. The family has to make a decision before the full budget is known. A rule that is smooth in an annual model can feel like a sudden cliff when notices and bills arrive at different times.

## Follow the next dollar

A parent is offered extra hours. The paycheck rises, but the hours require paid child care and a longer commute. Food help falls at the same time. The parent may be better off, worse off, or unable to tell until the next notice. If the report is late, the benefit may stop even though earnings did not change.

Another parent in a smooth-taper system keeps part of the support and can plan the next step. Both families earn more, but their usable room and confidence differ.

The parent may also turn down work that looks profitable on paper. An extra shift can require a babysitter, a second car trip, a uniform, a late-night ride, or a missed appointment. If the job ends after a few weeks, the family may lose the benefit before the wages become reliable. A refusal can therefore reflect risk management, not a lack of interest in work.

The household's best choice may be to accept the work and absorb a temporary loss. That can be worthwhile if the job brings stable hours, health coverage, training, or a path to higher pay. It can be harmful if the work is unstable and the family loses support immediately. The result depends on job quality and duration, not only on whether employment began.

```text
extra work
  -> more wages
  -> taxes, care, travel, and benefit change
  -> net household resources rise, fall, or become uncertain
  -> work, food, debt, health, and trust change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NBER studies find SNAP work requirements reduced participation among parents without increasing work in the studied settings. | Removing support did not automatically create employment. | The settings do not represent every program or state. |
| HHS models how SNAP, tax credits, Medicaid, child care, and TANF interact as earnings rise. | Several programs can combine into a high effective rate for some families. | A model does not describe every family’s full budget. |
| HBS describes how care and transport needs make the same job benefit useful to one worker and less useful to another. | Wage value depends on necessary costs and family duties. | It does not measure every benefit interaction. |
| HHS distinguishes phase-outs from cutoffs. | Rule design changes the payoff from the next dollar. | It does not establish which design gives the best long-term result. |

Sources: [NBER 32441](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32441), [NBER 28877](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28877), [HHS effective marginal tax rates](https://aspe.hhs.gov/topics/poverty-economic-mobility/marginal-tax-rate-series), and [HBS caregiver support](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-to-support-your-best-employees-who-are-probably-caregivers).

This is a coordination problem across programs. Each agency can apply its own rule correctly while the family faces one combined budget. A gradual SNAP taper may be offset by a health-coverage cutoff; a higher wage may be consumed by child care; a tax credit may arrive months after the cost of working. The household experiences the combined schedule, not the separate program manuals.

The political question is what kind of movement the system wants. A sharp cutoff may reduce program spending but make a job offer risky. A smooth taper may support work but cost more and require better records. A work rule may be presented as a path to independence while removing the food or health buffer that makes stable work possible. The answer should be judged by stable work, health, food, housing, and cash—not just participation or exit.

The deeper link is between income risk and benefit risk. A worker with steady hours can plan around a taper. A worker with changing shifts may cross the threshold one month and fall below it the next. If the support does not return quickly, the family carries the downside alone. A program can therefore punish volatility even when it intends to reward earnings.

## The deeper finding

The right unit is usable room after the change. That includes cash, food, health coverage, care, transport, taxes, debt, and the time needed to keep the benefit. A family can work more and have less room, or work less because extra hours make its support too fragile.

This is also an information problem. A family cannot plan around a wage increase if it cannot predict which support will fall, when it will fall, and whether the new job will last. A policy that looks generous on paper may feel risky at the kitchen table.

## What remains unknown

The evidence does not show whether an exit made a particular family safer, poorer, or more independent. It does not show which support fell first, whether the family understood the change, or whether the new work was stable enough to replace the lost help.

It also does not show the full cost of taking work, the delay before benefits restart, the effect on children or care, or whether households use debt and family help to cross the gap. Employment and participation records cannot tell us whether the household had more usable room.

## Next test

Follow families before and after an earnings change for one year. Join wages, benefits, taxes, necessary costs, work hours, job stability, debt, food security, housing, and the work opportunity accepted or refused. Compare a smooth taper with a sudden loss.

Time-stamp every wage, notice, benefit change, child-care bill, commute, tax payment, and job separation. Show the combined budget before the work decision and after each program changes. Compare sudden cutoff, smooth taper, temporary bridge, and automatic restoration when earnings fall. Measure net cash, food and health coverage, care stability, work duration, missed work, debt, housing, and whether the family could take the next job without risking the basics.

## Reading rule

Do not call a program exit progress or a wage increase a gain until you count the help lost and the new costs required to earn it.
