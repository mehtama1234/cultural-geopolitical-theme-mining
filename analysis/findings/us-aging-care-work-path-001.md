# When family care becomes a work decision

## Short answer

Unpaid eldercare is a large use of time that often sits beside paid work. The hidden cost is not simply “lost work.” It is the set of work hours, job choices, rest and family duties that must be arranged around care.

The careful finding is that care and work are joined in the same household problem. Current time-use data show the scale and the overlap. They do not prove that care caused a person to leave a job or lose earnings.

## What the sources show

### 1. Unpaid eldercare is common

BLS reports that 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24. That was 14% of the civilian noninstitutional population age 15 and over. Fifty-two percent of providers gave care at least several times a week, and one-quarter gave care daily.

Care can mean transport, meals, medical help, housework, paperwork, companionship or being available. A short diary entry can therefore sit on top of a longer duty to stay reachable.

[BLS unpaid eldercare release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/elcare.htm)

### 2. Care also meets paid work and childcare

Among eldercare providers who were parents of children living at home, 86% were employed and 72% worked full time. There were 7.6 million providers in this group. Their care was not an alternative to work; it was another demand on the same schedule.

This is the point where eldercare and childcare should be read together, while keeping their costs separate. The same adult may be paying for care, giving care and protecting paid work at once.

[BLS eldercare and parent findings](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/elcare.htm)

### 3. Care timing differs by employment status

BLS reports that employed eldercare providers gave care on 21% of days, compared with 38% of days for providers who were not employed. On days they gave care, employed providers spent 2.8 hours in care and providers who were not employed spent 4.8 hours.

These figures describe who provides care and when. They cannot tell us whether care changed employment, or whether employment status changed who could provide care.

### 4. Earnings effects need a stronger design

An NBER study links reported family caregiving to administrative earnings records over a long period and examines different labor effects by gender. It is the right kind of evidence for the next step because it connects care reports to later work records. Its timing and design still need close review before a causal claim.

[NBER: Caregiving and Labor Supply](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31450)

## The connection

```text
older person's need
  -> family time, paid care, leave, schedule change, or work exit
  -> earnings, rest, savings, housing, or childcare choice
  -> employer, family, and public response
```

The first link is measured. The next links are a mechanism to test, not a settled result.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** Millions of people provide unpaid eldercare. Many provide it repeatedly. A large share of providers who also have children are employed.

**Inference:** The care system may be shifting risk onto family schedules before the cost appears as a formal bill. Work flexibility can help, but it may also hide who is carrying the duty.

**Unknown:** We do not know which workers reduced hours, changed jobs, lost earnings, used savings or bought paid care because of eldercare. We also do not know who could not make those choices.

## What would change the finding

- A panel following care start, care intensity, work hours, earnings and job changes for the same people.
- Results by gender, race, income, disability, household type and place.
- Employer records on leave, schedule control and promotion after care begins.
- The price and availability of paid care in the same local areas.
- Evidence that substantial unpaid care has little effect on work or household security.

## Next test

Read the NBER study closely, then use BLS ATUS microdata to compare care timing with employment and work hours. Add the childcare packet only where the same household schedule is visible. Keep care time, paid-care spending and lost earnings as separate outcomes.

## Reading rule

Unpaid care is not automatically lost work. Employment is not proof that care is easy. Count the hours, the money and the choices together.
