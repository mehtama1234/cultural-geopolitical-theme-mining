# Safety oversight can stretch as the system it covers expands

## The deeper finding

FAA’s National Simulator Program oversees flight simulators used by airlines and flight schools to train pilots. From 1990 to 2025, the number of simulators increased by more than 500%, while program staffing stayed roughly steady. Evaluation activity remained relatively steady from 2019 through 2024 partly because FAA extended evaluation intervals for qualifying simulators from the standard 12 months to as much as 36 months.

GAO found that FAA had not clearly communicated how it decides eligibility for the extended interval. Six of 10 selected sponsors raised communication concerns, and two cited possible safety issues from longer intervals. FAA had also identified mission-critical skills gaps, including standards development, without addressing all of them.

```text
more simulators and newer technology
  -> more oversight demand
  -> risk-based interval extension and stable evaluation volume
  -> sponsor feedback, standards, staff skills, correction
  -> [open] simulator fidelity, pilot performance, and aviation safety
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is an infrastructure version of the capacity-versus-coverage tension. A regulator can keep its visible workload stable by changing the schedule and prioritizing consistently strong performers. That may be efficient and safe—but only if the risk classification, communication, standards, and workforce remain good enough to identify when a simulator no longer qualifies.

The feedback route is part of oversight, not an optional courtesy. Sponsors operate the equipment and may have information about technical changes or emerging risks. If they do not understand the decision process or cannot provide relevant information, the regulator loses part of the evidence needed to set evaluation intervals.

## What the report does not establish

- The sponsor interviews were a selected, nongeneralizable sample, even though they covered over 62% of simulators.
- Longer evaluation intervals are not proof of unsafe oversight.
- Sponsor concerns are not evidence of simulator failure or increased accident risk.
- Stable evaluation counts do not reveal whether workload was fully met, reduced by efficiency, or deferred.
- The report does not follow a simulator to a pilot-training outcome, incident, or accident.

## Next test

Track each simulator through technology changes, evaluation eligibility, interval decision, sponsor feedback, staff review, deficiency, correction, pilot-training performance, and later safety signal. Publish the criteria and timing logic for extended intervals, with enough detail for sponsors and safety researchers to test whether coverage keeps pace with risk.

## Reading rule

Do not call oversight adequate because evaluation counts stayed stable. Ask what changed in the coverage rule, who supplied information, and whether the regulator still had the skills to detect risk.
