# When seller visibility becomes a customer cost

## The argument

On a marketplace, advertising can be the price a seller pays to be seen. The customer may never see that charge, but it can change which products appear first, how much the seller must charge, and which small firms can reach buyers. A sponsored result changes the first comparison before the customer reaches the receipt.

The current evidence comes from a newly filed FTC complaint. Its conduct and figures are allegations, not court findings. That matters because a claim that ad costs reached consumers requires a full chain: what the auction charged, what the seller did with the cost, what the customer saw, and what the customer finally paid.

That makes visibility a scarce input. A seller can have a good product and a low price yet receive few orders if buyers do not reach the listing. Paying for placement can then look less like optional marketing and more like rent for access to the shelf. But the seller still has several possible responses: pay and keep the price, pay and raise the price, cut other costs, accept fewer sales, or move to another channel. The platform's effect depends on how hard those alternatives are.

## Follow one sponsored purchase

A small seller pays to appear beside a search result. The charge rises. The seller absorbs it, raises the price, reduces quality, stops advertising, or leaves the platform. The customer sees a changed shelf before seeing a changed receipt. Another seller may pay more and win attention despite offering a worse fit. Visibility and price can move together, but neither movement is automatic.

The customer also pays with time. If paid placement pushes a better-fitting product down the page, the buyer must search longer, compare more reviews, and take more risk. If the buyer trusts the platform's order, the sponsored product may win without a full comparison. A platform can therefore change the market without changing every listed price: it changes what is easy to notice and what the customer must work to find.

The burden is different for different products. For a common item with many close substitutes, a seller may absorb a small ad charge or lose little by leaving. For a specialty part, a medicine-related product, or a product with strong repeat demand, visibility may be harder to replace. A small seller with one product may have less room to spread the charge than a large seller with many products. The same auction rule can therefore create different pressure across categories and firm sizes.

```text
ad-auction charge → seller visibility and cost → margin, price, quality, or exit
→ customer sees a changed set of products → search time, choice, trust, and final price change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Placement | The FTC complaint describes Sponsored Product, Sponsored Brand, and Display placements beside search results. | Paid placement can shape attention. |
| Auction claim | The FTC alleges Amazon represented a second-price auction but charged near the winning bid about 80% of the time by 2024. | The alleged rule could raise seller cost. |
| Scope claim | The complaint alleges the practice affected more than one million brands and sellers, including over 500,000 small and medium businesses. | The alleged exposure is broad. |
| Pass-through claim | The FTC alleges higher advertising costs were largely passed to American consumers. | The central price claim needs a seller-to-customer test. |
| Legal status | The figures and conduct are allegations in a filed case. | They are not established facts. |

## The deeper finding

Visibility is part of the price system even when it is not printed on the receipt. A seller may pay for attention, and a customer may pay through a higher price or a narrower set of visible options. The seller may also absorb the cost or leave the auction. The real market effect depends on which response is common for small and large sellers.

The deeper power is the combination of shelf control and measurement control. The platform can decide where a product appears, what data the seller sees, how performance is judged, and which fees count as the cost of reaching a buyer. A seller may know that sales fell but not whether the cause was price, ranking, ad delivery, reviews, or a change in the search system. That uncertainty makes it harder to bargain or plan inventory. A fee can affect a seller even before it is passed through because the seller must make decisions under incomplete information.

This can create a quiet loop. More sellers buy visibility because unpaid placement performs poorly. The platform gathers more data from the paid activity and uses the resulting sales to justify the ranking. Sellers then treat advertising as necessary to remain visible, while customers see a shelf increasingly shaped by payments. That loop is an inference from the alleged conduct, not a finding established by the complaint. It is the mechanism worth testing.

The public question is not whether advertising should exist. It is whether a marketplace that controls discovery can make sellers pay for access while presenting the result as a neutral search. The answer affects small-business entry, customer trust, prices, product variety, and the ability of a seller to leave without losing its customer base. A fee may be lawful and still reshape who can compete; a legal case must establish the separate question of whether the alleged conduct violated the law.

## What remains unproven

The complaint does not measure every arrow from auction charge to seller price to customer purchase. We still need auction logs, seller margins, product prices, quality, customer exposure, purchases, returns, and exit. A legal allegation cannot be presented as a measured household price increase.

We also do not know whether paid placement improved matching for some customers, whether customers noticed the sponsored label, or whether sellers would have spent the money on other ads. A higher advertising bill could reduce a seller's margin without raising the price. A lower listed price could conceal lower service, slower delivery, or weaker support. The test must preserve these possible responses instead of assuming pass-through.

## Next test

Collect auction bids, rules, charges, placements, seller prices, margins, product quality, customer views, purchases, returns, and exit. Compare small and large sellers across high- and low-demand periods, and track whether customers chose sponsored products or merely saw them.

Use controlled changes in placement or fee rules where legally and ethically possible. Follow the same product when it receives paid placement, unpaid placement, or placement on another channel. Measure discovery time, substitute comparison, conversion, final price, returns, seller margin, repeat purchase, and entry or exit. Ask customers after purchase what they saw and why they chose it. The key result is the full cost of access: money paid by the seller, attention spent by the customer, and the change in quality or choice that follows.

## Reading rule

Do not say an ad charge raised household prices until the record links the auction payment to the seller's price and the customer's final purchase.
