# Payment choice can move a cost through the price of everything else

## The argument

A payment method is not only a checkout preference. It can decide who receives a reward, who pays interest, who can dispute a charge, who gets a record, and who pays a merchant fee that may enter common prices.

The evidence supports uneven exposure, not a simple story that one group always loses. A credit-card user who pays in full may receive rewards and protection. A revolving borrower may pay more in interest than rewards return. A cash or debit user may pay a price that reflects card costs without receiving the reward. The net result depends on the same person's price, reward, balance, protection, and ability to switch.

## Follow one purchase

A shopper buys the same item as three neighbors. One uses a rewards card and pays in full. One carries a balance. One uses cash. The merchant sees different payment costs but may post one price. The reward system and the common price connect all three households even though only one sees the points.

```text
payment choice → merchant fee and reward funding → common price or payment benefit
→ household net cost, balance, protection, and trust → ability to shop or switch
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Choice | Federal Reserve evidence shows credit, debit, and cash use differ by income and age. | Payment access is uneven. |
| Transfer | NBER estimates about $30 billion a year in transfer through interchange-funded rewards. | A system cost can move between users through prices. |
| Reward | CFPB reports reward denials, changing value, and hard-to-see conditions; revolving borrowers may pay more in interest and fees than rewards return. | The advertised reward is not always the net gain. |
| Missing household result | Public studies do not show one shopper's price, reward, interest, protection, and ability to switch together. | System exposure is not a personal loss proof. |

Sources: [NBER payment rewards](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067), [Federal Reserve payment research](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/pay-by-bank-and-the-merchant-payments-use-case-benefits-20250707.html), and [CFPB rewards report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-frustrations-with-credit-card-rewards-programs/).

## The deeper finding

Payment systems distribute value through ordinary prices. That makes checkout rules part of household finance and customer power. A lower-cost payment method is not automatically better if it is less safe or cannot preserve a record. A reward is not automatically a gain if it creates a carried balance.

## What remains unproven

The NBER estimate is a system result, not a bill for one household. The sources do not yet show the same person's prices, rewards, interest, fraud protection, privacy, and ability to choose another method over time.

## Next test

Link household purchases to payment method, merchant fee, posted price, reward, interest, dispute, fraud, and later balance. Compare users by income, age, place, bank access, and ability to pay in full.

## Reading rule

Keep the $30 billion system estimate separate from a person's actual net result. Uneven exposure is supported; “this household lost” still needs linked records.
