# Payment choice is a distribution question, but not yet a household welfare result

## Short answer

The payment system appears to spread costs and benefits unevenly. The clearest evidence is a system estimate: merchant card fees can move money from cash and debit users toward higher-reward credit-card users through common prices. Federal Reserve evidence shows that payment choice differs by income and age. CFPB evidence shows that rewards can be hard to receive and may be outweighed by interest for people who carry a balance.

That is enough to treat payment choice as a customer and distribution question. It is not enough to say which household is worse off, because we do not yet see the same person's price, reward, interest, security benefit and ability to switch together.

## What the matched evidence adds

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Choice | Credit, debit and cash are all common; use differs by income and age. | Whether the choice was free, forced, safer, or cheaper for that person. |
| Merchant side | NBER estimates about $30 billion a year in transfer through interchange-funded rewards. Sorting and merchant fee differences reduce, but do not remove, it. | The exact shelf-price change faced by one shopper. |
| Cardholder side | CFPB reports reward denials, changing value and hard-to-see conditions; revolving borrowers may pay more in interest and fees than rewards return. | How common each problem is across all card users. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 35067](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067), [Federal Reserve payment research](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/pay-by-bank-and-the-merchant-payments-use-case-benefits-20250707.html), [CFPB rewards report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-frustrations-with-credit-card-rewards-programs/).

## The connection

```text
payment need or preference
  -> payment method
  -> merchant fee and reward funding
  -> common price or payment benefit
  -> household net cost, balance and trust
  -> ability to shop, save, borrow, or switch
```

The deeper point is about exposure. A payment method is not only a checkout tool. It can decide who receives a reward, who pays an interest charge, who can dispute a transaction, and who has a safe way to pay when cash or a network is unavailable.

## Four views

**Household:** Count price, reward, interest, annual fee, fraud protection, records, privacy and the ability to use another method.

**Company and sector:** Merchants choose whether to absorb fees, change prices, add a surcharge, limit payment types, or use rewards to bring customers back.

**Money and finance:** A reward is not a gain if the balance carries costly interest. A low-fee method is not always usable or safe for every purchase.

**Public power:** Rules about interchange, disclosures, surcharges, cash access and dispute rights shape who can avoid the cost and who cannot.

## What would change the finding

- Same-household prices, rewards, balances, interest and payment protections over time.
- Payment choice by income, age, place, race, disability and access to banks or cash.
- Merchant prices and margins before and after a payment-fee or rewards-rule change.
- The value of security, records, credit access and convenience, not only dollars moved.
- A strong counterexample in which lower-cost payment users receive equal or greater net value.

## Reading rule

Keep the $30 billion system estimate separate from a person's actual net result. “Uneven exposure” is supported; “this household lost” still needs linked records.
