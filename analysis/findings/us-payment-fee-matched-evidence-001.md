# When payment choice moves a cost through everything else

## The argument

A payment method is not only a checkout preference. It can decide who receives a reward, who pays interest, who can dispute a charge, who gets a clean record, and who helps fund a merchant fee that may enter common prices.

The evidence supports uneven exposure, not a simple story that one group always loses. A credit-card user who pays in full may receive rewards and protection. A revolving borrower may pay more in interest than the rewards return. A cash or debit user may face a price that reflects card costs without receiving the reward. The net result depends on price, reward, balance, protection, and ability to switch for the same person.

## Follow one purchase through three households

Three neighbors buy the same item. One uses a rewards card and pays in full. One carries a balance. One uses cash. The merchant sees different payment costs but posts one price. The reward system and the common price connect all three households even though only one sees the points.

```text
payment choice → merchant fee and reward funding → common price or payment benefit
→ household net cost, balance, protection, and trust → ability to shop or switch
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Uneven access | Federal Reserve evidence shows credit, debit, and cash use differ by income and age. | Payment choice is not equally available. |
| Transfer | NBER estimates about $30 billion a year in transfer through interchange-funded rewards. | A system cost can move between users through prices. |
| Reward risk | CFPB reports reward denials, changing value, and hard-to-see conditions; revolving borrowers may pay more in interest and fees than rewards return. | The advertised reward is not always the net gain. |
| Consumer protection | Credit may provide dispute or fraud protections that cash does not. | A lower-money-cost method may carry a different risk. |
| Limits | Public studies do not show one shopper's price, reward, interest, protection, and ability to switch together. | System exposure is not proof of personal loss. |

Sources: [NBER payment rewards](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067), [Federal Reserve payment research](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/pay-by-bank-and-the-merchant-payments-use-case-benefits-20250707.html), and [CFPB rewards report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-frustrations-with-credit-card-rewards-programs/).

## The deeper finding

Payment systems distribute value through ordinary prices. Checkout is therefore part of household finance and customer power. A reward is not automatically a gain if it creates a carried balance. A cash purchase is not automatically cheaper if the posted price includes card costs and the cash user lacks a useful dispute path.

## What remains unproven

The NBER estimate is a system result, not a bill for one household. The sources do not show the same person's prices, rewards, interest, fraud protection, privacy, and ability to choose another method over time. They also do not show how merchants change prices when payment costs change.

## Next test

Link household purchases to payment method, merchant fee, posted price, reward, interest, dispute, fraud, and later balance. Compare users by income, age, place, bank access, and ability to pay in full.

## Reading rule

Keep the $30 billion system estimate separate from a person's actual net result. Uneven exposure is supported; “this household lost” needs linked records.
