# Payment choice changes who carries the price

## The argument

The price on a shelf is not always the whole price of buying. A merchant may pay a different fee when a customer uses cash, debit, or a rewards credit card. The merchant can respond with one common price, a surcharge, a discount, a lower margin, or a decision about which payments to accept. The customer may then carry a cost without seeing it as a payment cost.

That does not make every card purchase harmful. A person who pays in full may receive a useful reward and fraud protection. Another person may carry a balance and pay interest. A cash or debit user may face a price that reflects card costs without receiving the reward. The same payment system can give value to one household and take value from another.

The deeper finding is about the path between the fee and the household. A national transfer estimate is not a family budget. To know who gains or loses, we need the price paid, reward received, interest charged, protection used, and payment choices available to that household.

## Follow one ordinary purchase

Three neighbors buy the same item. One uses a rewards card and pays in full. One carries a balance. One uses cash. The merchant sees different payment costs but posts one price. The reward system and common price connect all three households even though only one sees the points.

The first customer may gain after rewards. The second may pay more in interest than the reward returns. The third may avoid debt but lack a useful dispute path. If the merchant later adds a surcharge or stops taking cash, the available choices change again.

```text
payment method
  -> merchant fee and customer reward or interest
  -> common price, surcharge, discount, margin, or acceptance
  -> household’s effective cost and cash flexibility
  -> shopping place, debt, protection, and future payment choice
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NBER estimates about $30 billion a year in transfer through interchange-funded rewards. | Payment design can redistribute value even when the shelf price looks shared. | It is a system estimate, not one household’s bill. |
| Federal Reserve evidence shows credit, debit, and cash use differ by income and age. | Payment choice is not equally available. | Use is not the same as net benefit. |
| CFPB reports reward denials, changing value, and hard-to-see conditions; revolving borrowers may pay more in interest and fees than rewards return. | The advertised reward is not always the net gain. | The report does not calculate every user’s full cost. |
| Credit may provide dispute or fraud protections that cash does not. | A lower-money-cost method can carry a different risk. | Protection depends on the product and situation. |
| Public studies do not show one shopper’s price, reward, interest, protection, and ability to switch together. | System exposure is not proof of personal loss. | Household linkage remains the next step. |

Sources: [NBER payment rewards](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067), [Federal Reserve payment research](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/pay-by-bank-and-the-merchant-payments-use-case-benefits-20250707.html), and [CFPB rewards report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-frustrations-with-credit-card-rewards-programs/).

## The deeper finding

Payment choice is a distribution system. It can move money from cash and debit users to rewards-card users, from shoppers to merchants, or from today’s purchase to a later interest bill. Checkout is therefore part of household finance and customer power.

The right comparison is not “card or cash.” It is the household’s net position after price, reward, interest, access, dispute protection, privacy, and the ability to pay the balance in full. A policy that changes one fee can move the burden rather than remove it.

## What remains unknown

The evidence does not show the complete net cost for the same US households over time. It does not tell us how payment fees change grocery prices in each place, how often low-income households pay interest after using rewards cards, or whether cash discounts reach the people most affected.

## Next test

Follow households and merchants for one year. Record payment method, merchant fee, posted price, surcharge or discount, reward, interest, credit limit, balance payment, merchant acceptance, shopping location, and the purchase that would have been made if the first method were unavailable. Compare results by income, savings, credit access, and place.

## Reading rule

Keep the merchant fee, common price, reward, interest bill, and household’s net cost separate.
