# The cost of staying in a home is a stack of bills and risks

## The argument

Housing becomes unsafe before a household moves. The mortgage or rent may be unchanged while energy, insurance, repairs, transport, and debt payments take more of the monthly budget. Each bill is often studied on its own. The household experiences the stack.

Picture a low-income owner in a coastal county. The home needs a repair, the insurance renewal is higher, and summer power costs rise. The owner can pay the bills, cut food or medicine, borrow, delay the repair, reduce coverage, or sell. A renter faces a different version: the landlord controls insulation and appliances, while the renter pays the power bill and may pay higher rent when the building’s insurance rises. The same place can therefore create different risks for the person who owns it and the person who lives in it.

The deeper finding is not that energy or insurance alone causes displacement. It is that the households with the least cash, credit, and ability to change the building may have the fewest ways to lower the cost of staying. The evidence supports the separate pressures. It does not yet show which household pays which cost first.

## What the sources establish

DOE’s LEAD tool estimates average energy burden at about 6% of gross income for low-income households and about 2% for other households; in some places the burden is above 30%. EIA’s 2024 survey reports energy insecurity in 43.56 million US homes, including 32.89 million homes that reduced or gave up food or medicine to pay energy costs.

GAO reports that homeowners insurance premiums rose unevenly between 2019 and 2024, with increases of 25% or more in some southern coastal areas, while high disaster risk also reduced coverage availability. NBER’s study of more than 74 million mortgage-linked premium observations estimates that higher premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes. That is an estimate for exposed places, not a loss for every owner.

These measures establish pressure and local exposure. They do not follow the same household through the power bill, insurance renewal, repair, food choice, borrowing, and move.

Sources: [DOE LEAD](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf), [GAO homeowners insurance](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107867), and [NBER property insurance and home prices](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579).

## Follow the stack

home quality, fuel, weather, local risk, and transport → energy, insurance, repair, and travel costs → food, care, debt, savings, comfort, or ability to move → aid, repairs, coverage changes, borrowing, sale, or staying put

The stack matters because the household cannot always choose the cheapest response. A renter may not be able to weatherize. An owner may lack the cash or credit for a roof. A family in a high-risk place may find fewer insurers. A person already cutting food for power has less room for a new premium or repair.

These are working mechanisms, not measured outcomes from one linked record. The important search question is where control sits: with the household, landlord, utility, insurer, lender, or public program.

## What must stay separate

- A bill’s dollar amount and its share of income.
- Energy insecurity and insurance noncoverage.
- A home’s market value and its ability to shelter the household.
- A renter’s cost and the owner’s cost.
- A repair delayed by choice and a repair delayed because cash is absent.
- A premium increase and a forced move.

## Next test

Choose two high-exposure states. Join county or ZIP-level energy burden, insurance premium share, building age, tenure, utility arrears or shutoff, insurance nonrenewal, repair aid, and moves. Compare renters, mortgaged owners, and owners without mortgages. The first useful result is not a grand causal claim; it is a map showing which costs meet in the same places and periods.

## Reading rule

Do not say a home is unaffordable from rent or mortgage alone. Ask what it costs to keep the home warm, insured, repaired, reachable, and safe.
