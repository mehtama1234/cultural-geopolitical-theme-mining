# The cost of staying in a home is a stack of bills and risks

## Short answer

A home can become harder to keep before anyone moves. Energy takes a larger share of income in low-income households. Insurance has risen much faster in some disaster-prone places and can also affect the value of a home. These pressures are usually counted in separate systems, but they meet in one household budget.

The deeper finding is simple: the people with the least money to repair, move, or replace equipment may have the fewest ways to lower the cost of staying. The sources do not yet prove that the same households face all of these pressures at once. That is the next test.

## What the sources show

### 1. The energy bill takes a different share of the budget

The Department of Energy's LEAD tool estimates average energy burden at about 6% of gross income for low-income households and about 2% for non-low-income households. In some places, the burden is above 30%. The measure covers electricity, gas and other household fuels, but not transportation.

The difference matters even when two households pay the same dollar bill. A bill that is manageable for one family can crowd out food, care, rent, debt payments or savings for another.

[DOE Low-Income Energy Affordability Data tool](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool)

### 2. Energy insecurity is a choice made under pressure

The 2024 Residential Energy Consumption Survey reports that 43.56 million of an estimated 132.54 million US homes had some form of energy insecurity. About 32.89 million reported reducing or forgoing food or medicine to pay energy costs. These are household reports, not a model of what happened next.

This is important because “energy burden” can sound like a price measure. The survey shows a household response: people change another need to keep the home supplied with energy.

[EIA 2024 household energy insecurity table](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf)

### 3. Protection from outside risk can become a second housing bill

The Government Accountability Office found that average US homeowners insurance premiums rose about 3% after inflation from 2019 through 2024. In some southern coastal areas, premiums rose 25% or more. It also found that high disaster risk can reduce the availability of coverage, not only raise its price.

That makes “can I afford the home?” a different question from “can I keep the home insured?” Renters, homeowners with mortgages, and people in state-backed or last-resort plans may face different versions of this problem.

[GAO homeowners insurance review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107867)

### 4. The insurance bill can reach the asset as well as the budget

Keys and Mulder study more than 74 million premiums inferred from mortgage escrow payments from 2014 through 2024. They estimate that rising premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes.

This is an estimate for the most exposed places, not a $40,000 loss for every homeowner. It does show why insurance belongs in a housing and household-wealth story: a family can pay more each month while the home becomes less attractive to buyers.

[NBER Working Paper 32579](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579)

## The connection

These sources fit a single household question:

```text
home quality, fuel, weather, and local risk
  -> energy bill and insurance bill
  -> food, care, debt, savings, comfort, or ability to move
  -> utility relief, repairs, public aid, lender rules, or political pressure
```

The evidence supports each step up to the bills and reported energy choices. It does not yet show the whole path for one family. The useful finding is therefore not “energy costs cause displacement” or “insurance causes political anger.” It is that housing stability is being priced through several channels, while the ability to change those channels is uneven.

## Who may have less room to respond

- A renter may pay the energy bill but cannot install insulation or replace a failing appliance.
- A low-income owner may own the building but lack cash or credit for repairs and may face a higher insurance bill.
- A household in a high-risk place may pay more for coverage, accept a larger gap in protection, or find fewer insurers.
- A family already cutting food or medicine for energy has less room to absorb a new insurance or repair cost.

These are mechanisms to test, not measured outcomes from the four sources above.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** DOE reports different average energy burdens by income. EIA reports household energy insecurity. GAO reports uneven insurance changes and availability. NBER estimates a home-value effect in the most exposed ZIP codes.

**Inference:** These costs may reinforce one another in the same household budget, especially where people cannot change the building, fuel, insurer, or location.

**Unknown:** We do not yet know which cost is paid first, which need is cut first, or whether the household moves, borrows, seeks aid, or stays put.

## What would change the finding

- Linked household or property records showing energy payments, insurance premiums, income and moves over time.
- Separate results for renters, owners with mortgages, and owners without mortgages.
- Evidence on whether weatherization lowers later food, care, debt or medical tradeoffs.
- Utility arrears, shutoff, insurance nonrenewal and assistance records joined by place and income.
- A comparison of similar homes where mitigation aid or insurance support changed the available choices.

## Next test

Choose two high-exposure states and compare counties by income, tenure, building age, energy burden, insurance premium share, shutoff or arrears records, and moves. Start with public state and utility records. Do not claim that the costs reinforce one another until at least two of those measures can be placed on the same geography and time line.

## Reading rule

The source sections report findings from separate studies. The connection and household examples are working interpretations. They should guide the next search, not be read as a completed causal account.
