# When the cost of staying in a home becomes a stack of bills

## The argument

Housing becomes unsafe before a household moves. The mortgage or rent may be unchanged while energy, insurance, repairs, transport, and debt payments take more of the monthly budget. Each bill is often studied alone. The household experiences the stack.

A low-income owner in a coastal county may face a higher insurance renewal, a roof repair, and a summer power bill at the same time. A renter faces a different version: the landlord controls insulation and appliances while the renter pays the power bill and may later face higher rent when the building's costs rise. The same place creates different risks for the person who owns it and the person who lives in it.

The timing matters. A household may manage one high bill, but not three arriving before the next paycheck. It may keep the mortgage current by letting the car repair wait, or keep the power on by using a credit card. The home becomes harder to keep when several required costs draw on the same small reserve.

The stack is not just the sum of bills. It includes the cost of responding when one bill rises: extra transport after a repair, interest after a card charge, a deposit after an insurance cancellation, or food lost during a shutoff. Those second-order costs can be larger than the first increase. A useful housing measure must record what the household did next.

## Follow the stack through one month

Rent is due Friday. The power bill is high after a heat wave. A repair cannot wait, and the family's car is needed for work. The household can cut food or medicine, borrow, delay the repair, reduce comfort, ask for aid, or move. None of these choices is simply “housing cost.” They are ways of keeping the home reachable and usable.

The order matters. Paying insurance may protect the house but leave the car unrepaired. Paying the car may protect work but leave the utility bill late. Taking a loan may protect all three this month while making next month tighter. A yearly total cannot show which protection the household bought first or what it sacrificed to do so.

This is why a household can be “housed” and still be close to losing the conditions that make the housing work. It may stay indoors while living without enough cooling, insurance, transport, or repair capacity. A move is the visible end of the process; the strain often starts much earlier.

```text
home quality, weather, local risk, and transport → energy, insurance, repair, and travel costs
→ food, care, debt, savings, comfort, or ability to move → aid, coverage change, borrowing, sale, or staying
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Energy burden | DOE's LEAD tool estimates average energy burden at about 6% of gross income for low-income households and about 2% for other households; some places are above 30%. | The same bill takes different shares of income. |
| Food and medicine trade | EIA's 2024 survey reports energy insecurity in 43.56 million US homes, including 32.89 million that reduced or gave up food or medicine to pay energy costs. | Energy pressure can reach basic needs. |
| Insurance | GAO reports homeowners-insurance premiums rose unevenly between 2019 and 2024, with increases of 25% or more in some southern coastal areas, while high disaster risk reduced coverage availability. | Local risk can add a second housing bill and reduce choice. |
| Home value | NBER estimates higher premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes. | Protection costs and property value can move together. |
| Limits | These measures do not follow one household through power, insurance, repair, food, borrowing, and moving. | Pressure is established; the full sequence is not. |

Sources: [DOE LEAD](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf), [GAO homeowners insurance](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107867), and [NBER property insurance and home prices](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579).

## The deeper finding

The households with the least cash, credit, and control over the building may have the fewest ways to lower the cost of staying. A renter may not be able to weatherize. An owner may lack cash for a roof. A family already cutting food for power has less room for a new premium. The stack matters because the responses compete for the same dollars.

There is also a threshold effect. A household may cope with rent, energy, and insurance separately until one more increase removes the last reserve. After that point, the response can change sharply: a missed payment, a move, a dangerous repair delay, or a loss of coverage. The average bill increase may look small even when it crosses a household’s practical limit.

The stack also separates responsibility from exposure. A renter may pay the power bill but cannot replace a leaking window. An owner may control the building but cannot control an insurer's renewal decision. A worker may need the car to reach the home and the job that pays for it. Each person faces a cost without controlling the part of the system that could lower it.

When households repeatedly patch the stack with debt, lower comfort, family help, or delayed repairs, they may blame the landlord, insurer, utility, lender, or government depending on which bill arrived first. A local insurance rule, weatherization program, utility payment plan, or transit change can alter the whole budget even if it does not change rent. The evidence supports this linked pressure; it does not show which institution households blame or whether the pressure changes a vote.

This is why the stack is also a political map. The household meets the landlord at rent, the utility at service, the insurer at renewal, the lender at repayment, and the road or transit system at the commute. A single household problem is distributed across institutions, so responsibility can be hard to name. A serious study should observe the contact and the rule that came before the response, rather than assume blame from the final move.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show which household pays which cost first, whether aid arrives before the break, or whether delayed repair, lower comfort, noncoverage, or moving creates the largest later loss. It does not show how renters and owners divide the cost when a building is inefficient or a policy is not renewed. A premium increase or energy burden is not by itself a forced move.

It also does not show the reserve before the bill, the unpaid family help, the debt terms, or the quality of the home that the spending preserved. Two households can have the same total housing share and different safety because one is one bill away from losing service and the other has savings.

## Next test

Choose two high-exposure states. Join energy burden, insurance share, building age, tenure, utility arrears or shutoff, nonrenewal, repair aid, transport access, and moves. Compare renters, mortgaged owners, and owners without mortgages by month, not only by annual average. Follow households through a heat wave, renewal notice, or major repair and record which bill was paid, which was delayed, and what support arrived before the next shock.

Build a monthly home ledger with every required housing-related payment, due date, reserve, credit used, family help, service status, indoor condition, repair, travel, and move option. Mark each cost as controlled by the household, landlord, insurer, utility, lender, or public rule. Follow the household for two years and compare bill aid, repair, weatherization, insurance support, transit, and debt relief. Report remaining cash, safe use, coverage, work and care access, debt, and housing stability separately. This would show when the stack crosses a threshold and which intervention can actually lower it.

## Reading rule

Do not say a home is affordable from rent or mortgage alone. Ask what it costs to keep the home warm, insured, repaired, reachable, and safe.
