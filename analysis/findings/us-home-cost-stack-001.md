# When the cost of staying in a home becomes a stack of bills

## The argument

Housing becomes unsafe before a household moves. The mortgage or rent may be unchanged while energy, insurance, repairs, transport, and debt payments take more of the monthly budget. Each bill is often studied alone. The household experiences the stack.

A low-income owner in a coastal county may face a higher insurance renewal, a roof repair, and a summer power bill at the same time. A renter faces a different version: the landlord controls insulation and appliances while the renter pays the power bill and may later face higher rent when the building's costs rise. The same place creates different risks for the person who owns it and the person who lives in it.

## Follow the stack through one month

Rent is due Friday. The power bill is high after a heat wave. A repair cannot wait, and the family's car is needed for work. The household can cut food or medicine, borrow, delay the repair, reduce comfort, ask for aid, or move. None of these choices is simply “housing cost.” They are ways of keeping the home reachable and usable.

```text
home quality, weather, local risk, and transport → energy, insurance, repair, and travel costs
→ food, care, debt, savings, comfort, or ability to move → aid, coverage change, borrowing, sale, or staying
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Energy burden | DOE's LEAD tool estimates average energy burden at about 6% of gross income for low-income households and about 2% for other households; some places are above 30%. | The same bill takes different shares of income. |
| Food and medicine trade | EIA's 2024 survey reports energy insecurity in 43.56 million US homes, including 32.89 million that reduced or gave up food or medicine to pay energy costs. | Energy pressure can reach basic needs. |
| Insurance | GAO reports homeowners-insurance premiums rose unevenly between 2019 and 2024, with increases of 25% or more in some southern coastal areas, while high disaster risk reduced coverage availability. | Local risk can add a second housing bill and reduce choice. |
| Home value | NBER estimates higher premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes. | Protection costs and property value can move together. |
| Limits | These measures do not follow one household through power, insurance, repair, food, borrowing, and moving. | Pressure is established; the full sequence is not. |

Sources: [DOE LEAD](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf), [GAO homeowners insurance](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107867), and [NBER property insurance and home prices](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32579).

## The deeper finding

The households with the least cash, credit, and control over the building may have the fewest ways to lower the cost of staying. A renter may not be able to weatherize. An owner may lack cash for a roof. A family already cutting food for power has less room for a new premium. The stack matters because the responses compete for the same dollars.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show which household pays which cost first, whether aid arrives before the break, or whether delayed repair, lower comfort, noncoverage, or moving creates the largest later loss. A premium increase or energy burden is not by itself a forced move.

## Next test

Choose two high-exposure states. Join energy burden, insurance share, building age, tenure, utility arrears or shutoff, nonrenewal, repair aid, and moves. Compare renters, mortgaged owners, and owners without mortgages by month, not only by annual average.

## Reading rule

Do not say a home is affordable from rent or mortgage alone. Ask what it costs to keep the home warm, insured, repaired, reachable, and safe.
