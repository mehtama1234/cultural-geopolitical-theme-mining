# Efficiency savings are real but smaller than the promise

## The argument

Weatherization can lower energy use and improve safety and comfort. But projected savings can overstate what a household actually receives. An upgrade is not successful because a model predicted a lower bill. The home must use less energy without leaving people cold, unsafe, or unable to pay another basic cost.

The same measured decline in energy use can mean different things. The building may have improved. A family may still be rationing heat. Equipment may have changed. The bill may have fallen because the household spent less time at home. Without temperature, air, safety, health, and work records, the number is hard to interpret.

The deeper finding is that energy work sits between housing quality and household finance. A good upgrade can protect cash and health for years. A badly targeted or delayed upgrade can leave the family paying the same risk in a different form.

The work also has a time cost. A household may wait through a winter, move before the contractor arrives, or take time off for an inspection and repair. A tenant may qualify but lose the lease before the owner approves the work. A retrofit that is free at the point of service can still be hard to reach if the family cannot wait or cannot control the building.

## Follow one home through winter

A renter lives in a drafty apartment. Weatherization seals leaks and checks the furnace. The bill falls, but the family still uses less heat than it needs because money is tight. Or the home becomes warmer, the child misses fewer school days, and the parent misses less work. The energy number alone cannot tell these stories apart.

Renters also depend on landlord permission, contractor quality, and the time spent waiting. A household can qualify and still receive no help before the next winter.

The benefit can arrive through fewer bad days rather than a lower annual bill. A safer furnace may reduce carbon-monoxide risk. A drier home may reduce mold exposure. A more stable temperature may improve sleep. A family may use more energy after the work because it stops rationing heat. That can be a successful health result and a weak bill result at the same time.

The upgrade can fail in ordinary ways. A seal may not address a broken system, a filter may not be replaced, or a repair may be delayed because the owner does not want the cost. A contractor may complete the listed measure while leaving the largest source of loss untouched. The work order is evidence that something happened, not proof that the home became safe or cheaper to run.

```text
weatherization work
  -> actual energy use and bill
  -> temperature, air quality, safety, and comfort
  -> health, work, care, and household cash
  -> future repair, housing, and trust choices
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NBER’s experimental evaluation of more than 30,000 households found actual energy savings roughly half the level predicted by engineering models. | Forecast savings should not be treated as household savings. | It does not explain every measure or home. |
| NBER found no evidence of significantly higher indoor temperatures after weatherization. | Lower energy use did not clearly translate into warmer rooms in that study. | Temperature, drafts, and comfort need more direct measurement. |
| DOE includes indoor air, combustion, carbon monoxide, mold, and equipment checks. | Health and safety are part of the work, not side benefits. | The rules do not show the result in each home. |
| DOE serves renters and owners and prioritizes vulnerable and high-burden households. | Need and access are recognized in program design. | Completion, wait time, and landlord permission remain open. |

Sources: [NBER weatherization evaluation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21331), [DOE weatherization application](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/wap/how-apply-weatherization-assistance), [DOE LEAD](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), and [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=56640).

There is a control and value problem. The tenant may receive lower bills while the landlord receives a higher-value property. The owner may pay for the work while the tenant's behavior determines use. Public money may reduce a private owner's repair cost without a rule that protects the resident from a rent increase or loss of the home. The distribution of the benefit belongs in the evaluation.

Weatherization also competes with other forms of help. A bill grant can stop a shutoff quickly; an upgrade may take months but reduce future use. A repair can protect health without lowering much energy. A housing move can solve a building problem while breaking access to school, work, or care. The best intervention depends on the first failure and the time available.

The political meaning comes from the gap between promise and lived result. If a program advertises a large saving and delivers a smaller one, households may distrust the program even if comfort or safety improved. If it reports health gains without showing who paid the bill and who controlled the fix, owners and tenants may read the result differently. Clear reporting should show predicted, actual, and lived outcomes separately.

## The deeper finding

Lower measured use does not automatically mean a healthier home. It may mean that the upgrade worked, that the household is still rationing heat, or that another change affected use. The real result has at least five parts: bill, safe temperature, air and equipment safety, health, and money left for other needs.

This changes how public programs should be judged. A projected saving is a planning estimate. An actual bill is one result. A household outcome shows whether the upgrade gave people more room to live safely.

## What remains unknown

The evidence does not join same-home bills, temperatures, air quality, equipment, health events, missed work, care, shutoffs, and housing stability before and after work. It does not show which homes explain the gap between predicted and actual savings.

It also does not establish whether lower use reflected efficiency, less time at home, mild weather, equipment failure, or continued sacrifice. Nor does it show how often renters lose access because of landlord refusal, how long households wait, or whether repairs last. Average savings cannot answer those questions.

## Next test

Follow renters, owners, manufactured homes, rural homes, and low-income households through two heating and cooling seasons. Join work completed, bill, temperature, air quality, safety repair, health, work, care, shutoff, rent, and debt. Compare predicted savings with actual household room.

Record application, approval, inspection, contractor visit, repair, move, rent, and ownership dates. Measure energy use, bill, indoor temperature, humidity, air quality, carbon monoxide, equipment uptime, sleep, respiratory or heat events, school and work attendance, food and medicine tradeoffs, and debt. Compare weatherization, direct bill aid, repair, rent protection, and relocation help by the home's first failure. The key result is whether the household gained safe comfort and cash room that lasted beyond the next season.

## Reading rule

Do not call weatherization successful from a modeled saving. Show the bill, the room, the air, the safety result, and the household’s later choices.
