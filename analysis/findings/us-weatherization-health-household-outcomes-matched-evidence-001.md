# Efficiency savings are real but smaller than the promise

## Short answer

Weatherization can lower energy use and improve safety and comfort, but projected savings can overstate what households receive. NBER's experimental evaluation of more than 30,000 households found actual energy savings roughly half the level predicted by engineering models and found no evidence of significantly higher indoor temperatures after weatherization. DOE treats health and safety as part of the work and gives access to renters and owners. The deeper finding is that a successful upgrade must be judged by actual bills, safe temperature, air quality, health, and household cash—not by projected energy savings alone.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Layer | What the evidence says | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Promise | Engineering models predicted more savings than the NBER evaluation observed. | Which measures and homes explain the gap. |
| Bill | Weatherization can reduce energy use and cost. | Same-home bill change after work and over time. |
| Comfort | NBER found no evidence of significantly higher indoor temperatures in weatherized homes. | Temperature, drafts, noise, and comfort by season. |
| Health and safety | DOE includes indoor air, combustion, carbon monoxide, mold, and equipment checks. | Health events, missed work, care, and medical cost. |
| Access | DOE serves renters and owners and prioritizes vulnerable and high-burden households. | Completion, wait time, landlord permission, and unequal reach. |

Sources: [NBER weatherization evaluation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21331), [DOE weatherization application](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/wap/how-apply-weatherization-assistance), [DOE LEAD health and affordability](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool), and [EIA energy insecurity](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=56640).

## The connection

```text
weatherization work
  → actual energy use and bill
  → temperature, air quality, safety, and comfort
  → health, work, care, and household cash
  → future repair, housing, and trust choices
```

The NBER study anchors the actual-savings result. DOE documents the broader health and safety path. The longer-run household outcome remains open.

## The useful surprise

Lower measured energy use does not automatically mean a household is warmer or healthier. A family may use less energy because the home improved, because it still cannot afford enough heat, or because the equipment or behavior changed. The outcome must be measured directly.

## Four views

**Household:** Count bill, temperature, air quality, safety, comfort, repair, health, work, care, and time.

**Customer and resident:** Record who requested the work, who gave permission, what was installed, and whether the home remained usable during it.

**Money and finance:** Compare predicted and actual savings with program cost, rent, debt, medical cost, and money left for essentials.

**Public power:** Measure eligibility, waitlist, completion, contractor quality, landlord cooperation, and outcomes by place and group.

## What would change the finding

- Same-home bills, temperatures, air quality, and equipment records before and after work.
- Health, missed-work, care, shutoff, and housing-stability records.
- Predicted savings compared with actual savings by measure and home type.
- Results for renters, owners, manufactured homes, rural homes, and low-income households.
- Completion and wait-time records, including deferred health and safety repairs.

## Reading rule

Do not call weatherization successful from a modelled saving. Show the bill, the room, the air, the safety result, and the household's later choices.
