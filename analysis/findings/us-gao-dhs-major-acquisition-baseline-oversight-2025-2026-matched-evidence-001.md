# A security program can be on track after the target moves

## The deeper finding

GAO reviewed 27 major Department of Homeland Security acquisition programs.
Nineteen had approved cost, schedule, and performance baselines. Fifteen of
those 19 later revised their baselines, and collectively projected costs rose
by $11.4 billion, or 26%, from the initial baselines. Eighteen of the 19 were
meeting their most recent baseline goals.

At the same time, eight programs experienced staff reductions of at least 20%
in FY2025, with lost subject-matter expertise or technical skills. DHS also
dissolved a department-level acquisition oversight office in October 2025;
officials were unsure in January 2026 what level of oversight would continue,
and at least part of the office was reconstituted by May.

```text
national-security requirement
  -> cost/schedule/performance baseline
  -> revision, staffing change, or oversight change
  -> current-plan status
  -> [open] delivered capability, mission use, user performance, external effect
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a geopolitical version of the atlas's capacity-versus-control and
capability-versus-realization tensions. “On schedule” can be true relative to
the latest plan while the original promise has moved. A large appropriation or
a current green status therefore tells only part of the story: the institution
may be adapting its target as costs, technology, staffing, and threat demands
change.

The cultural and political meaning is about credibility. Public officials,
workers, contractors, and allies may hear “the program is meeting goals,” while
the relevant goal has been revised and the oversight structure is unsettled.
That does not make revision illegitimate. It means legitimacy depends on making
the changes visible and showing what capability is actually available.

## What the report does not establish

- The 27 programs were selected from DHS's largest acquisitions, not sampled
  randomly.
- Rebaselining can reflect better information or changed requirements; it is
  not proof of concealment or failure.
- Meeting the latest baseline does not establish that an operational capability
  was delivered or used successfully.
- Staff reductions and oversight reorganization are not shown to have caused a
  missed milestone or safety problem.
- The report does not measure a common mission outcome, user experience,
  contractor performance, or changed foreign behavior.

## Next test

Follow one named DHS program from its original requirement through baseline
revision, funding, staffing, testing, operational delivery, user or unit
performance, sustainment, cost, and external response. Keep the original and
current baselines side by side, and record who approved each change and what
evidence justified it.

## Reading rule

Do not read “meeting the current baseline” as “delivered the original promise.”
Keep the original target, revised target, resources, oversight, and operational
result visible as separate stages.
