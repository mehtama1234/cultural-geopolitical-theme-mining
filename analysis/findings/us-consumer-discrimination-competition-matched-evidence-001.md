# Competition helps only when the customer can leave

## The argument

More sellers do not automatically mean fairer treatment. Competition helps a customer only when the customer can compare, switch, and receive a reasonably similar service somewhere else. Distance, money, information, identity checks, language, transport, and the cost of moving all decide whether choice is real.

This matters for discrimination because a firm’s incentive depends on the customer’s actual exit. A customer who can move to another lender or store can punish poor treatment. A customer who needs a particular branch, network, language service, credit product, or accessible location may have several logos but no cheap substitute. The market looks open from above while the customer is trapped inside a narrow path.

The deeper finding is that customer power is the missing link between competition and fair treatment. Seller count is a weak measure. The stronger question is: what could this person do today if the firm raised the price, denied service, or treated them worse?

## Follow one customer across firms

A customer gets a higher price or worse service and considers switching. The other firm is farther away, asks for more paperwork, has a weaker network, or uses a different risk screen. The customer stays—not because the first firm served them well, but because leaving costs too much.

The same pattern can appear online. A search tool may show alternatives, but only if the customer has time, data, confidence, and enough information to tell whether the offers are truly comparable. A lower advertised price that carries a higher deposit or weaker service is not a clean substitute.

```text
customer group + real ability to leave
  -> firm’s incentive to compete or discriminate
  -> price, service, treatment, denial, or access
  -> household cost and trust
  -> switching, complaint, regulation, or withdrawal
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| An NBER historical study finds nondiscrimination increased in the least competitive markets. | Competitive pressure can change treatment. | The historical setting is not a current universal estimate. |
| The study finds higher prices at nondiscriminatory firms in its sample. | Fairer treatment and lower price do not always arrive together. | Price is not the same as total service value. |
| NBER finds retail chains often charge similar prices across stores with different customers and competition. | One posted price can weigh differently by income and access. | The price does not reveal each customer’s alternatives. |
| NBER finds online search and some FinTech lending can narrow observed price gaps. | Better information can improve bargaining power. | Tools may not work equally for every customer. |

Sources: [NBER competition and discrimination](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33547), [NBER uniform retail pricing](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23996), [NBER consumer lending](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25943), and [NBER internet car pricing](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8668).

## The deeper finding

Competition is experienced as an exit route. If a customer can leave at low cost, a firm has reason to retain them through price and treatment. If leaving means losing a local branch, restarting an application, traveling farther, explaining a disability again, or risking a new denial, the customer’s threat is weak.

This also changes how to read a complaint pattern. A group may complain less because it is treated better—or because it expects no remedy and has nowhere else to go. A market with low switching can hide harm inside silence.

## What remains unknown

The studies do not follow a current US customer across firms, prices, service quality, complaints, denials, switches, and repeat treatment. They do not fully measure time, transport, language, disability, trust, or the cost of proving identity. We cannot yet say which customers benefit from digital comparison and which are left behind.

## Next test

Match customers across comparable firms. Record search, travel, wait, identity checks, price, quality, denial, complaint, switch, and repeat outcome by income, race, age, disability, language, and place. Measure the cost of exit before calling a market competitive.

## Reading rule

Do not say competition protects customers until you show who can compare, who can leave, and who receives the same product and treatment.
