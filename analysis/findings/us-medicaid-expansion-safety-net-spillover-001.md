# One public benefit can open the route to another

## The bounded finding

The Census CES study of the ACA Medicaid expansion finds that expanding health coverage also increased participation in SNAP and, in the individual analysis, TANF. The programs did not simply replace one another. In the preferred county-border estimate, a typical move from no non-categorical Medicaid eligibility to 138% of the poverty line reduced uninsurance by about 2.2 percentage points and increased SNAP participation by about 0.6 people per 100. In the restricted ACS, SNAP rose by 0.49 percentage points.

The paper estimates that roughly one in five adults induced to gain public insurance was also induced to participate in SNAP. The strongest interpretation is not that Medicaid created new need. It is that contact with one public program can make another program visible or easier to claim, especially where eligible people were not already connected to the safety net.

```text
Medicaid eligibility expands
  -> application, information, or administrative contact
  -> SNAP/TANF becomes visible or reachable
  -> participation rises across programs
  -> [open] benefit adequacy, food security, health, debt, and trust
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a useful correction to the idea that public programs operate as isolated boxes. A household may encounter the state through health coverage and then reach food or cash assistance through the same information channel. That means a participation increase can signal improved connection to help rather than worsening hardship.

The pattern also exposes a design boundary. Medicaid expansion reduced uninsurance, but the paper still finds that many people receiving Medicaid did not receive SNAP. A shared eligibility threshold does not create a shared route. Enrollment systems, local rules, and prior unmet need still shape who converts formal eligibility into actual participation.

The effects were larger in places with lower baseline SNAP take-up and more relaxed SNAP eligibility rules. The paper did not find a clear relationship with its three-part measure of easier enrollment, so “administrative burden” remains a live mechanism rather than a fully measured explanation.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The evidence comes from contiguous border counties and a 2010–2017 policy window, not every state-policy setting or the current safety net.
- The ACS is a repeated cross-section. It does not follow the same household from Medicaid application through food security, debt, health, or later exit.
- Participation is not benefit adequacy. The study does not show whether food insecurity fell, whether medical care improved, or whether a family had more room after costs.
- The preferred EITC estimate is small and statistically imprecise; it should not be presented as a confirmed cross-program effect.
- More SNAP participation may reflect unmet eligibility being reached, not a deterioration in household income. The study’s labor-supply tests do not identify every income or work response.

## Next test

Link privacy-protected application and enrollment records across Medicaid, SNAP, and TANF for the same household. Record which program was encountered first, the time and documents required, whether an application was abandoned, the benefit amount, and later food, health, work, debt, and renewal outcomes. Compare states and counties with different online, phone, mail, and in-person routes. That would distinguish a real cross-program safety net from a set of programs that happen to report participation in the same people.
