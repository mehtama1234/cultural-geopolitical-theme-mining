# When removing medical debt repairs the record but not the illness

## The argument

“Medical debt” names several events: someone got sick, care had a price, insurance paid only part, a bill arrived, a payment failed, a collection record appeared, and the household feared the next bill. Debt relief can remove one link while leaving the illness, price, billing process, or future risk in place.

That is why relief can improve credit or reduce payments without improving average health. This is not a contradiction. It tells us what the intervention actually touched. A clean credit record is useful, but it is not the same as affordable care or a treated condition.

The timing matters. By the time a bill becomes a collection record, the household may already have borrowed from family, missed rent, delayed another appointment, or used a credit card. Removing the collection entry can stop one future penalty without returning the money or time already lost. A relief program should therefore distinguish a balance erased, a payment avoided, a record corrected, and a choice restored. They are related, but they are not the same result.

## Follow one bill

A patient needs care and cannot compare the price before treatment. The bill is larger than expected. Payments compete with rent and food, and the account reaches collections. Relief removes the collection balance and may improve access to credit. The illness remains, and the next appointment may still carry an unknown cost.

The patient may also face several bills for one episode: the hospital, physician, anesthesiologist, laboratory, ambulance, and prescription. One account can be relieved while another remains. Insurance disputes can add time and calls before the patient knows what is actually owed. A household may stop answering unfamiliar numbers, miss a financial-assistance deadline, or pay the most threatening bill first. The visible debt total can understate the work required to make the account safe.

Credit changes can reach beyond borrowing. A cleaner record may help with an apartment, a utility deposit, a car loan, or an insurance price. But those gains depend on a future application and on other debts still present. If relief improves the record but the household's income, rent, and illness remain unchanged, the family has more room in one part of the system without having escaped the underlying pressure.

```text
illness or care need → price, insurance, and billing process → bill or collection record
→ payment, credit, care, health, and trust change → relief repairs some links, not all
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Price | HBS describes wide health-care price differences and weak ability to compare value before care. | The bill can begin before a patient can judge the cost. |
| Intervention | NBER studied $169 million of debt relief for 83,401 people in two randomized experiments. | Relief can be tested rather than inferred from correlation. |
| Credit | Relief modestly improved credit access where medical debt would otherwise have been reported. | Removing a record can change future borrowing. |
| Payments | Relief caused a moderate reduction in payments on existing medical bills. | Some current cash pressure falls. |
| Health and care | NBER found no average effect on surveyed mental or physical health, health-care use, or reported financial wellness. | Credit repair is not automatic health repair. |
| Limits | The experiments do not fully show effects for people whose bills never reach collections or for different illnesses, debt sizes, incomes, and insurers. | Average results cannot settle every patient path. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 32315](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32315), [HBS on health-care price transparency](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-american-health-care-needs-its-own-sec), and [HBS on billing costs](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/electronic-health-records-were-supposed-to-cut-medical-costs-they-haven-t).

## The deeper finding

The useful unit is the exact link removed: a payment, interest charge, collection mark, or legal threat. Relief may give back borrowing room while doing nothing to prices or care quality. A program should say what it repairs and avoid claiming the rest without evidence.

There is a provider-side question too. If a hospital knows that unpaid bills can later be bought or erased, that may change its collection strategy, financial-assistance process, or willingness to negotiate. If relief reaches patients but not the price-setting or billing process, the same problem can return after the next visit. Debt relief is a repair to the aftermath; price, coverage, and care design determine how often the damage is created.

The political meaning depends on which failure is being named. Relief can be framed as a credit-market repair, a patient protection, a subsidy to providers, or a substitute for price reform. Those are different claims. A program that improves credit may be worthwhile even with no average health effect, but it should not be asked to prove that the health-care system is affordable. Keeping the claims separate makes the result easier to judge and harder to turn into a slogan.

There is an unequal ability to wait for relief. A person with savings may settle a bill before it reaches collections and receive no benefit from a collection-focused program. A person with no cash may endure months of calls and credit damage before relief arrives. The same medical event can therefore produce different measured outcomes because the household crossed a reporting threshold at a different time. Eligibility rules are part of the economic effect.

## What remains unproven

The experiments do not measure whether relief changes trust or willingness to seek needed care for the same patient. They also do not show whether other debt, time loss, untreated illness, or the next bill remained, or whether outcomes differ by race, illness, income, and insurance.

They also do not establish whether the lack of an average health effect means no one benefited. Averages can hide a patient who returned for treatment, a family that avoided a high-cost loan, or a person whose stress fell without changing a survey score. Nor do the studies show whether relief changes provider billing, the price of later care, or the chance that a patient receives a new collection notice.

## Next test

Follow patients for two years from the first bill, not only from the collection record. Join illness, care, quoted and final price, insurance, bill, relief, credit, housing, work, later care, and the next financial shock.

Create an episode ledger that joins every bill to the care event while allowing for separate providers and disputed charges. Randomly offer early account help, debt relief, price navigation, or both where service capacity permits. Measure money returned, collections avoided, credit applications, housing and utility access, delayed or completed care, time spent resolving bills, provider behavior, and the next twelve months of debt. Report results by the point of intervention: before care, after billing, at collections, and after credit reporting. That would show whether the best repair is relief, clearer prices, better coverage, or a change in care itself.

## Reading rule

Do not use the link between medical debt and poor health as proof that debt relief will improve health. Name the illness, bill, record, payment, credit, care, and health result separately.
