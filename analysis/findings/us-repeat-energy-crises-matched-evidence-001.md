# Repeated help is a signal, not yet a diagnosis

## Short answer

A second energy crisis can mean several different things: a short income gap, a benefit that was too small or too early, a home that wastes energy, broken equipment, a landlord who controls the repair, or a bill that stays too high. HHS already separates energy burden, restoration, and prevention in its LIHEAP performance system, which is useful. But public totals do not yet show the same household's path from first aid to next crisis. The deeper finding is that repeat assistance should open a case review, not be counted as proof that the program failed or that the household failed.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Layer | What the evidence says | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| First crisis | EIA counts unsafe temperature, skipped food or medicine, shutoff notices, and failed heating or cooling equipment. | Which event began the case and whether it had happened before. |
| Immediate help | FY24 LIHEAP helped nearly 6 million households or families and reported 279,000 restored-energy instances. | Benefit size, timing, and how long service stayed on. |
| Program measure | HHS separates energy burden, restoration, and prevention of service loss. | A household-level repeat history joining those measures. |
| Home cause | GAO finds utility allowances can leave assisted renters rent-burdened. | Whether the next crisis came from the home, allowance, income, fuel price, or payment timing. |
| Repeat need | A second request shows that something may remain unresolved. | The actual reason and the intervention that would change it. |

Sources: [HHS LIHEAP performance measures](https://stage.liheappm.acf.hhs.gov/what-are-pm/), [HHS LIHEAP data warehouse](https://stage.liheappm.acf.hhs.gov/performance-measures), [HHS FY24 LIHEAP report](https://ocsannualreport.acf.hhs.gov/annual-report-fy24/priorities-and-fy24-spotlights), [EIA 2024 energy-insecurity table](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf), and [GAO utility review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-105532).

## The connection

```text
first energy crisis
  → payment, restoration, repair, or weatherization
  → next bill and home condition
  → repeat crisis or stable service
```

The first two steps are visible in federal program records. The missing link is what changed—or did not change—between the first and second event.

## The useful surprise

Repeat assistance is not one kind of failure. It can show that the first help worked for a month but not a season, that the payment prevented a shutoff but not an unsafe temperature, or that a home problem was never repaired. Counting every repeat case together hides the fix.

## Four views

**Household:** Follow each crisis, aid date, next bill, room temperature, equipment state, food or medicine choice, and later recovery.

**Customer and resident:** Record whether the resident, utility, landlord, or program had control over the next fix.

**Money and finance:** Compare income timing, benefit size, arrears, fees, fuel price, rent, allowance, and repair cost.

**Public power:** Use repeat need to route a household toward payment help, repair, weatherization, rent support, or a different fuel and rate plan.

## What would change the finding

- A household-level history of applications, benefits, restoration, prevention, shutoff, and repeat crisis.
- The next bill and service status at 30, 90, and 180 days after aid.
- Home audit, equipment, landlord, and weatherization records joined to utility data.
- Benefit size and payment date compared with arrears, income timing, and the next due date.
- Repeat outcomes separated by renter or owner status, housing type, climate, fuel, disability, age, and place.

## Reading rule

Do not label repeat assistance as program failure or household failure until the record shows what the first intervention changed and what it left untouched.
