# When the budget reaches the dinner table

## Short answer

Food is one place where a money problem becomes a daily condition. A household may keep the lights on or a debt current by buying less food, buying different food, borrowing, or asking for help. The burden is not spread evenly: lower-income households report food insufficiency much more often than higher-income households.

The careful finding is that food is a visible outcome of limited room in the budget. We do not yet know which bill was paid first, whether credit prevented a worse food outcome, or whether food hardship later changed health or work.

## What the sources show

### 1. Food insecurity is common enough to be a system measure

USDA estimates that 13.7% of US households—18.3 million—were food insecure at some point in 2024. The more severe measure, very low food security, affected 5.4% of households, or 7.2 million. The annual rate was not statistically different from 2023.

This is not a claim that food hardship suddenly worsened in 2024. It is a measure of how many households lacked dependable access to enough food during the year.

[USDA Household Food Security in the United States in 2024](https://www.ers.usda.gov/publications/113622)

### 2. The short window shows a steep income divide

In the Federal Reserve's 2024 household survey, 7% of adults said people in their household sometimes or often did not have enough to eat in the prior month. The figure was 19% for adults with family income below $25,000 and 2% for adults with family income of $100,000 or more.

The Fed and USDA measures are not interchangeable: one asks about the prior month and the other covers conditions during the year. Their agreement on the income pattern is still useful. The same national food price does not create the same room to adjust.

[Federal Reserve income and expenses report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-income-and-expenses.htm)

### 3. Children add another layer of exposure

USDA reports that 18.4% of households with children were food insecure in 2024. In about 318,000 households with children, at least one child experienced very low food security.

The household measure does not tell us which adult changed work, borrowing, care or food purchases. It does show why family type belongs in the analysis: a household with children has more people whose needs must be protected with the same budget.

[USDA food-security key statistics](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-nutrition-assistance/food-security-in-the-us/key-statistics-graphics)

### 4. Food and medical tradeoffs can sit beside each other

The same Fed report found that 28% of adults went without some medical care in 2024 because they could not afford it. Food insufficiency and skipped care are separate survey questions. Neither proves that one caused the other.

Their proximity matters for the next test. A household with no margin may not face a single choice called “food or health.” It may move between food, medicine, rent, energy and debt as different due dates arrive.

[Federal Reserve household well-being report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-income-and-expenses.htm)

### 5. Credit may be a bridge, but the sequence is missing

The credit pass found that 15% of adults used buy now, pay later in the prior year, and 24% of users reported paying late. The rate was 40% for users with family income below $25,000. This does not show that BNPL was used for food, or that it caused food hardship.

It gives us a testable question: when a household uses credit to keep one need covered, does the next payment reduce room for food or care? The current sources do not follow that sequence.

[Federal Reserve banking and credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-banking-and-credit.htm)

## The connection

```text
price, income, energy, health, or housing shock
  -> food budget becomes harder to hold
  -> lower-quality food, less food, credit, family help, or public assistance
  -> health, work, school, stress, or political response
```

The sources support the first outcome and show related choices. They do not prove the order of the arrows for the same family. The useful finding is that food insecurity is not only a food story: it is a record of how much room a household has left after other claims arrive.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** USDA reports annual food insecurity and a subgroup result for households with children. The Fed reports one-month food insufficiency, a large income gap, skipped medical care and credit behavior.

**Inference:** Food can be an early place where limited cash, high bills and payment timing become visible in daily life.

**Unknown:** We do not know which household paid energy, rent, debt or medical bills first, whether credit prevented less food, or whether food hardship changed later health, work or political views.

## What would change the finding

- A panel that records food, energy, rent, medical and debt payments for the same households over time.
- Food hardship by credit use, insurance cost, energy burden and housing tenure.
- State and county comparisons tied to food prices, SNAP rules, school meals and utility assistance.
- Evidence on whether emergency credit prevents food hardship or merely moves it to a later month.
- Health and work outcomes after a household reports reduced food intake.

## Next test

Use Census HTOPS for a place-level comparison of recent food insufficiency, difficulty paying usual expenses, housing and energy use. Pair it with USDA state estimates and one credit measure from the Fed or New York Fed. Keep food insecurity, food insufficiency, borrowing and skipped care as separate outcomes.

## Reading rule

USDA, Fed and Census measures use different questions and time periods. The connection is a working interpretation, not a completed causal account.
