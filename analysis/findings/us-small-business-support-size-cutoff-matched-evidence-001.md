# A wider small-business rule can push the smallest firms out

## Short answer

When a US program lets larger firms qualify as “small,” the program may reach more firms while giving less room to the smallest ones. A Census-linked study finds lower revenue and higher exit for the smallest firms after size limits rose, with public contracts shifting toward larger firms.

The finding is not that larger firms are always bad recipients. It is that an eligibility change can quietly change who receives public demand. The next question is whether the shift changes worker pay, customer prices and quality, local choice, and owner households.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | US industry size standards expanded, allowing larger firms into small-business support. | Whether each program had the same rule or purpose. |
| Public demand | Government procurement contracts shifted toward larger firms after the changes. | Whether larger firms delivered better value or service. |
| Small firms | The smallest firms lost revenue and were less likely to survive. | Whether each exit caused a local service or job loss. |
| Wider effects | The study reports lower wages and patenting, with stronger effects for younger, more productive, and financially constrained firms. | The same worker's pay, hours, or later job. |
| Public value | A wider rule can expand reach while crowding out some smaller firms. | The best balance between scale, access, price, quality, and resilience. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 35703](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35703), [US Census CES Working Paper 24-28](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/CES-WP-24-28.html), [SBA small-business programs](https://www.sba.gov/contracting/government-contracting-programs).

## The connection

```text
eligibility rule changes
  -> larger firms gain access to support or contracts
  -> public money and demand move across firms
  -> smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival
  -> wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change
```

The quiet change is who gets the first chance to grow. A program can look broader while becoming less reachable for the firm with the least cash and fewest other buyers.

## Four views

**Household:** Follow owner income, debt, wealth, and family security instead of treating firm revenue as household welfare.

**Company and workers:** Separate firm exit from worker pay, hours, benefits, and the next job.

**Customer:** Check price, quality, delivery, repair, and the number of real alternatives.

**Public power:** State the goal first: lowest price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, or innovation.

## What would change the finding

- The exact program, cutoff, contract, firm, and amount received.
- Customer prices, quality, delivery time, and local alternatives after the shift.
- Worker pay, hours, benefits, and movement to another job.
- Owner household income, cash, debt, and business investment.
- Results by firm age, place, industry, race, and financial constraint.
- Evidence that larger-firm access creates enough scale or reliability to offset small-firm losses.

## Next test

Follow one changed program across two years: identify which firms gained contracts, which lost them, and what happened to prices, quality, workers, innovation, and local choice. Keep public reach and public value as separate measures.

## Reading rule

Do not call a program fair because more firms qualify. Ask which firms gained, which lost demand, and what customers and workers received afterward.
