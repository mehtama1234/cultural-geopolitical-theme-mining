# A wider small-business rule can push the smallest firms out

## The argument

When a public program lets larger firms qualify as “small,” it may reach more firms while giving less room to the smallest ones. Public contracts and demand can shift toward firms with more staff, cash, and capacity. The smallest firms may lose revenue, investment, or survival even though the program's eligible list has grown.

The key question is not whether larger firms can deliver value. It is what the program is trying to buy: low price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, innovation, or resilience. A wider rule can improve one goal and damage another.

## Follow one changed cutoff

A government raises the size limit for a support program. A larger firm now qualifies and wins a contract. A small local firm loses the work, delays hiring, and closes. Customers may get reliable delivery, or they may lose a nearby supplier. “More firms qualify” does not tell us who received demand or what the place received.

```text
eligibility rule changes → larger firms gain public demand
→ smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival
→ wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change
→ public value depends on the stated goal
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | US industry size standards expanded, allowing larger firms into small-business support. | Eligibility changes who can compete. |
| Public demand | Census-linked research finds procurement contracts shifted toward larger firms. | Public money can move across firms. |
| Small firms | The study finds the smallest firms lost revenue and were less likely to survive. | A wider rule can crowd out smaller firms. |
| Wider effects | The study reports lower wages and patenting, with stronger effects for younger, more productive, and financially constrained firms. | Firm size rules can reach work and innovation. |
| Missing customer result | The evidence does not show customer price, quality, delivery, or local alternatives. | Firm effects are not yet customer welfare. |

Sources: [NBER small-business size standards](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35703), [Census CES Working Paper 24-28](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/CES-WP-24-28.html), and [SBA small-business programs](https://www.sba.gov/contracting/government-contracting-programs).

## The deeper finding

Eligibility is a demand-allocation rule. It decides which firms get the first chance to grow, and that can alter jobs, prices, service, innovation, and local ownership. Public reach and public value are different measures.

## What remains unproven

The study does not show the exact program, contract, customer, worker, or owner household behind each effect. It also does not establish whether larger-firm access created enough scale or reliability to offset small-firm losses.

## Next test

Follow one changed program for two years. Identify firms that gained and lost contracts, then measure prices, quality, delivery, worker pay and hours, innovation, owner household cash, and local alternatives.

## Reading rule

Do not call a program fair because more firms qualify. Ask which firms gained, which lost demand, and what customers and workers received afterward.
