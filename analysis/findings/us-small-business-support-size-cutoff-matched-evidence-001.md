# When a wider small-business rule crowds out the smallest firms

## The argument

When a public program lets larger firms qualify as “small,” it can reach more firms while leaving less room for the very smallest ones. A larger company may have more staff, cash, bidding experience, and delivery capacity. It can win public work that once helped a young or local firm grow. The eligible list gets wider, but the market opportunity may become more concentrated.

The question is not whether larger firms can deliver value. It is what the program is trying to buy: low price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, innovation, or resilience. A rule can improve one goal and damage another. “More firms qualify” is not a result until we know which firms received the demand and what customers and workers received afterward.

Size is also a rough measure of capacity. Two firms with the same number of employees may have different cash, equipment, owners, contracts, and ability to survive a late government payment. A firm just below the cutoff may be growing quickly; a firm just above it may be old but fragile. A single line is easy to administer, but it can create a sharp change in opportunity where the underlying businesses are not sharply different.

## Follow one changed cutoff

A government raises a size limit. A larger firm now qualifies and wins a contract. A small local supplier loses the work, delays hiring, and closes. Customers may receive more reliable delivery—or lose a nearby repair, supplier, or specialist. The public contract has changed the shape of the local market even if the agency's immediate purchase looks successful.

The contract can affect firms that never bid. A supplier may invest in a machine, hire a worker, or learn a certification because it expects public demand. If the threshold changes, that investment may no longer pay. A new firm may decide not to enter because the largest buyer is now harder to reach. The immediate award is visible; the missing entries, delayed investment, and lost local capability are harder to count.

There is a timing problem for the smallest firm. A contract can bring revenue but also require payroll, inventory, insurance, bonding, and delivery before payment arrives. A larger firm may finance that gap more easily. If the program rewards the lowest bid without considering payment timing, the rule can favor firms with cash even when a smaller firm could provide the service at a reasonable total cost. Public demand may then look open while its working-capital test is hidden.

The customer result can go either way. A larger supplier may lower the unit price or deliver across many locations. A local firm may know the place, repair faster, keep money nearby, or provide a service that is hard to replace. If the local firm exits, customers may travel farther or wait longer even if the contract price falls. The program must define whether resilience and access are part of the value it is buying.

```text
eligibility rule changes → larger firms gain public demand → smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival
→ wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change → public value depends on the stated goal
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | US industry size standards expanded, allowing larger firms into small-business support. | A definition changes who can compete for public demand. |
| Demand | Census-linked research finds procurement contracts shifted toward larger firms. | Public money can move across firms through an eligibility change. |
| Small firms | The study finds the smallest firms lost revenue and were less likely to survive. | A wider rule can crowd out firms with less cushion. |
| Wider effects | It reports lower wages and patenting, with stronger effects for younger, more productive, and financially constrained firms. | A size rule can reach work and innovation, not just contract counts. |
| Missing customer result | The evidence does not show customer price, quality, delivery, or local alternatives. | Firm effects are not yet customer welfare. |

Sources: [NBER small-business size standards](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35703), [Census CES Working Paper 24-28](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/CES-WP-24-28.html), and [SBA small-business programs](https://www.sba.gov/contracting/government-contracting-programs).

## The deeper finding

Eligibility is a demand-allocation rule. It decides which firms get the first chance to grow, and that first chance can alter jobs, prices, service, innovation, and local ownership. Public reach and public value are different measures. A program can serve more eligible firms while making it harder for new firms to become capable competitors.

This is why cutoff design can become a market-shaping choice rather than a paperwork detail. A firm that receives early contracts may build references, staff, equipment, and credit. A firm denied that chance may never reach the scale needed to compete later. The rule can therefore affect the future supply of competitors, not only today's list of awardees. A short-term effort to improve delivery may produce a long-term market with fewer independent options.

The political dispute is often described as small versus large business, but the actual split is more precise: which public goal is being funded and who bears the tradeoff. A broader rule may support continuity and lower administrative cost. A narrower rule may support entry, local capacity, and ownership spread. Neither is automatically fair. The test is whether the distribution of contracts matches the stated reason for the program.

There is also a local household link. A small firm's revenue can become wages, owner income, nearby purchases, and a service that saves residents a trip. Losing the contract does not prove a community was harmed, but it creates channels through which a procurement rule can reach workers and customers. A public buyer should not claim only a firm-level result when its decision changes the local network around that firm.

## What remains unproven

The study does not show the exact program, contract, customer, worker, or owner household behind each effect. It does not establish whether larger-firm access created enough scale, quality, or reliability to offset small-firm losses, or whether the result differs by industry and place.

It also does not show whether the cutoff caused firms to split, merge, change reported size, or stop growing to keep eligibility. Nor does it reveal how much of the effect came from the rule itself versus procurement scoring, payment delays, financing, or industry change. Contract counts alone cannot show whether the public received a better service at a lower full cost.

## Next test

Follow one changed program for two years. Identify firms that gained and lost contracts, then measure prices, quality, delivery, worker pay and hours, innovation, owner cash, customer travel, and local alternatives. Compare the stated public goal with the actual distribution of work.

Add the timing of award, work, invoice, and payment; bonding and insurance costs; bids from firms just below and above the threshold; subcontracting; entry and exit; and customer use after the contract. Test a narrow threshold, a graduated preference, and a set-aside with working-capital support. Measure delivery reliability, total public cost, firm survival, new entry, wages, local service distance, and future competition. The key result is not the number of eligible firms but whether the program builds the kind of market it says it wants.

## Reading rule

Do not call a program fair because more firms qualify. Ask which firms gained demand, which lost it, and what customers and workers received afterward.
