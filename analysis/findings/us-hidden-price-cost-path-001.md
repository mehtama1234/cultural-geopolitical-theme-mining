# When the first price is not the final price

## The argument

A shopper often decides with the number that is easiest to see: the headline price, the monthly payment, the reward, or the promise of free delivery. The full cost may appear later, after the customer has spent time comparing, entered payment details, or become used to a recurring service. A fee may also be spread across all prices, so the customer who pays with cash helps fund the rewards received by a card user.

Price display and payment design are therefore part of the product. They change what customers can compare, how quickly they decide, and which households carry the cost. The right question is not whether every fee is unfair. It is whether the customer can see the final price, understand the trade, and choose another payment or leave before the charge becomes hard to reverse.

## Follow one ordinary purchase

A shopper sees a low monthly price for a service. At checkout, taxes and a service fee appear. The shopper pays with a rewards card, partly because the reward is visible and partly because cash feels less convenient. Months later, the service renews without a fresh decision. The shopper may still value it; another customer may have forgotten it. Meanwhile, the merchant has priced the cost of card acceptance into the goods sold to everyone.

Each step is small. Together they make “price” a moving target.

```text
headline price, reward, or monthly charge → limited comparison and attention
→ fee, shared price, or renewal appears later → purchase, cancellation, or payment method changes
→ households carry different effective costs
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Comparison | NBER clickstream evidence finds that hiding price information makes comparison harder and leads consumers to spend more than they otherwise would. | What is hidden can change a purchase before the customer weighs the full cost. |
| Payment transfer | An NBER study using US merchant and payment data estimates interchange fees transfer about $30 billion each year from cash and debit users to credit-card users. | A private reward system can redistribute cost through general prices. |
| Renewal | NBER payment-card data across ten products show a sharp rise in cancellations when replacement cards force subscribers to actively renew. | Inattention can keep a service alive when an active decision would end it. |
| Size of effect | The subscription study estimates inattention raises seller revenue by 14% to more than 200% compared with immediate cancellation after value falls below price. | The possible effect is large, but it is a model range, not a measured loss for every household. |
| Limits | A reward may provide real value, a fee may fund a real service, and a non-canceling customer may still want the product. | Design evidence does not by itself prove unfairness or harm. |

Sources: [NBER price information](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186), [NBER payment fees](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35067), and [NBER inattention and subscriptions](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31547).

## The deeper finding

The first price shapes the decision; the final price shapes the household. A customer with cash room may absorb a fee or forget a renewal. A customer living close to the edge may change payment method, skip the purchase, or carry debt. When the cost is spread through prices, the person who cannot use rewards may pay for someone else's discount without seeing the transfer.

The hidden issue is not only disclosure. It is the unequal ability to wait, compare, cancel, and choose.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not follow one household across hidden price information, payment choice, renewal, and later financial stress. It does not show whether clearer disclosure changes behavior for long, or whether firms respond by raising the visible price, reducing service, or changing rewards. A model estimate is not the same as a measured household loss.

## Next test

Choose one everyday fee or recurring service. Record the first price, final price, payment method, reward, renewal event, cancellation steps, use, and later household outcome. Compare customers with different cash room and test an all-in price shown at the beginning against the usual design.

## Reading rule

The first price is not the final price. “Did not cancel” is not the same as “did not value.”
