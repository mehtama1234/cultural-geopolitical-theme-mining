# A safe temperature can compete with food and medicine

## The argument

Energy insecurity is not simply an unpaid bill. It is the moment a household has to decide which basic need can wait.

The preliminary 2024 EIA survey counts several breaks: a household leaves home at an unhealthy temperature, gives up food or medicine to pay energy costs, receives a disconnect notice, or cannot use heating or cooling equipment. These categories can overlap, but they describe different losses. A family may keep the electricity on by skipping medicine. Another may pay the bill and still live with a broken air conditioner. A third may receive a shutoff notice before service actually stops.

This distinction matters because the fix is different in each case. A payment can prevent a shutoff. It cannot repair a furnace. A weatherization job can improve a building. It may arrive too late for a household facing a bill today. Calling all of these cases “energy burden” makes the problem sound smaller and the response less precise.

The same dollar bill can create different risks depending on the home. A well-insulated apartment with working equipment may need less energy than an older house with leaks. A renter may not be allowed to replace a window unit or fix insulation. A person using medical equipment may need power even when the bill is high. The bill is real, but it is only one part of the exposure.

## Follow the choice inside the home

Imagine a family with a high summer bill and a sick child. The family can pay the bill, buy medicine, keep enough food, or limit cooling. It cannot fully protect all four if the cash is gone. The choice may never appear as a dramatic event. It may look like a warmer room, a delayed prescription, a smaller grocery trip, or a trip to someone else's home.

That is why the room matters as much as the account. A household can be current on its bill and still be unsafe. It can also receive a shutoff notice and avoid disconnection without having enough money for the next month. The visible payment record is only one part of the story.

The household may also hide the problem by moving the body instead of fixing the home: sleeping in one room, visiting a relative, going to a public cooling site, or leaving for a few hours. Those actions can prevent the worst outcome while adding travel, care, and lost work. A home can be technically connected and still not provide safe use.

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | EIA estimates 17.55 million homes reported leaving home at an unhealthy temperature in 2024. | A bill problem can become a living-condition problem. |
| Essentials | EIA estimates 32.89 million homes reported reducing or forgoing food or medicine to pay energy costs. | Energy can compete directly with health and food. |
| Service | EIA estimates 16.19 million homes reported a disconnect or delivery-stop notice. | A notice is an immediate risk, even before service ends. |
| Equipment | EIA estimates 6.83 million homes could not use heating equipment and 8.16 million could not use air-conditioning equipment. | A payment alone may not restore a usable home. |
| Response | DOE weatherization includes energy and safety checks and can serve renters and owners. | Public help is meant to address the home as well as the bill. |

Sources: [EIA 2024 household energy insecurity table](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/hc/pdf/HC11.1_2024.pdf), [EIA 2024 RECS](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/), [DOE weatherization application](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/wap/how-apply-weatherization-assistance), and [DOE LEAD](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/scep/low-income-energy-affordability-data-lead-tool).

## The argument has limits

EIA reports the conditions separately. It does not show that every household moved through them in one sequence. The numbers can overlap, so they should not be added together as separate families. They also do not tell us the exact temperature, length of exposure, medical result, missed work, or later housing choice.

DOE describes what weatherization is intended to address. That is not the same as a measured health improvement. The honest conclusion is narrower: a safe-home problem exists at the intersection of energy cost, equipment, housing control, and health, and the current public data do not yet follow those links for the same household.

There is a control problem as well as a cost problem. The person paying the bill may not own the furnace, wiring, windows, or insulation. A landlord may receive the benefit of an upgrade while a tenant pays the energy bill. A program can identify the right repair and still fail if permission, contractor supply, or relocation time is missing.

## What this changes

The right question is not “Did the household pay the bill?” It is “What did the household give up to keep the home safe, and did the intervention remove that choice?”

That question changes how assistance is judged. A good result might be no skipped medicine, a working cooling system, a safe room temperature, fewer missed workdays, or no repeat crisis. A lower energy bill is useful, but it is not the complete result.

It also changes the time horizon. A bill credit may protect a household this week. A repair or weatherization job may protect it next summer. A health or housing result may appear later. The evaluation must report immediate safety, ongoing cost, equipment function, and repeat exposure separately so a short-term rescue is not mistaken for a durable fix.

## What remains unproven

The next test is a same-home record linking bills, indoor temperature, equipment, food and medicine choices, illness, missed work, repair, weatherization, and shutoff status before and after help. Results should be separated for renters, owners, manufactured homes, older homes, hot places, and cold places.

Install low-cost temperature and equipment logs for a voluntary panel across one hot and one cold season. Record bill due dates, payment or aid, indoor temperature, equipment failure, room used, food and medicine tradeoffs, travel to another safe place, work loss, landlord permission, repair, and repeat exposure. Compare bill credits, emergency repairs, weatherization, and no intervention. Measure safe hours at home, current balance, ongoing energy cost, health disruption, and next-season resilience separately.

## Reading rule

Do not call energy insecurity a bill problem alone. Ask what the household gave up to keep the home safe, and whether help changed that choice.
