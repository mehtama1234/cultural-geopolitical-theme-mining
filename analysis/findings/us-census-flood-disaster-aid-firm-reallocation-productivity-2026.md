# Finding: Disaster aid can change which firms survive, not just repair what was damaged

**Status:** bounded disaster, firm-reallocation, public-finance, and place-recovery finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census study follows manufacturing plants after large federally declared
floods. Within two years, plant shutdowns rose by 3.6 percentage points—more
than 30% above the pre-flood baseline—and the exits were concentrated among
the least productive plants.

The surviving plants replaced machinery and became more productive. By year six,
their productivity was more than 11% above the pre-flood baseline. New entrants
were about 14% more productive than entrants before the flood. Bank lending rose
about 3.1%, especially in small loan categories, suggesting that federal disaster
finance helped young and small firms obtain credit and buy into the reallocation.

The comparison matters: after severe floods without a federal declaration,
aggregate productivity fell about 0.73% and exit did not select firms in the same
way. The paper estimates that county income also recovered better after declared
events, but that part is not a direct household panel.

## What this adds to the broader theme atlas

This gives disaster recovery a deeper route than “money arrived”:

```text
flood shock
  -> plant damage, credit constraint, and forced exit
  -> federal declaration and financing access
  -> incumbent rebuilding, used-capital transfer, new entry, or closure
  -> productivity, jobs, income, local capacity, and household recovery [open]
```

Public spending can shape the economic structure that comes after a disaster.
It may preserve some firms, allow others to enter, and move machines toward
firms that can use them more productively. That can be good for long-run
capacity while still being painful for workers, owners, tenants, suppliers, and
communities attached to firms that close.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The cleanest estimates concern manufacturing plants, not every local business
  or household.
- The study excludes frequently flooded coastal regions; repeated-disaster and
  relocation settings may behave differently.
- Revenue-based productivity and selective exit do not measure worker welfare,
  job continuity, health, housing, displacement, or trust.
- A county-income recovery estimate does not show that every resident recovered
  or received federal help.
- The event-study comparisons and declaration/denial contrasts do not eliminate
  every concern about disaster selection, local demand, migration, or financing.
- The fiscal-return calculation is model-based and depends on estimated income
  and tax channels; it is not a guarantee that every disaster program pays for
  itself.

The next decisive artifact would follow one protected disaster episode through
plant damage, aid application, credit, closure or rebuilding, worker continuity,
capital transfer, household income, housing, health, migration, and local
political response.
