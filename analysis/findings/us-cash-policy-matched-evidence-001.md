# A cash benefit can change the household budget and the economic mood

## Short answer

The 2021 Child Tax Credit changed more than a tax form. Research links the monthly payment to household spending, rent or mortgage room, living arrangements, and consumer sentiment. The strongest political conclusion is still limited: families judged their economic situation differently, but we do not yet know whether that changed trust, party support, turnout, or voting.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Spending | NBER estimates that families spent $75 of each $100 of imputed CTC payment, including $28 on food and $31 on housing. | What each family would have cut when the payment ended. |
| Housing room | NBER finds less reported difficulty affording rent or a mortgage and less past-due housing debt among low-income parents receiving the monthly credit. | Whether the same family later moved, stayed, or borrowed. |
| Family structure | NBER finds changes in living arrangements, including fewer mothers living with a partner, rather than a simple increase in doubling up. | Whether the change was chosen, safe, lasting, or better for every family. |
| Economic mood | NBER estimates that each $1,000 lost after expiration reduced consumer sentiment by 1.7 points, with larger effects for lower-income families with multiple children; the effect lasted nearly two years. | A change in sentiment, trust, blame, turnout, or vote choice. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 31412](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31412), [NBER Working Paper 31339](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31339), [NBER Working Paper 35059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35059).

## The connection

```text
benefit starts or ends
  -> cash, food, housing, and family room change
  -> household judges its own economic position
  -> sentiment, trust, blame, or policy support changes
  -> spending, contact, turnout, or vote may change
```

The new point is that material policy can affect how people read the economy, not only how much they spend. But a person can feel worse without changing a vote, and a political judgment can change without a direct cash effect.

## Four views

**Household:** A monthly payment can protect food, rent, housing independence, debt payments, or a small buffer. Its value depends on timing, not only the yearly total.

**Company and sector:** Stores, landlords, lenders, care providers, and employers may see changes in payment timing and demand. The current studies do not identify which firm captures the change.

**Money and finance:** Separate cash received, spending, arrears, debt, savings, and stress. Sentiment is not a balance sheet.

**Public power:** Payment design, eligibility, renewal, explanation, and expiration shape how families experience government. The link to voting remains an open test.

## What would change the finding

- The same family's cash, spending, rent, food, debt, savings, work, and sentiment after the benefit ends.
- Trust in government, perceived fairness, policy knowledge, blame, political contact, turnout, and vote choice.
- News, party identity, local prices, jobs, housing, and other policy changes.
- Effects by income, family size, race, state, disability, and housing status.
- Evidence that restoring the benefit reverses sentiment or only eases the budget.

## Next test

Use household payment and survey records around a benefit start or end. Measure material room first, then economic mood, then political action. Do not use sentiment as a shortcut for voting.

## Reading rule

Do not call lower sentiment a changed vote. Do not call a cash loss only a feeling.
