# When staying with a bank becomes a household choice

## The argument

Leaving a bank can save interest or fees, but moving an account is work. Direct deposit, automatic bills, fraud protection, branch access, trusted staff, and account history all sit around the old account. Staying can be a sensible choice. It can also leave a customer with a lower rate because the cost of switching is larger than the visible gain.

The customer’s inaction is therefore not simple laziness. It is a trade between money, time, service, safety, and error risk. A customer may know another bank pays more and still decide that one missed bill or frozen account would cost more than the extra interest. Another customer may lack the cash or digital access to move. The bank receives steady funding; the customer may receive convenience instead of the best return.

The deeper finding is that competition depends on completed switching, not advertised alternatives. If accounts become part of a household’s routines, the bank gains a stable relationship and less pressure to improve. The customer’s real choice is the rate difference minus the work and risk of moving.

## Follow one account

A worker keeps an emergency fund in a savings account paying little interest. A new bank offers more, but the worker must update payroll, rent, utilities, and identity checks. She delays the move because payday is near and a failed deposit could cause a late fee. The account stays, even though the household could have used the extra interest to pay down debt or build a buffer.

The same staying behavior can protect a person from an error or show that switching is too costly. The account record alone cannot tell which.

The risk is largest when the account has to work without interruption. A missed direct deposit, rejected rent payment, fraud hold, or locked login can cost more than the interest gained by moving. That makes the decision more than a rate comparison. It is a choice between a visible return and an uncertain failure cost. The bank may learn that continuity reduces the pressure to raise its rate, while the customer may experience that same continuity as safety.

```text
bank account opens → bills, direct deposit, and habit settle around it
→ switching takes time and attention → customer accepts a rate or term
→ bank gains steady funding → household buffer and bank stability move differently
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Switching | Using account openings and closures at more than 900 banks, NBER reports only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. | Most depositors do not actively move in a given year. |
| Bank value | NBER’s model estimates depositor “sleepiness” accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value and softens competition. | Inertia can be valuable to banks. |
| Stability | The model finds sleepiness reduced default probabilities during the 2023 banking turmoil. | A stable deposit base may help banks, though this is a model result. |
| Customer groups | HBS reports 94% of depositors leave funds at the same bank each year, with lower switching among older and checking or savings customers. | Staying differs by customer and account type; the figures use related but different measures. |

Sources: [NBER Dynamic Competition for Sleepy Deposits](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34267) and [HBS on sleepy customers](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-banks-need-sleepy-customers).

## The deeper finding

Inertia is both a service feature and a market force. A familiar bank can reduce mistakes and effort, but the same familiarity can weaken the customer’s bargaining power. The household should not be judged for staying until we know what the move would have risked and what the rate gap would have bought.

The dollar difference can be small in one moment and meaningful over time. A one-percentage-point gap on $10,000 is $100 a year before tax. Staying may be reasonable if moving could interrupt a paycheck or bill. But if the move is simple and safe, the same choice leaves money with the bank and weakens the customer’s reason to demand a better deal. Switching can help one household while making a bank’s funding less stable; that wider effect is a possible connection, not a result this evidence proves.

## What remains unproven

The sources do not show the same depositor’s rate gap, fees, switching effort, service use, safety, and household budget over time. They do not establish whether staying improved or reduced financial well-being for a particular customer, or whether digital switching works equally well for older, disabled, rural, or low-income customers. They also do not show whether a bank with sleepier deposits provides better service, takes more risk, or passes any funding benefit back to customers.

## Next test

Follow depositors through a full rate cycle. Match balances and terms to notices, bill links, switching attempts, service use, fees, interest received, and later saving or debt. Record failed moves, direct-deposit problems, fraud holds, and the cost of an account error. Compare the money gained from switching with the continuity value each customer actually uses, and test whether portable bill and payroll links reduce the cost of leaving.

## Reading rule

Do not call staying lazy. Measure convenience, rate gap, switching cost, and household use of the money together.
