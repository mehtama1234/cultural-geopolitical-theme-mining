# When staying with a bank becomes a household choice

## Short answer

Leaving money in the same bank can be sensible: the account is familiar, bills are linked to it, and moving may take time or create a new risk. But the same quiet habit can let a bank pay less than a customer could earn elsewhere.

The careful finding is that customer inaction has two sides. It can reduce competition and give banks steady funding, while leaving some households with less interest. The evidence does not show the net cost for each depositor or that switching is always better.

## What the sources show

Using account openings and closures at more than 900 banks, an NBER study reports that only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. Its model estimates that depositor “sleepiness” accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value, softens competition, and reduced default probabilities during the 2023 banking turmoil in the model.

An HBS summary reports that 94% of depositors leave funds at the same bank each year, with lower switching among older and checking or savings customers. It says online account holders may be less likely to move because changing automatic payments takes time. The figures use related but not identical data and measures and should be reconciled before being combined.

The sources do not show the same depositor's rate gap, fees, switching effort, lost interest, safety, and household budget over time.

- [NBER Working Paper 34267: Dynamic Competition for Sleepy Deposits](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34267)
- [HBS: Why Banks Need Sleepy Customers](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-banks-need-sleepy-customers)

## The connection

```text
bank account opens
  -> bills, direct deposit, and habit settle around it
  -> switching takes time and attention
  -> customer accepts a lower rate or weak terms
  -> bank gains cheap, steady funding
  -> customer buffer and bank stability move together, but not equally
```

Staying can protect convenience and reduce the chance of a bad move. A bank benefit is not proof that the customer received a fair return.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A person weighs interest against automatic bills, direct deposit, access, fraud protection, service, taxes, and the risk of an account error. A small rate gap matters more on a large balance or over a long period.

**Company and sector:** Banks value deposits that remain in place and can use them as steady funding. Competition may improve rates, but a rapid shift of deposits can also make funding less stable during stress.

**Money and finance:** Count the rate gap after fees and taxes, the balance, switching time, and what the extra money would do for debt, saving, or bills. A headline rate is not the household result.

**Public power:** Deposit insurance, disclosure, account portability, open banking, and competition rules shape the cost of staying or moving. Public policy must weigh customer choice against bank resilience.

## What would change the finding

- Account balance, rate, fees, taxes, and the actual interest difference.
- Automatic payments, direct deposit, switching time, errors, and service quality.
- Account type, bank size, safety, access, and alternative products.
- Changes in deposits, lending, rates, and bank risk when switching rises.
- Differences by age, income, race, place, disability, and digital access.
- Whether the customer knew about the alternative and valued the convenience.

## Next test

Follow depositors through a full rate cycle. Match account balances and terms to notices, bill links, switching attempts, service use, fees, interest received, and later saving or debt. Compare the value of moving with the time and risk it imposed on the same household.

## Reading rule

Do not call staying lazy. Do not call a bank’s cheap funding a customer benefit. Measure the convenience, the rate gap, the switching cost, and the household use of the money.
