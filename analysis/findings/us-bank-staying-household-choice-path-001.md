# When staying with a bank becomes a household choice

## The argument

Leaving a bank can save interest or fees, but moving an account is work. Direct deposit, automatic bills, fraud protection, branch access, trusted staff, and account history all sit around the old account. Staying can be a sensible choice. It can also leave a customer with a lower rate because the cost of switching is larger than the visible gain.

The customer’s inaction is therefore not simple laziness. It is a trade between money, time, service, safety, and error risk. The bank receives steady funding; the customer may receive convenience instead of the best return. We need to measure both sides.

## What the sources establish

Using account openings and closures at more than 900 banks, NBER reports that only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. Its model estimates that depositor “sleepiness” accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value, softens competition, and reduced default probabilities during the 2023 banking turmoil in the model.

An HBS summary reports that 94% of depositors leave funds at the same bank each year, with lower switching among older and checking or savings customers. The figures use related but different data and measures, so they should not be combined as one exact rate.

These sources do not show the same depositor’s rate gap, fees, switching effort, lost interest, safety, and household budget over time.

Sources: [NBER Dynamic Competition for Sleepy Deposits](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34267) and [HBS Why Banks Need Sleepy Customers](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-banks-need-sleepy-customers).

## Follow the account

bank account opens → bills, direct deposit, and habit settle around it → switching takes time and attention → customer accepts a rate or term → bank gains steady funding → household buffer and bank stability move in different directions

Staying may prevent an account error or preserve a useful service. A bank’s cheap funding is not automatically a customer benefit. The rate gap must be compared with the work and risk of moving, and with what the extra money would do for debt, saving, or bills.

## What must stay separate

- Staying by choice and staying because switching is too hard.
- Convenience and financial return.
- A bank’s funding benefit and the customer’s benefit.
- A headline rate and the interest actually received.
- Switching access and switching success.

## Next test

Follow depositors through a full rate cycle. Match balances and terms to notices, bill links, switching attempts, service use, fees, interest received, and later saving or debt. Split results by age, income, race, place, disability, and digital access.

## Reading rule

Do not call staying lazy. Measure convenience, rate gap, switching cost, and household use of the money together.
