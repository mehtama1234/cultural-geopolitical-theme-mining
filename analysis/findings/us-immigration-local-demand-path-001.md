# When new residents change the local customer base

## The argument

New residents arrive as workers, customers, neighbors, and users of public services at the same time. A town may gain workers and new stores while also facing higher demand for homes, schools, health care, transport, and language help. The gains and costs can land on different people, and they can arrive at different speeds.

That is why “immigration helps” and “immigration hurts” are both too blunt. A restaurant may hire more workers and serve more customers. A family may find better food or child care while paying more rent or waiting longer for a clinic. A local employer may gain from a larger labor pool while a worker in a different job faces stronger competition. The useful question is where new demand lands, how quickly supply responds, and who has the power to absorb the change.

The deeper finding is that immigration is a local adjustment problem, not one national score. The same arrival can expand a business, raise a landlord’s income, fill a school, and increase pressure on a public service. A local average can hide those different paths and turn a real distribution question into a slogan.

## Follow one town through a year

A small town receives new workers and their families. A grocery store adds customers and a home-care agency fills open shifts. Rents rise because homes are scarce. A school adds language support and a clinic’s wait grows. Some long-time residents benefit from more services; others face a higher housing bill. The new residents may earn wages while paying taxes, but the town still needs time and money to expand capacity.

None of these outcomes cancels the others. They are separate links that must be measured in the place where the change occurs.

```text
new residents arrive → more workers, customers, and needs
→ firms, jobs, wages, housing, and public services respond
→ residents experience different prices, access, and belonging
→ local political demands change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Local jobs | Using US Census data from 1980 to 2000, NBER estimates each immigrant created about 1.2 local jobs for local workers, mostly in services used where they are provided. | New demand can support employment near where people live. |
| Labor and firms | Other NBER work finds immigrants can expand labor supply and competition while also starting firms and expanding labor demand. | Worker competition and new demand can happen together. |
| Prices | Recent NBER research on the post-pandemic immigration surge estimates roughly offsetting effects on inflation. | A national price result can hide different local pressures. |
| Ideas | HBS reports mixed migrant-local teams produced more socially useful business ideas than local-only teams in its study. | New residents may add knowledge as well as labor and demand. |

Sources: [NBER Working Paper 21123](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21123), [NBER Working Paper 27778](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27778), [NBER Working Paper 35168](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35168), and [HBS migrant-local teams](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/when-migrants-and-locals-innovate-together-communities-gain).

## The deeper finding

The important unit is the local bottleneck. If workers arrive faster than homes, clinics, buses, or schools can expand, the town experiences a capacity problem even while firms gain. If supply expands, the same inflow may produce more jobs and services with less price pressure. The outcome depends on what can adjust, who owns it, and who pays for the extra capacity.

## What remains unproven

The findings do not show current housing, service capacity, wages, taxes, or political response in one place. They do not establish that new residents caused a particular rent increase, wait time, or local attitude. They also do not show how outcomes differ by legal status, income, race, age, language, or neighborhood.

## Next test

Follow matched places for five years before and after a change in new-resident inflow. Join migration, customer demand, firm entry and survival, housing, wages, service wait times, tax receipts, school capacity, and local attitudes. Report who gains jobs and who carries higher costs.

## Reading rule

Do not treat immigration as one market effect. Count workers, customers, housing pressure, services, and belonging separately.
