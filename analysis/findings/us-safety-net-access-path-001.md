# When help disappears before the need does

## Short answer

A public benefit can be available on paper and still be hard to keep. In the settings studied by NBER researchers, SNAP work requirements caused many people to leave the program without raising employment. A separate study found that closing an enrollment office reduced nearby participation.

The deeper finding is that access is part of the policy. A rule, form, deadline or long trip can change household food support before the household's work changes. This does not mean every work rule has the same result, or that every person who leaves still needs the benefit.

## What the sources show

### 1. Leaving SNAP was not the same as finding a job

Gray, Leive, Prager, Pukelis and Zaki used linked SNAP and earnings records to study work requirements. They found that requirements increased exits from SNAP by 23 percentage points among incumbent participants after 18 months. They found no employment effect.

This matters because a program count can fall for two very different reasons: people may earn enough to leave, or they may lose access without earning more. The study's design helps separate those possibilities in its policy setting.

[NBER: Employed in a SNAP?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28877)

### 2. Parents also left without working more

Cook and East studied SNAP work requirements affecting parents. They found that parents did not increase work but were much less likely to receive SNAP. They identify administrative burden as the main driver of the disenrollment effect in their setting.

The result does not say that work never changes under any benefit rule. It says that removing support can be the first measured response, while the intended work response does not appear.

[NBER: The Disenrollment and Labor Supply Effects of SNAP Work Requirements](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32441)

### 3. The route to help changes participation

Bitler, Cook, East, Porter and Tiehen studied SNAP enrollment-office openings and closures in one state. They estimate that closing an office reduced participation in nearby census tracts by 7–9% over two years. The study found suggestive increases after openings.

An office is not only a building. It can be a place to learn the rule, submit a form, fix an error or complete a renewal. The result gives a concrete place-based mechanism, while still leaving open whether the change altered food security for each affected household.

[NBER: The Intersection of Place and Need](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34529)

### 4. SNAP can improve food security when people receive it

USDA's research summary says SNAP improves food security in several studies. It also reports that food security worsens before benefits arrive and improves afterward, and that past benefit increases reduced food insecurity while later decreases reduced food spending and raised food insecurity.

This is an important counterweight. The issue is not simply whether a benefit works. It is whether the people who need it can receive and keep it at the time it matters.

[USDA SNAP statistics and research](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-nutrition-assistance/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap/key-statistics-and-research)

### 5. Need does not guarantee take-up

GAO estimated that about 59% of potentially eligible food-insecure college students did not report receiving SNAP in 2020. College students face special eligibility rules, so this is not a national estimate for all households. It does show how a legal eligibility path and actual receipt can diverge.

[GAO food-insecure college students and SNAP](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-107074)

## The connection

```text
food or income need
  -> eligibility, work rule, office distance, renewal task
  -> benefit kept, lost, or never reached
  -> food, work, debt, health, or trust outcome
  -> policy change, complaint, or political response
```

The evidence supports the middle of this path: rules and office access can change participation, and the studied work requirements did not produce a matching employment increase. The final food, debt, health and political effects need a same-household test.

## What is fact, inference, and unknown

**Fact:** NBER studies find increased SNAP exits without increased employment in their studied settings. Another NBER study finds participation falls after an enrollment-office closure. USDA reports that SNAP improves food security in research studies.

**Inference:** Administrative work is a form of household cost. It can take time, travel, documents, internet access, language help or confidence, and the cost may be largest when the household is already short on food or money.

**Unknown:** We do not know which households lost food support, whether they borrowed or skipped care, or whether another source of help replaced SNAP.

## What would change the finding

- A linked household record showing benefit loss, earnings, food security and debt after a rule or office closure.
- Results separated by disability, children, race, language, rurality, internet access and distance to an office.
- A comparison of online renewal, in-person help and automatic renewal.
- Evidence that a work requirement raises stable earnings after accounting for care, travel and irregular hours.
- Measures of trust or political response after a person seeks help and cannot get it.

## Next test

Use county or census-tract participation records with office locations, food-security measures and local employment data. Start with a place and time where an office opened or closed. Treat participation loss as the first outcome; test food and work outcomes separately.

## Reading rule

Program exit is not employment, and eligibility is not receipt. The memo connects those distinctions without claiming that every public-benefit rule has one result.
