# Consultation is not the same as shared control

## The deeper finding

GAO reviewed federal-Tribal agreements for managing public lands and waters that are also ancestral territories. It identified 11 features that can strengthen shared decision-making agreements, including a commitment to seek consensus, clear roles, communication procedures, implementation plans, and dispute resolution.

But the report also shows why a signed agreement is not the same as equal control. GAO examined five agreements in depth, interviewed their signatories, and spoke with 10 additional Tribes and one Alaska Native Corporation. Officials said leadership, advocacy, relationships, and legal authority helped agreements form. The Forest Service and NOAA had fewer authorities than Interior agencies to enter some self-governance-type agreements, and agencies had not assessed their workforce capacity after major workforce changes began in 2025.

```text
ancestral land or water
  -> federal authority plus Tribal interest
  -> consultation or written agreement
  -> roles, staff, information, consensus, and dispute route
  -> decision changed, shared, or left with federal final review
  -> [open] community consent, cultural result, trust, and durable control
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a political-cultural version of the program’s recurring access problem. Voice can be formally invited while usable influence depends on authority, staffing, time, information, and a way to contest disagreement. The relationship itself can matter: GAO reports that repeated in-person work and respect for Tribal history helped build trust and made later difficult decisions more workable.

The burden is uneven too. The report says Tribal advocacy helped develop some agreements, including advocacy lasting months or years. That can be a source of power, but it can also mean that communities must spend scarce organizational time pressing agencies to recognize a role that the formal relationship is supposed to support.

## What the report does not establish

- Five selected agreements are illustrative, not a national success rate.
- Eleven recommended features do not prove that every agreement contains them or that every feature changes an outcome.
- “Shared decision-making” does not mean equal legal authority; agencies may retain final review authority.
- The report does not measure which disputed decisions changed, whether communities consented, or whether land, water, cultural, health, or economic outcomes improved.
- Trust and relationship-building are reported by interviewees; they are not a standardized longitudinal measure of community-wide trust.

## Next test

Follow named agreements through each major decision: who was notified, what information was shared, what decision rights existed, what Tribal input changed, whether disagreement triggered a dispute route, who paid the participation cost, and what happened afterward. Preserve both the formal agreement and the practical record of decisions, staffing, delays, correction, community response, and later revision.

## Reading rule

Do not call consultation shared control until a named decision shows what input changed, who had authority, what resources were available, and how disagreement was handled.
