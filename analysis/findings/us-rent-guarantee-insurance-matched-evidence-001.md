# Rent insurance may protect a home before the crisis, but access is the problem

## The argument

Rent-guarantee insurance is meant to pay a limited number of rents after an income or health shock. A model says that early protection could reduce security deposits and homelessness. The same model says broad private coverage is not financially viable: insurers would favor better-off renters, while public coverage would need to focus on people most at risk.

This creates the central tension. The person who most needs a buffer may be least able to pay a premium or pass the screen needed to get one. Insurance may protect a landlord's expected rent while the tenant still faces a premium, exclusions, an indemnity, or a collection demand after the claim.

## Follow one insured tenant

A renter buys a guarantee because a landlord requires it instead of a large deposit. Months later, illness reduces income. The provider pays the landlord under the policy, but the tenant still owes under the agreement. If the claim arrives after a cure period or court step, the payment may protect the landlord without preserving the home.

```text
income or health shock → rent cannot be paid
→ insurance claim, public aid, legal help, or no protection
→ landlord receives payment or starts a case
→ housing, credit, premium, and tenant debt change
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-shock design | NBER's model finds limited rent protection could raise welfare and reduce security deposits and homelessness. | Protection before the shock may matter. |
| Access | The model says unrestricted coverage is not financially viable; private insurers favor better-off renters and public insurers target high-risk renters. | Coverage design determines who enters. |
| Emergency aid | NBER finds rent assistance increased payment and reduced eviction concerns but had little average effect on housing stability. | Pre-shock insurance may differ from late aid, but is not proven better. |
| Court power | NBER finds legal representation reduced judgments, warrants, and completed evictions in New York City. | Insurance does not replace a legal defense. |
| Landlord choice | Insurance may pay a landlord during a tenant shock. | It could change deposits, screening, rent, and renewal. |

Sources: [NBER rent-guarantee model](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32582), [NBER rental assistance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32463), and [NBER legal representation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29836).

## The deeper finding

Insurance is not one outcome. It can reduce the landlord's loss, lower the tenant's entry deposit, preserve a lease, or create a later tenant debt. The useful comparison is not insured versus uninsured in the abstract. It is who could buy protection before the shock, what the claim paid, and whether the tenant remained housed afterward.

## What remains unproven

The model is not a measured US market result. We do not yet have real premiums, exclusions, take-up, denied claims, landlord responses, repayment, or two-year housing outcomes by tenant group.

## Next test

Follow a real pilot for two years. Compare insured, publicly supported, and uninsured renters after similar income or health shocks. Measure deposits, premiums, claims, rent, eviction filings, repayment, credit, renewal, and moves.

## Reading rule

Do not call a model a market. Ask who can buy protection before the shock and who is left with the loss.
