# Rent insurance may protect a home before the crisis, but access is the problem

## Short answer

Rent-guarantee insurance is meant to pay a limited number of rents after an income or health shock. A model finds that it could raise welfare, reduce security deposits, and lower homelessness. The same model finds that broad coverage is not financially viable: private insurers would favor better-off renters, while public coverage would need to focus on people most at risk.

Observed rental evidence gives the needed counterweight. Emergency rent aid can improve payment and reduce fear without a large average housing-stability effect; legal help can change the court case. Insurance adds protection before the crisis, but it is not yet a measured US market result.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-shock design | NBER’s model covers limited rent payments after an income or health shock and finds higher welfare and smaller deposits. | Whether a real tenant can buy or keep the policy. |
| Access | The model says unrestricted coverage is not financially viable; private insurers target better-off renters and public insurers target those at highest risk. | Premiums, exclusions, take-up, and denied claims. |
| Emergency aid | NBER finds rent assistance increased payment and reduced eviction concerns, but had little average effect on housing stability. | Whether insurance would work better before arrears. |
| Court power | NBER finds legal representation reduced judgments, warrants, and completed evictions in New York City. | Whether a policyholder receives legal help or can stay after the case. |
| Landlord choice | Insurance could pay the landlord during a tenant shock. | Whether landlords change screening, rent, deposits, or renewal decisions. |

Sources: [NBER Rent Guarantee Insurance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32582), [NBER emergency rental assistance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32463), [NBER legal representation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29836).

## The connection

```text
income or health shock
  -> rent cannot be paid
  -> insurance claim, public aid, legal help, or no protection
  -> landlord receives payment or starts a case
  -> housing, credit, and household path
```

The central problem is selection. The people who most need protection may be least able to pay a premium or pass the screen needed to make private coverage work.

## Four views

**Household:** Count premium, deposit, rent, income risk, health cost, claim, and future payment.

**Customer and resident:** Ask whether coverage protects the tenant’s home, improves lease choice, or simply helps a landlord collect.

**Money and finance:** Separate pooled risk, premium, deductible, debt, credit, and public cost.

**Public power:** Choose who is protected, when, and at what cost; do not hide distribution inside the word insurance.

## What would change the finding

- A real US product or pilot with premiums, claims, exclusions, denials, landlord response, and tenant outcomes.
- Housing and credit outcomes after a claim ends.
- Who buys coverage and who is screened out by income, credit, rent, or employment.
- Effects on deposits, rents, screening, eviction filings, and affordable-unit supply.
- A cost comparison with direct rent aid, legal help, and landlord incentives.

## Next test

Follow a pilot for two years. Compare insured, publicly supported, and uninsured renters after similar income or health shocks, and measure both prevented displacement and later payment burden.

## Reading rule

Do not call a model a market. Ask who can buy protection before the shock and who is left with the loss.
