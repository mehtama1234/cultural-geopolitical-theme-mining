# When trade policy becomes a delayed shelf price

## Short answer

A tariff does not move from a policy announcement straight to every checkout price. It can pass first through the importer, then through a retailer's supplier, margin, product size, or choice of goods. Some effects can appear quickly; others can arrive months later.

The careful finding is that trade policy can create a direct and a delayed price path. The available studies do not show one household's full bill, wage, purchase, or political response.

## What the sources show

An NBER study matching daily prices, country of origin, and tariff rates estimates about 20% retail pass-through for the 2025 tariffs and a roughly 0.7 percentage-point contribution to the all-items CPI by September 2025. Imported goods rose about twice as much as domestic goods in that study.

Another NBER study estimates that about 26% of the tariff increase passed through to consumer prices relative to less-exposed goods. About 64% of that increase was direct, while 36% came indirectly through imported inputs and higher domestic markups as imports became less competitive. The direct effect was quick; the indirect effect took about nine to twelve months.

HBS tracker work using nearly 360,000 products at five major US retailers estimates that consumers carried 43% of the tariff burden after seven months. These estimates use different products, periods, and methods and should not be added together.

## The connection

```text
trade policy
  -> importer cost
  -> supplier, retailer margin, sourcing, or product choice
  -> shelf price now or months later
  -> household trading down, buying less, or changing stores
  -> firm, worker, and voter response
```

An average pass-through is not the same as a household bill. Firms may absorb a cost, change suppliers, reduce choice, change package size, or spread an increase across goods.

## The four outside views

**Household:** A family notices the goods it buys often and compares them with wages, rent, debt, and savings. A delayed increase can be hard to connect to the policy that started it.

**Company and sector:** Importers, retailers, domestic producers, and brands decide how to share the cost and whether to change supply, quality, size, or product choice. A higher price may also fund new production.

**Money and finance:** The key account includes price, quantity, quality, margin, wages, jobs, savings, and debt. A lower price in one store may hide a smaller package or a move to a less preferred product.

**Public power:** Officials must weigh consumer cost against jobs, supply security, bargaining power, and other policy goals. Clear timing and evidence matter when the public assigns credit or blame.

## What would change the finding

- Household purchases, income, wages, product size, quality, and location.
- Importer cost, retailer margin, supplier change, domestic output, and firm entry.
- Price timing after a tariff, rollback, retaliation, or sourcing change.
- Jobs, hours, pay, investment, and competition in exposed sectors.
- Media exposure, policy knowledge, trust, blame, and voting.
- Differences by income, race, age, family type, place, and product basket.

## Next test

Follow households and products for 18 months around a tariff change. Join country of origin and tariff exposure to checkout prices, quantities, package size, wages, jobs, savings, and debt. Ask when a family noticed the change and whether it linked that cost to the policy.

## Reading rule

Do not treat a tariff rate, an importer cost, and a checkout price as the same thing. Do not call a delayed price effect a direct bill without following the product and the household.
