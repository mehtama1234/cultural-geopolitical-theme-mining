# The same income can buy a different life by place

## Short answer

A national income number can hide a local customer reality. Prices differ across the United States, especially for housing, and the gap is wider for low-income households. NBER finds that expensive places offer less real consumption to less-educated households, while the same link is much weaker for college graduates.

This does not make every low-cost place better. A place also offers jobs, services, safety, family ties, and ways to move. The careful finding is that the same paycheck does not carry the same buying power everywhere.

## What the matched evidence shows

| Part of the path | What the record shows | What it does not show |
|---|---|---|
| Local prices | BEA's 2024 state RPPs range from 110.7 in California to 86.9 in Arkansas; housing-rent RPPs range from 154.3 in California to 54.2 in West Virginia. | What one neighborhood or family actually pays. |
| Unequal exposure | NBER builds price indexes for more than 140 products and finds nearly twice the spatial spread for low-income as high-income households. | Whether the difference comes from choice, access, quality, or constraint. |
| Real consumption | NBER finds that high-school graduates and dropouts in expensive places have lower real consumption; the estimated difference between the most and least affordable commuting zones is large. | Whether price caused a move, a product change, or lower well-being. |
| Higher-income response | NBER finds little relationship between local prices and real consumption for college graduates with similar observable characteristics. | That high-income households face no cost or limit. |

Sources: [BEA Regional Price Parities](https://www.bea.gov/data/prices-inflation/regional-price-parities-state-and-metro-area), [BEA real consumer spending by state](https://www.bea.gov/data/consumer-spending/real-consumer-spending-state), [NBER Working Paper 29533](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29533).

## The connection

```text
place and income
  -> local price, housing, service, and job mix
  -> real buying power
  -> product, housing, work, or moving choice
  -> household security and local political judgment
```

The deeper point is not that one state is simply cheap or expensive. It is that local price exposure depends on what a family earns, buys, needs, and can change.

## Four views

**Household:** Count income, rent, food, transport, health, care, services, family ties, and the cost of moving.

**Company and sector:** Local prices shape wages, customer demand, store locations, labor supply, and which businesses can survive.

**Money and finance:** Real buying power is income after local prices, but a low price can come with a weaker job market or fewer services.

**Public power:** Housing supply, transit, health care, schools, wages, taxes, and local investment decide whether a place's price gap becomes a real loss.

## What would change the finding

- The same household's income, prices, purchases, housing, transport, health, job access, and moves over time.
- Product quality, service availability, safety, and family support by place.
- Moving costs and whether a worker can keep or find a job after moving.
- Results by income, education, race, age, disability, family, and tenure.
- Evidence that public services or higher wages offset the price gap for lower-income households.

## Next test

Compare similar families across two high-price and two low-price commuting zones. Follow the full basket—housing, food, transport, health, care, and work—before calling a place more or less affordable.

## Reading rule

Do not call a place cheap or expensive without naming the household group and the things it must buy.
