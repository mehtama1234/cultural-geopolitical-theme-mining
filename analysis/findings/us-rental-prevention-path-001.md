# When housing prevention buys time but not a new rent level

## Short answer

Three tools meet the same housing problem at different points. Rent aid adds money. Legal help changes a tenant's position in court. A landlord's waiting or payment plan changes the timing of the decision. Each can prevent a forced move for some households, but none proves that the rent problem has ended.

The point in time is part of the intervention. A dollar before a notice may prevent a missed payment. The same dollar after a judgment may only help with moving. A lawyer before filing may stop a case; a lawyer after a warrant may reduce the damage but not restore the home. Prevention is therefore not one outcome.

The useful question is not “did the policy work?” It is: which household, at what point, received what kind of help, and what happened after the help stopped? A household can stay housed and still carry a debt, a payment plan, a credit mark, or the same unaffordable rent.

## What the sources show

### 1. Cash can improve the immediate payment

An NBER lottery-based study of emergency rental assistance in four US cities found that aid increased rent payment and reduced concerns about eviction. It also found suggestive improvements in self-reported mental and physical health. The average effect on housing stability and financial distress was small.

The distinction is important: paying more rent is an immediate behavior result, while remaining housed and becoming less financially strained are later results. A household may use aid to pay rent and then face the next month with no buffer.

[NBER emergency rental assistance study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32463)

### 2. A lawyer can change the court result

NBER's study of New York City's legal-representation rollout found fewer possession judgments, smaller money judgments, fewer eviction warrants and fewer evictions among tenants who gained lawyers. The effect was larger in poorer places and places with more noncitizens.

Legal help changes the tenant’s position in a process. It does not necessarily change the rent, repair, income, or landlord relationship that caused the case. A court result can improve while the household remains unable to afford the next bill.

[NBER legal representation study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29836)

### 3. The landlord may already be waiting for information

NBER's landlord-ledger study finds that many tenants who fall behind later catch up and repay overdue rent. Landlords often wait before filing because they do not know whether the missed payment is temporary. The study's model says rental aid, legal delay and a filing tax help different groups, and broad policies may not prevent most evictions.

Waiting can be a form of private insurance for a tenant who will recover soon. It can also leave a landlord carrying unpaid rent and make the eventual case larger. The policy question is whose timing is being protected and who carries the cost during the wait.

[NBER nonpayment and eviction study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33155)

### 4. An eviction can widen the loss

Other NBER evidence finds that an eviction order can increase homelessness and hospital visits and reduce earnings, durable-goods spending and credit access. Longer-run results include more debt and lower credit scores.

[NBER eviction and poverty study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30382)

## The connection

```text
income, health, or rent shock
  -> missed payment or notice
  -> cash aid, legal help, landlord patience, payment plan, or no help
  -> payment, court judgment, move, or eviction
  -> later work, health, debt, credit, and housing choices
```

The sources support the fact that each tool can change one part of this sequence. They do not support one complete chain for the same household.

## What is fact, interpretation, and unknown

**Fact:** Cash aid improved rent payment and eviction concerns in the four-city study. Legal help improved several housing-court outcomes in New York City. Landlord records show that some tenants recover after falling behind. Eviction orders have later costs in selected cities.

**Interpretation:** Prevention is a timing problem as much as a money problem. Help may arrive before filing, after filing, or after a judgment; the same dollar or lawyer may have a different value at each point. A stay, a payment, a lower judgment, and a later stable home should be counted as separate outcomes.

**Unknown:** We do not yet know which mix of aid, legal help and payment plans protects the same household after assistance ends, or whether the household avoids later debt and credit damage. We also do not know whether a payment plan restores room or simply moves the arrears into future months.

## What would change the finding

- A shared record of the first shock, rent owed, application date and help received.
- Court records joined to actual moves, credit, work and health outcomes.
- A comparison of cash aid, legal help, payment plans and combinations of them.
- Results after six, twelve and twenty-four months, not only while emergency rules are active.
- Cost per prevented eviction, including landlord and public costs.

## Next test

Build a household-level timeline around the missed payment: first notice, application, legal contact, payment plan, court event, move and later record. Compare who received help early enough to change the path. Record the next rent bill, arrears, credit, work, health, and housing result after assistance ends. Report four outcomes separately: payment made, case avoided or changed, home retained, and room restored.

## Reading rule

Do not turn a payment increase into a housing-stability claim. Do not turn a court win into recovery. Keep immediate relief, prevented displacement and later household room as three separate outcomes.
