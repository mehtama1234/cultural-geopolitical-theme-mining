# When one office day changes the remote week

## The argument

One office day can add more than a commute. It can create contact with coworkers and managers that continues after everyone goes home. That contact may improve feedback, learning, output, or the decision to stay. The same day also takes time, transport money, care room, sleep, and energy from the worker.

The useful finding is therefore a trade, not a verdict about remote or office work. A shared day may help a worker who needs feedback and lives nearby. It may cost more than it gives a worker with a long trip, a disability, a child-care duty, or a health limit. The right comparison is not office versus home as labels. It is **what contact the day creates, what work it replaces, and who pays for the connection.**

## Follow one worker through the week

Consider a customer-service worker who works remotely and attends the office once a month. The trip takes two hours each way. She pays for transport and changes a care arrangement. At the office she meets a manager, hears how experienced coworkers handle difficult cases, and sits near someone she later messages for help.

The office day may raise her output over the next weeks. It may also use the only evening she had for rest or family work. A coworker living ten minutes away receives much of the same contact at a lower price. A third worker cannot attend reliably because of disability or a care schedule. The average result can improve while the rule distributes the gain and burden unevenly.

There is a second risk. Contact can help a worker do better without helping that worker advance. Managers may notice office presence more than remote output. A rule can therefore improve communication while also creating a new visibility test for promotion.

```text
remote work → fewer routine encounters → planned in-person contact
→ communication, feedback, learning, or belonging
→ output, retention, promotion, and worker choice
→ commute, care, time, and household cost
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Link | Evidence | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Output | An NBER randomized trial of 248 customer-service employees found one office day a month led to 7.8% more calls per hour. | A small amount of planned contact can affect measured output in this setting. | It does not identify the best schedule for all jobs. |
| Communication | The trial found 36 extra minutes of coworker communication after office visits, more manager feedback, and 11 percentage points more contact among assigned desk neighbors. | The likely mechanism is continuing contact, not office presence by itself. | More contact does not prove better ideas, pay, or promotion. |
| Retention | Attrition was one-third lower in the trial. | Contact may affect the decision to stay. | Staying may reflect preference, labor-market limits, or the intervention itself; it is not proof of worker welfare. |
| Remote work tradeoff | An earlier NBER experiment in a China-based call center found work from home could raise productivity and satisfaction while lowering promotion rates. | Home work can improve some outcomes and weaken visibility. | The setting is older, outside the US, and not directly comparable. |
| Proximity | NBER research on coworker proximity tests a tradeoff between short-term output and knowledge sharing. | Where people sit can change which kind of work is easier. | It does not test one office day per month. |

Sources: [NBER one-office-day trial](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35331), [NBER Chinese work-from-home experiment](https://www.nber.org/digest/sep13/evidence-chinese-experiment-working-home), and [NBER coworker proximity study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880).

## The deeper finding

Office contact is a work resource with a household price. The employer may gain feedback, knowledge sharing, and lower attrition. The worker may pay in fuel, transit, care, sleep, or lost time. The later career gain may go to the worker who is most visible rather than the worker who gained the most from the contact.

This makes “one day” a poor policy unit by itself. A monthly gathering may be valuable when the work depends on trust, learning, or a new team. A weekly requirement may add little if the same contact can be made through a planned online meeting. The value depends on what is done together and whether the result lasts after the trip.

Remote work also has a real benefit that is easy to leave out: it can widen the job market, remove a commute, make care and health needs easier to manage, and allow quiet concentration. Those gains are not proof that home work is best. They show why a fair test must count the worker's saved time as an outcome, not treat it as empty space.

## Four views of the same office day

**Worker:** What contact, learning, feedback, and visibility were gained? What commute, care, rest, or money was lost?

**Manager:** Did the day improve work that needs trust or shared problem-solving, or did it mainly make attendance visible?

**Company:** Did output, errors, service, retention, and promotion change after the contact continued online?

**Household and place:** Who pays for travel and care? Which local businesses, housing choices, and job options change when office travel returns?

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether office contact improved long-term pay or promotion for the same workers, whether an online meeting could create the same contact, or how the rule affects workers with long commutes, disability, family care, different incomes, or limited local job options. It does not settle the value of office work outside customer service.

## Next test

Follow workers for one year before and after a planned office-day rule. Record attendance, commute time and cost, care changes, output, feedback, peer help, promotion, pay, retention, preference, and household stress. Compare an office day with an online meeting designed to create the same contact. Report both the work gain and the time taken to obtain it.

## Reading rule

Do not call office contact free or remote work isolated. Measure what the shared day adds and what the trip takes from the same worker.
