# When the family and the business share one cash pool

## Short answer

For a small-business owner, a family bill and a business bill may compete for the same money. A shock can show up as lower household spending, less business investment, delayed hiring, or a child opportunity that is postponed. There is no single order that fits every owner.

The careful finding is that the owner's household is part of the business balance sheet in practice, even when the records are kept apart. The result depends on savings, credit, subsidies, and the next payment that cannot wait.

## What the sources show

### 1. Business revenue and household spending moved together

NBER linked business and owner-household account data. During the first two months of the pandemic, business revenue fell 40%, while owner consumption fell about 1.6 cents for each dollar of lost revenue.

The pandemic had subsidies and unusual limits on spending. The result does not describe every business shock.

[NBER Working Paper 28151](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28151)

### 2. A family bill can reach business investment

HBS reports linked data for about 90,000 small businesses and their owners, showing how college costs can affect household spending and business investment. This is evidence of a shared cash-flow decision, not a general rule for every family or business.

[HBS: What Paying for College Can Do to a Family Business](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/what-paying-for-college-can-do-to-family-business)

### 3. The tradeoff can differ by family resources

An NBER experiment outside the United States found that a liquidity increase changed spending on children's education and business investment, with different results by parent literacy. It is a mechanism comparison, not direct US evidence.

[NBER Working Paper 29816](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29816)

## The connection

```text
business or family cash-flow shock
  -> owner chooses which payment or investment to protect
  -> household spending, business capital, or children's opportunity changes
  -> hiring, local service, family wealth, or business survival may change
```

The US sources support a shared cash-flow problem. The later jobs, local-service, and family-wealth effects remain open.

## The four outside views

**Household:** Keeping family spending steady can protect food, care, housing, or education. It can also leave less money to keep the business ready for the next month.

**Company and sector:** Cutting investment or hiring may keep a firm alive, but can reduce service, quality, or local work. The sensible short-term choice may carry a later cost.

**Money and finance:** Savings, credit limits, interest, subsidies, and lender decisions determine which side absorbs the first loss. Revenue alone does not show the owner's room.

**Public power:** Grants, tax rules, education costs, health costs, and small-business lending can change the tradeoff. The records do not show which policy reaches the owner soon enough.

## What would change the finding

- Linked business and household income, spending, debt, savings, and investment.
- The original shock, subsidy, lender response, and payment timing.
- Hiring, hours, wages, closure, local prices, and customer access.
- Family education, health, housing, and care spending.
- Results by owner wealth, race, gender, industry, business size, and place.
- A comparison of owners with similar shocks but different cash buffers.

## Next test

Follow owners and their businesses for twelve months after one cash-flow shock. Record the first payment protected, the investment delayed, the borrowing used, and the later worker and household result.

## Reading rule

A business can survive by asking the family to carry the loss. A family can stay stable by asking the business to carry it. Show which side paid first and what happened next.
