# Equal data access can open the market and weaken the data supply

## The argument

When a platform controls data that sellers need, equal access may help outside sellers compete. But if data sharing is cheap or compulsory, the platform may have less reason to gather, clean, and update the data. Customers can then face a trade: more sellers and less gatekeeper power, but stale data, longer search, or worse matches.

This is not a choice between sharing data and protecting the platform. The useful questions are who gets access, what it costs, how accurate the data are, who can correct them, what the platform still invests, and what the customer receives at the end.

The deeper finding is that data access is market design. Opening a data service changes both competition around the data and the incentive to produce the data in the first place.

## Follow one product search

A seller needs platform data to price and place a product. An equal-access rule opens the data to rivals. Entry rises, but the platform invests less in collection and updates. The data become stale or costly. Customers see more sellers but spend longer searching, receive less useful offers, or pay more for a poor match.

The reverse can also happen. The platform keeps strong incentives to improve data but uses control to favor its own products or charge rivals more. Customers get accurate results but fewer independent paths. Neither outcome can be assumed from the rule’s name.

```text
customer data
  -> platform gathers and improves a data service
  -> sellers seek access and compete
  -> access price and control change
  -> platform investment and data quality change
  -> customer price, choice, service, and privacy
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Evidence | Meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| HBS reports more sharing after privacy rules in one receipt-and-reward app. | Data supply begins with customer permission. | It does not measure seller competition. |
| NBER’s model says equal data and equal data prices can open competition. | Access can reduce a gatekeeper’s advantage. | The result is modeled, not observed in one named market. |
| The model says equal access may reduce the platform’s reason to produce or improve data. | Opening access can weaken investment. | The size of the effect depends on industry design. |
| The model says customer welfare need not rise in every case. | More competition is not the only outcome. | It does not identify the best rule for every platform. |

Sources: [NBER Data Neutrality, Data Supply, and Market Competition](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35159) and [HBS More Trust, More Data](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/more-trust-more-data-the-upside-of-privacy-laws-for-companies).

## The deeper finding

The customer result requires four measures together: seller entry, data quality, platform investment, and final price or fit. Privacy adds a fifth question: whether customers knowingly supplied the data that made the market work.

This is why a simple “open” versus “closed” debate misses the hard part. An open system can have poor data. A closed system can have excellent data and weak seller power. The public choice is about the full chain, including correction rights, access fees, updates, and who bears the cost when the data are wrong.

## What remains unknown

We do not yet have a named US platform with measured access prices, update rates, collection and maintenance spending, seller outcomes, privacy choices, and customer welfare before and after a rule. The model does not predict one automatic result for every industry.

## Next test

Choose one platform and measure data-access fees, quality, collection and update spending, seller entry, prices, variety, search time, privacy choices, and customer outcomes. Include small sellers and customers with few alternatives.

## Reading rule

Do not call equal data access good or bad until you measure seller entry, data quality, platform investment, and the customer’s final result together.
