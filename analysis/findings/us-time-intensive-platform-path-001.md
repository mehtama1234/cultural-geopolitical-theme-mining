# When a free app charges in time

## The argument

A free app can have a real price even when no money changes hands. The user pays with attention and minutes that cannot be used for sleep, care, work, a local business, or another service. That time may be worth paying: the app can provide connection, news, entertainment, or a way to reach people who matter. The question is not whether time online is good or bad. It is what the user gets, what the activity replaces, and how much choice the user has to leave.

Advertising makes the trade harder to see. A platform may increase ads, change its design, or make switching difficult. A user may stay because the service is useful, familiar, or where friends and customers are. Staying does not prove the price is low; it may show that alternatives are weak.

## Follow one evening through a household

After work, a parent opens a free app to answer a message. The app's feed and alerts keep the parent there for an hour. The time may have supplied real social contact, but it also replaced sleep, a child's conversation, or a local purchase. Another user sees more ads but leaves quickly because the service is less important. A third cannot leave because work, friends, and community information are concentrated there.

```text
free or low-money-price service → time and attention cost → staying, switching, or offline substitute
→ user value and household tradeoff → platform competition and public rule
```

## What the sources actually establish

| Step | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| User response | Two field experiments on Facebook and Instagram find users respond only a little to ad load in the study setting. | More ads do not automatically produce immediate exit. |
| Time value | The NBER paper finds that the share of a person's time devoted to an activity and the cost of that time affect which online or offline activities can replace it. | The same minute has different value for different users and households. |
| Platform structure | The paper models a possible separation of the two platforms. | Competition and user choice depend on how connected the services are. |
| User benefit | Connection, information, rest, or customer reach can be a real return for time spent. | Time is a price, not proof of harm. |
| Limits | The evidence does not show long-run well-being, work, care, sleep, household effects, or the result of an actual platform separation. | The experiments do not settle the full social cost. |

Source: [NBER Working Paper 34743](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34743).

## The deeper finding

The market price of a platform is not its whole price. A household feels the missing minute before it sees a bill. If the app is where friends, customers, news, and work contacts gather, the user may have little practical choice even when the app is technically free to leave. Platform power therefore reaches into the household calendar and the local economy, not only the advertising market.

## What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether more time on the app reduced sleep, care, work, or well-being for the same users. It does not show whether ad load, notifications, content quality, or social ties are the main reason people stay. Nor does it tell us whether a separation would create better alternatives or simply split one social network into two weaker ones.

## Next test

Follow users for six months before and after a change in ad load, alerts, ranking, or platform design. Join measured time use to content, work, care, sleep, spending, well-being, switching, offline substitutes, and exit. Compare the value of the service with the activity displaced for different household roles and incomes.

## Reading rule

Keep money price, time price, attention, use, well-being, and competition separate. A free service is not low-cost until its time cost is counted.
