# Finding: A productivity decline can come from different firm-level problems

**Status:** bounded manufacturing, firm-allocation, and measurement finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

A Census study examines U.S. manufacturing establishments and separates a
common measure of productivity dispersion into two pieces: differences in
revenue margins over costs, and differences in input cost shares across plants.
The two pieces contribute similarly to the study's measured rise in
misallocation.

The decline is broad across manufacturing, but a small number of industries
account for more than half of the aggregate decline. That means a national
productivity story can hide a concentrated set of industry-level problems.

The route is:

```text
firm conditions and market distortions
  -> margin or input-mix differences
  -> measured allocative efficiency
  -> [open] investment, jobs, pay, prices, local capacity, and household life
```

## What this adds to the atlas

The broader institutional insight is that “the economy is less efficient” is not
one explanation. Different plants may be struggling with pricing power,
financing, inputs, technology, demand, or other constraints. The remedy and the
people affected could differ by component and industry.

## Limits and counterexamples

- Measured revenue productivity and allocative efficiency are model-based,
  not direct measures of worker productivity or household welfare.
- Dispersion can reflect real technology, product, quality, or input differences,
  not only waste.
- The abstract does not identify the causal policy, technology, labor, financing,
  or market-power mechanism for each industry.
- A few industries' large contribution does not mean every plant there is
  inefficient.
- Worker pay, employment, control, consumer prices, and local outcomes are not
  observed in the bounded abstract.

## Next decisive test

Join plant-level distortion components to employment, wages, hours, worker
voice, investment, prices, customers, local suppliers, entry/exit, and later
household or place outcomes. Preserve the difference between measured
dispersion, inferred distortion, and lived consequence.
