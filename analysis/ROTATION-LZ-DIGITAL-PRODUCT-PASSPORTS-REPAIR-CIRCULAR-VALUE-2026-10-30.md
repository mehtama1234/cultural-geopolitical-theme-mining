# Rotation LZ — Digital product passports, repair rights and circular value

**Date:** 2026-10-30
**Research question:** When digital product passports and repair-rights rules make product composition, durability, repairability and lifecycle information more visible, do they create usable circular value for households, repair workers, manufacturers and places—or mainly add compliance data, fragmented identifiers and new reporting burdens without affordable repair, material recovery, worker protection or local value retention?

## Why this packet matters

Digital product passports (DPPs) sit at the intersection of industrial policy,
trade, data governance, consumer rights, repair culture and circular-economy
geography. A product record can travel across manufacturers, importers,
retailers, repairers, recyclers and regulators, but the record is only useful if
the right actor can access, interpret, correct and act on it at the moment of
purchase, repair, resale or disposal.

```text
product / material / producer / import
  -> identifier, data carrier, lifecycle and conformity information
  -> buyer, repairer, recycler, authority or platform encounter
  -> diagnosis, repair, resale, reuse, safe handling or material recovery
  -> cost, quality, labor, privacy, waste and local-value outcome
  -> durable product life, circularity and accountable supply chain
```

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| LZ1 | Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 establishes the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products framework and provides for a digital product passport architecture across product sustainability information. | EU legal framework and information-infrastructure pathway. | Usable access, data quality, interoperability, affordability, repair completion or circular outcome. |
| LZ2 | The regulation’s product-information logic includes sustainability characteristics and actors across the value chain, making data governance part of product policy rather than an optional marketing layer. | Value-chain information and accountability mechanism. | Whether small firms, importers, repairers, consumers or informal workers can obtain and use the data. |
| LZ3 | Directive (EU) 2024/1799 creates common rules promoting the repair of goods, including repair obligations or repair information in defined circumstances and a European repair platform pathway. | Formal repair-rights and service-access mechanism. | Affordable repair, available parts, local technician capacity, quality, warranty interaction or actual repair uptake. |
| LZ4 | The DPP and repair frameworks create a cross-border data and service problem: the product, owner, manufacturer, repairer and recycler may be in different jurisdictions and use different systems. | Geographical and interoperability risk. | Recognition of identifiers, liability for incorrect data, language accessibility, privacy, data portability or cross-border remedy. |
| LZ5 | Product sustainability information can improve procurement, resale and recycling decisions only if it is timely, machine-readable, durable through ownership changes and sufficiently specific to the product configuration. | Decision-support mechanism. | Consumer comprehension, repairer workflow fit, data correction, counterfeit resistance or better downstream behavior. |
| LZ6 | Repairability and durability metrics can shift value toward longer product life, but they can also become compliance scores that omit spare-part price, waiting time, tools, software locks, transport, labor conditions and the user’s actual ability to pay. | False-completion boundary for circular design. | Equal repair access, household savings, worker safety, local employment or reduced waste. |
| LZ7 | DPP data creates strategic and commercial questions over proprietary process data, supplier disclosure, cybersecurity, competitive sensitivity and control of the identifier or platform. | Data-governance and geopolitical industrial-policy mechanism. | Fair bargaining power for suppliers, independent repairers, public oversight, vendor exit or sovereignty over industrial data. |
| LZ8 | Circularity claims can shift burdens geographically: extraction and manufacturing may occur in one place, repair and reuse in another, and final dismantling or disposal in a third, often with unequal labor, environmental and regulatory protections. | Global value-chain and distributional mechanism. | Attribution of local health, labor, income, waste and ecological outcomes to the passport or repair rule. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

The passport is not merely a technical record. It can change who is authorized
to know a product’s history, who can repair it, whose labor is recognized and
which places retain value after first sale. A household may value repairability
but lack money, transport, time, a nearby technician or confidence that a repair
will preserve warranty and safety. An independent repairer may have physical
access but not diagnostic software, authenticated parts or a usable data
interface. A recycler may receive a material composition record after the most
valuable recovery decision has already been made.

Geography matters because circular systems are networks, not closed loops.
Passports can support cross-border traceability while also concentrating data
and compliance power in large manufacturers or platform providers. Local repair
and reuse ecosystems may create jobs and resilience, but only if rules recognize
their work, preserve safety and allow them to capture value. Export of used
goods, e-waste and repair labor can make a product appear circular while shifting
hazard and low-paid work to weaker jurisdictions.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A regulation, product passport, QR code, repair obligation, repair platform,
durability score, conformity record, recycled-content claim, resale listing or
recycling facility is not by itself affordable repair, longer product life,
household savings, safe work, local employment, material recovery, privacy,
interoperability or reduced environmental burden. A data-rich product can remain
unrepairable; a repair right can be unusable where parts or skills are absent;
and a circularity claim can relocate waste or labor rather than eliminate it.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Product-passport and repair-rights frameworks close
information, legal and service-design stages, not actual repair, affordability,
local circular employment, worker safety, privacy, material recovery or reduced
geographic burden.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is
**passport-to-affordable-repair-and-local-circular-value**. Follow one named
product configuration across purchase, ownership transfer, fault, repair search,
parts and software access, technician encounter, cost, repair outcome, resale,
recycling and geographic destination. Compare an independent repair route, a
manufacturer route and a non-repair or replacement route, while recording who
controls data and who captures value.
