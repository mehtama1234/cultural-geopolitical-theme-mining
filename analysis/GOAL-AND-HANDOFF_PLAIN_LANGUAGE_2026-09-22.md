# Goal and handoff — plain language

**Updated:** 2026-09-22  
**Status:** active; keep going

## The goal

Build a trustworthy, US-focused map of what is changing underneath the news.

Use many kinds of evidence to find repeated social, cultural, political,
economic, institutional, consumer, company, infrastructure, and geopolitical
patterns.

The main question is:

> When life, technology, policy, markets, or international conditions change,
> who gets real choices, who has to absorb the cost, who has to wait, and what
> happens to trust, culture, politics, public capacity, and power?

This is a broad-pattern project. It does **not** require following the same
person or household from the first problem to the final outcome. Different
sources can show different parts of the same larger pattern. We must say when
the sources cannot be joined.

## How to study each pattern

For every research pass, ask:

1. What changed?
2. Who was affected, and how much?
3. What choices were actually available?
4. What did people, firms, or institutions give up to cope?
5. Who carried the cost, risk, delay, or loss of control?
6. Did the official or private response fix the problem, or only record it?
7. What changed in trust, identity, behavior, political action, legitimacy, or
   dependence?
8. What important part is still unknown?

Every finding needs a defined source, population, place, time period,
denominator, method, alternative explanation, and stopping point.

## The data sources

The project draws from:

- household, income, spending, health, and hardship surveys;
- time-use, care, childcare, eldercare, and transportation data;
- opinion, identity, trust, belonging, voting, and civic-life surveys;
- benefit, government, court, enforcement, and administrative records;
- consumer complaints, privacy, safety, wage, and regulatory records;
- worker, employer, firm, market, ownership, and bargaining data;
- platform, digital-use, privacy, AI, moderation, and deletion records;
- housing, insurance, disaster, place, mobility, and provider data;
- energy, transmission, transit, construction, reliability, and rate records;
- defense, trade, sanctions, alliance, supply-chain, and geopolitical records;
- research papers and studies that measure a specific link or outcome.

These sources are compared carefully. They are not treated as one giant
dataset or as one person's life story.

## The current working idea

Across many areas, a repeated pattern is appearing:

> A system can offer real help, capacity, connection, or protection while
> people still lack practical control, usable alternatives, durable trust, or a
> safe way out.

This is a working idea, not a final answer. New evidence must be used to test
it, strengthen it, narrow it, or disprove it.

## When to stop chasing a source lane

Do not investigate one source family forever. Stop a lane once we can clearly
state:

- the visible change;
- who it affects;
- the practical trade-off or response;
- the wider social, cultural, political, institutional, or geopolitical
  meaning;
- a counterexample or alternative explanation; and
- the next most important missing evidence.

Then move to an under-covered theme. Return only if a new source closes a real
gap, tests the pattern, or shows an important exception.

## Where the work stands

The atlas currently contains:

- 493 validated records;
- 1,506 observations;
- 156 source packets;
- 14 themes;
- 199 cross-theme connections;
- 93 reading paths; and
- 837 finding pages and 861 published HTML pages.

The full publication gate last passed all 28 checks.

Recent passes include housing and vouchers, public systems, digital privacy and
exit, work and political influence, consumer and platform control,
infrastructure, transit, alliances, sanctions, cross-border labor remedies,
and geopolitical dependence. The latest pass added GAO's federal financial-
reporting audit: the government corrected some reporting problems while new
control weaknesses and older open recommendations remained. This strengthens
the broader idea that a visible public system is not automatically a fully
reliable or trusted one.
The latest political pass also shows that a more diverse eligible electorate
does not automatically mean more political power: eligibility, registration,
voting, representation, and policy influence are separate steps.
The newest public-program pass adds another boundary: money spent and people
served are not proof that a program solved the problem. The next useful work
is to find a named program with real outcome and follow-up evidence.
The newest political-voice pass adds another boundary: being invited to
comment on a rule does not prove people could participate equally or that
their comments shaped the final decision.
The newest place pass adds another boundary: investment and construction do
not automatically mean that local people got better jobs, housing, or security.
The newest geopolitical-infrastructure pass adds another boundary: NATO can
formally require civilian systems to support military readiness without showing
that countries implemented the requirements, that costs are fairly shared, or
that the public accepts the burden.
The follow-up rehearsal pass adds a more concrete stage: NATO and Denmark
practiced how allied forces might move, arrive, and be supplied, but that does
not prove a real crisis would go smoothly or that local people accepted the
costs.
The newest care pass makes hidden work visible: unpaid cooking, cleaning, and
care take almost four hours a day and are worth trillions in replacement value,
but that does not tell us who was exhausted, who had a choice, or who benefited.
The newest school pass shows that a bad building can interrupt learning: about
one in five districts canceled school for a facility problem, but we still do
not know the lasting effect on students or families.
The newest Native-housing pass shows that help can exist on paper while the
definition misses the problem: living with relatives may be support, crowding,
or both, and federal programs may not count it the same way communities do.
The newest disaster pass shows that recovery money can enter a place without
the government knowing whether local firms got the work or whether the local
economy recovered.
The newest cultural-place pass shows that outdoor recreation can be a huge
regional business without showing whether local people can afford or reach it,
or whether they share the benefits.
The newest household-economy pass shows that unpaid work inside homes is a major
part of the economy even when ordinary GDP leaves it out. Counting it does not
show who carries the work, whether it is chosen, or whether households can buy
safe help instead.
The newest firm-finance pass shows that small businesses do not face one credit
market: lender choice and reported risk change the route to approval, and
approval still does not prove money was received or that the firm survived.
The newest geopolitical pass shows that defense cooperation can be slowed by
review, staffing, cost-sharing communication, and internal coordination before
it becomes usable joint capability.
The newest household pass shows that people can feel okay about their own
finances while judging their local and national economies much more negatively.
That is a meaning gap, not proof of one political response.
The newest justice pass shows that someone can qualify for earlier reentry
support while missing data, limited halfway-house space, or late payments delay
the actual route back to housing, work, treatment, and community life.
The follow-up justice pass shows that fewer people under supervision does not
mean little state contact: about 3.68 million adults were still on probation or
parole at the end of 2024.
The newest international pass shows NATO turning a drone-and-sensor test into
an eight-country plan for shared Baltic coverage. That proves cooperation is
becoming more organized, not that an adversary was deterred.
The newest social pass shows that loneliness and not getting needed support are
much more common for some groups, including younger, poorer, bisexual, and
transgender adults. It shows unequal connection, not a proven cause or later
outcome.
The newest work pass shows that having a job and needing Medicaid or SNAP in
the household can happen at the same time. That does not prove employer abuse;
it shows that work, wages, health costs, hours, and family needs do not line up
into one simple measure of independence.
The newest small-business pass shows that employment can grow while revenue,
pricing power, profitability, and debt move differently. Jobs alone do not show
that a firm, its workers, or its local customers are secure.

## What to do next

Choose one under-covered source family or one later stage of an existing
pattern. Prefer evidence about what happened after an announcement or formal
route: actual use, cost, access, repair, public response, acceptance, or exit.

Use one bounded question. Add a finding only if the evidence supports it.
Then connect it to the broader themes, rebuild the site, and run:

```bash
python3 -u scripts/validate_long_term_publication_gate.py
```

Do not call the overall project finished. The goal is an ongoing, source-
traceable atlas that becomes clearer and more useful with each bounded pass.
