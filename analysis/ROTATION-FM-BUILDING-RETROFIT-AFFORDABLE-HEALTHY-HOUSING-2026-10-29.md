# Rotation FM — Building retrofit, affordable housing and healthy living

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 building-retrofit-to-affordable-healthy-housing-and-tenant-security conversion question  
**Research lane:** building retrofit / energy efficiency / housing affordability / tenants / landlords / thermal comfort / health / climate resilience / embodied carbon / workforce / skills / neighborhood change / split incentives / just transition

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers energy insecurity, extreme heat, climate insurance, eviction,
housing continuity, urban trees and climate relocation. This packet isolates the
building retrofit conversion: whether investments in insulation, ventilation,
heat pumps, cooling, efficiency and resilience reduce household burden and
improve health without raising rents, displacing tenants, creating unsafe work,
or concentrating benefits in already advantaged places.

Retrofit targets, grants, building codes, energy certificates, heat-pump sales,
emissions reductions, investment totals, property-value premiums or completed
projects are important signals. They do not by themselves establish affordable
energy, healthy indoor conditions, tenant protection, accessible delivery,
worker quality, reduced displacement or durable neighborhood resilience.

## Research question

When governments and owners finance building efficiency and climate upgrades,
under what conditions do named residents experience lower energy burden,
thermal safety, health and housing stability, while tenants, workers and
lower-income neighborhoods retain voice, affordability and a share of the
asset value created?

## Conversion chain

```text
inefficient or climate-vulnerable building / policy / grant / code
  -> assessment, finance, contractor, tenant notice and retrofit work
  -> insulation, ventilation, heating/cooling, water and resilience performance
  -> bill, comfort, health, safety, maintenance, rent and displacement outcome
  -> tenant agency, worker quality, neighborhood stability and trust
  -> affordable healthy housing, lower emissions and durable place resilience
```

The minimum unit is a named building, household, tenant, landlord, contractor,
worker, retrofit measure, bill, rent, health or climate episode, neighborhood
and time period. A retrofit completed or a building certificate is an
intermediate signal—not proof of resident benefit or housing security.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Buildings are a large climate and housing-affordability system

UNEP’s 2025–2026 Global Status Report says buildings account for 37% of global
emissions and nearly half of global material extraction, while the sector is
off track on decarbonization. It frames building action within a housing and
energy-affordability crisis and links climate action to bills, living conditions
and resilience.

Source: [UNEP, Global Status Report for Buildings and Construction 2025–2026](https://wedocs.unep.org/items/dd0a1d99-dba3-46da-93ab-ef2a26fc3c14).

### 2. Efficiency policy can reduce bills but must be targeted

IEA’s 2026 household-affordability analysis reports that lower-income households
often live in less efficient homes and cannot afford retrofits. It tracks more
than 120 affordability policies introduced in 2025, but finds only about one
third explicitly targeted households most in need. Policy coverage therefore
cannot be treated as equal benefit.

Source: [IEA, Household Energy Affordability](https://www.iea.org/reports/household-energy-affordability/executive-summary).

### 3. The landlord–tenant split incentive is a central mechanism

IEA’s affordability guidance identifies high upfront costs, administrative
complexity, the landlord–tenant split incentive, skills shortages and difficulty
reaching underserved communities. It recommends targeted support, simplified
design, local delivery and workforce development.

Source: [IEA, Designing Energy Efficiency Policies to Enhance Affordability](https://www.iea.org/reports/designing-energy-efficiency-policies-to-enhance-affordability).

### 4. Retrofit can create health and comfort benefits, but value can be captured by owners

IEA reports that efficient buildings can improve thermal, noise and light
comfort, health, safety and energy bills, while also producing sale and rental
price premiums. It warns that rent increases should remain aligned with energy
savings and identifies tenant protections as necessary so vulnerable occupants
share the benefits.

Source: [IEA, Energy efficiency and asset values](https://www.iea.org/reports/asset-values).

### 5. The construction transition has a workforce and material dimension

IEA reports growing energy-efficiency employment alongside persistent labor and
skills shortages and geographic concentration of investment. UNEP tracks both
operational emissions and material extraction. A retrofit can therefore improve
household conditions while creating worker-safety, training, supply-chain and
embodied-carbon questions.

Sources: [IEA, Energy Efficiency 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-efficiency-2025/executive-summary) and [UNEP Buildings-GSR 2025–2026](https://wedocs.unep.org/items/dd0a1d99-dba3-46da-93ab-ef2a26fc3c14).

### 6. Building performance varies by climate, place and household

IEA’s building analysis emphasizes different heating, cooling, appliance,
access and code conditions in advanced, emerging and developing economies.
Retrofit evidence must preserve climate, tenure, construction type, energy
system, household composition, disability, landlord role and local labor market
rather than treating “efficiency” as one universal intervention.

Source: [IEA, Buildings — Energy Efficiency 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-efficiency-2025/buildings).

## Bounded proposition 169

**A retrofit grant, building code, energy certificate, heat pump, insulation project, ventilation upgrade, efficiency rating, emissions reduction, investment total, property-value premium or completed renovation is not by itself affordable energy, healthy indoor conditions, thermal safety, tenant security, worker quality, climate resilience, neighborhood stability or just transition.** Buildings upgraded, bills forecast, emissions reduced, jobs counted, rents raised or property values increased do not establish that named residents receive lower total costs, safer air and temperature, accessible participation, stable tenure, health benefit or a fair share of the value created.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Housing is a lived health system.** Temperature, air, noise, light, damp, crowding and energy cost shape daily health, sleep, care and dignity.
2. **Tenure determines who can benefit.** Owners can capture asset value while tenants carry disruption, rent risk or split incentives unless protection and participation are explicit.
3. **Retrofit changes place identity.** Construction, relocation, new technologies and rent changes can strengthen a neighborhood or accelerate displacement and cultural loss.
4. **Climate and building type matter.** A measure that improves comfort in one climate or construction system may create moisture, overheating, ventilation or affordability problems elsewhere.
5. **Workers are part of the transition.** Quality depends on trained installers, safe sites, fair pay, credential access and local capability—not only equipment.
6. **Energy savings are not the sole outcome.** Families may use improved comfort to protect health, sleep, food, study, work and care; these uses require household-level observation.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A building can achieve a high energy rating while tenants face rent increases, construction disruption or inaccessible controls.
- Lower energy use can reflect improved efficiency, reduced occupancy, under-heating or energy deprivation; consumption alone is ambiguous.
- A heat pump can reduce emissions while increasing bills if tariffs, insulation, maintenance or controls are poor.
- A retrofit can reduce indoor exposure while shifting emissions, costs or material burdens elsewhere in the supply chain.
- A grant can reach owners but exclude informal tenants, undocumented residents, social housing, rural homes or small landlords.
- A job-count increase can coexist with precarious work, unsafe conditions, credential barriers or imported labor.
- A neighborhood’s improved property values can signal investment while reducing long-term affordability and cultural continuity.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for building emissions,
affordability, targeting, split incentives, health/comfort, asset-value,
workforce and geographic mechanisms. Keep the integrated building-retrofit-to-
affordable-healthy-housing-and-tenant-security outcome at **Tier 2** because
public sources rarely follow the same household and building through work,
performance, bills, rent, health, displacement and repeated seasons.

## Next test

Select one multifamily or social-housing building and one comparable non-retrofit
building. Follow tenants and workers before, during and after retrofit through
notice, consent, construction, indoor temperature/air, bills, rent, health,
maintenance, accessibility, complaints, displacement risk and one heat/cold
season. Compare owner, tenant, worker and neighborhood value capture.

## Source boundary

The packet closes building-emissions, affordability, targeting, split-incentive,
health/comfort, asset-value, workforce and geography mechanisms. It does not
close named household savings, indoor health, rent and tenure, accessible
participation, worker safety, material impacts, displacement, cultural
continuity, seasonal durability or remedy. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most retrofit sources. Investment, ratings and completed work are kept separate
from affordable, healthy and secure housing.
