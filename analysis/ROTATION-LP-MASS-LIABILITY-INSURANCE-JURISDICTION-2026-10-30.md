# Rotation LP — MASS liability, insurance and remote-centre jurisdiction

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When autonomous and remotely operated ships move navigational work from onboard crews to software, remote operators and shore infrastructure, does the emerging safety, insurance and legal architecture preserve a clear path to responsibility and remedy across ship, ROC, flag, port and coastal jurisdictions?

## Why this packet matters

The Norway, ASKO and Japan packets show that autonomous vessels are moving into trials, certification and commercial operations. They also show that a safety certificate is not a complete liability architecture. An incident can involve an autonomous planner, sensor supplier, connectivity provider, remote operator, master, owner, charterer, classification society, flag State, coastal State, port, passenger, cargo owner and ROC employer. The legal and insurance conversion must therefore follow the event across both sea and land.

```text
autonomous vessel / ROC / software / connectivity / port
  -> navigation or machinery decision
  -> collision, pollution, cargo, passenger, cyber, workplace or premises event
  -> applicable certificate, contract, insurer, flag/coastal jurisdiction and evidence
  -> compensation, investigation, learning and public legitimacy
```

## Evidence ledger

|---|---|---|---|
| LP1 | IMO’s 2026 MASS Code establishes a non-mandatory safety framework and requires a valid MASS Safety Certificate after approval. | Safety-certification architecture. | Allocation of civil liability, compensation, criminal responsibility or final jurisdiction. |
| LP2 | IMO says the master retains overall responsibility even when not onboard and that trained, certified ROCs operate under a Safety Management System. | Formal human-oversight principle and ROC governance. | How responsibility works when master, ROC operator and autonomous system disagree or when the ROC is in another State. |
| LP3 | IMO’s legal scoping work identifies the roles of master and remote operator, liability, fault, negligence, intention, terminology and carriage of certificates as issues requiring further work. | Official unresolved legal issue map. | A harmonized rule for attributing an autonomous decision or software defect. |
| LP4 | Norway’s Maritime Authority published a 2026 UNCLOS study focused on ROC operations from a State other than the flag State; it warns that effective flag-State jurisdiction and control may be compromised without adequate agreements. | Cross-border jurisdiction risk and need for bilateral/multilateral arrangements. | A final treaty interpretation, agreement model or case-specific remedy. |
| LP5 | Norway’s North Sea MoU creates an information and knowledge-exchange framework among Norway, Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom. | Regional cooperation mechanism. | Common liability rules, shared enforcement, mutual recognition or compensation fund. |

| LP7 | The International Group says ROC personnel conduct resulting in collision-related third-party liability can remain within standard mutual P&I, while ROC workplace injury and ROC premises damage are not marine P&I risks. | Sea/shore insurance boundary. | Who pays when a single event combines navigational, cyber, employment, premises and software claims. |
| LP8 | The International Group says malicious cyber risks remain within mutual P&I practice but encourages owners to assess autonomous-system exposures with Clubs and underwriters. | Cyber-insurance continuity and increased exposure signal. | Coverage for systemic software failure, supplier negligence, business interruption, data loss or correlated fleet events. |
| LP9 | IUMI says autonomous systems introduce new failure modes, unforeseen interdependencies, cyber attack, connectivity and human-system interaction risks, and require standards, certification and classification for insurability. | Insurer risk and evidence requirements. | Quantified loss frequencies, common standards, pricing or claims experience. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Liability is a form of social legibility. A passenger, fisherman, port worker, cargo owner or coastal resident needs to know not only that a vessel was certified, but whom to contact, which law applies, what evidence is preserved and whether compensation is reachable. Remote operations can increase technical capability while increasing attribution distance.

The sea/shore boundary is especially important. A collision may be a marine P&I matter, while a remote operator’s injury, a fire in the ROC, a software supplier’s negligence, a cyber incident or a port worker’s claim may fall under different policies and jurisdictions. Victims experience one event; institutions may divide it into several legal categories. This fragmentation can slow remedy and reduce public trust even when each sub-policy is technically valid.

Geography matters in at least four ways: the ship’s flag, the ROC’s location, the coastal or port State where harm occurs, and the jurisdiction where the owner, software supplier or insurer is incorporated. A Japanese vessel with a Japanese FOC is different from a Norwegian-flagged vessel monitored from another country. The emerging architecture may therefore produce a hierarchy in which large operators can assemble cross-border legal and insurance capacity while smaller domestic or island operators face uncertainty and higher costs.

The geopolitical implication is that remote operations centres become regulatory and strategic infrastructure. States may seek control over the data, personnel, certification, software and communications that make a vessel legally operable. The North Sea MoU and Norway’s UNCLOS study show that cooperation is being built, but information exchange is not the same as reciprocal jurisdiction, mutual recognition or enforceable remedy.

IMO’s legal scoping makes the attribution problem more concrete than a generic “regulatory gap.” Existing maritime concepts may not clearly capture remote operators, ROC providers, network and computer-system providers, system developers or programmers; even fault, negligence, intention and access to insurance certificates require interpretation. The Legal Committee’s incident and monetary-value methodologies create a route for evidence-based review, but they are not themselves a compensation regime for a MASS casualty.

The new evidence shows why operational records matter to liability. The US Coast Guard’s January 2026 finding treats a disabled collision-avoidance system during testing as a governance event requiring observer manning, trained operators, continuous local-authority communication and deviation reporting. This is not proof of a casualty, but it identifies the records a future investigation would need. Insurance is also moving from abstract possibility to a specialist market signal—the Shipowners’ Club reports 87 covered vessels—while the GAO’s cross-national review shows increasing local regulatory activity without a uniform answer to who counts as master or remote operator.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A MASS Safety Certificate, IMO Code, P&I pooling decision, classification notation, ROC certificate, cyber policy, North Sea MoU, liability-limit methodology or national approval is not by itself clear fault attribution, prompt compensation, universal insurance, cyber resilience, software accountability, passenger remedy, worker protection, port-community trust or harmonized cross-border enforcement. Marine insurance availability can coexist with gaps at the ROC, supplier, employment, premises, data and jurisdiction layers.

The additional boundaries are material: a safety finding can identify a control failure without proving an injury or liability outcome; a specialist policy count can show market formation without proving affordability or claim payment; and a rising volume of local approvals can show regulatory activity without harmonized authority or remedy. The atlas should therefore preserve operational records, policy wording, exclusions, claimant status and jurisdiction as separate fields rather than treating insurance availability as protection.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** MASS safety certification and marine P&I mechanisms are beginning to accommodate autonomous operations, but liability is still distributed across sea and shore risks, and cross-border ROC jurisdiction remains an active legal problem.

**Tier 2 cross-case conversion:** **MASS-certification-to-attributable-liability-insurable-sea-shore-risk-cross-border-remedy-and-public-trust**. The strongest next acquisition is a named incident or approved operating case linking vessel, autonomy level, ROC, software/connectivity supplier, flag and coastal jurisdictions, policy layers, evidence preservation, investigation, claimant access, compensation and post-incident learning.
