# Rotation AS — Public warning and cultural legibility

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** emergency information / language / disability / technology / geography / trust  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 warning-to-safe-outcome question

## Research question

When public agencies issue weather, evacuation or disaster alerts, what converts
technical delivery into understandable, trusted and actionable protection for
people across languages, disabilities, devices, rural geographies and unequal
access to transport or shelter?

## Conversion chain

```text
hazard detection
  -> alert authorization and delivery
  -> language, sensory and cultural interpretation
  -> trusted decision and feasible action
  -> evacuation, shelter, cooling, medical or protective behavior
  -> safe outcome, recovery and future trust
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| GAO-26-107680 weather safety | NWS alerts are generally English and Spanish; local authorities may provide essential evacuation information in English only; AI translation was limited in geographic coverage and lacked a full implementation plan. | delivery channels, language gap and translation project status | comprehension, trust, feasible action and safety outcome |
| GAO-26-108057 disability support | IPAWS supports images and text-to-speech, but alert accessibility varies by content, local authority and device; disability respondents reported sign-language and visual barriers. | platform capabilities and accessibility variability | whether recipients receive an accessible message and act safely |
| NOAA tsunami alert audit | IPAWS uses multiple delivery pathways including WEA, radio and television, but alert reach and system coordination remain operational concerns. | multi-channel architecture | receipt in a named at-risk place and protective behavior |
| FEMA/IPAWS structure | IPAWS validates and distributes messages through wireless, broadcast and other channels, while state and local authorities control much of the message content. | institutional responsibility chain | local message quality, language, accessibility and accountability |
| FCC/WEA rule pathway | Wireless alert systems are being expanded and modified, including multilingual support requirements with future effective dates. | formal rule direction | whether technical compliance changes real-world understanding and safety |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** hazard detection and alert issuance are observable through public
  alert systems and agency records.
- **Mechanism:** delivery channel, device, language, disability access, trust,
  social networks, transport, housing, income and local geography shape the
  conversion from warning to action.
- **Operation:** a person receives an alert, understands its urgency and can
  identify a practical next step.
- **Material consequence:** the person evacuates, shelters, obtains medicine,
  reaches cooling or assistance, or avoids exposure and injury.
- **Meaning:** an alert is culturally legible when it recognizes how people
  actually live, communicate and move. A message that arrives in the wrong
  language, inaccessible format or impossible instruction can deepen distrust
  even when the underlying forecast is accurate.
- **Power:** agencies and platforms control message authorization and format;
  households carry interpretation, transport, caregiving and compliance burden.
  The public sources reviewed here do not close this burden at recipient level.

## Bounded finding

> **An emergency alert is not emergency protection.** Delivery infrastructure,
> multilingual plans, AI translation, accessibility features and formal rules
> establish communication capacity, but do not establish that a named person
> understood the warning, had a feasible action, and reached safety.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The warning system is a social infrastructure. The relevant geography is not
only the hazard polygon; it includes language communities, disability networks,
mobile coverage, device settings, transport routes, shelters, workplaces,
schools, homes and trusted intermediaries. A warning can be technically
delivered and still fail culturally if it assumes a car, English literacy,
daytime availability, a stable address, visual access or the ability to leave
children, elders, animals or medical equipment behind.

AI translation may widen reach, but it introduces a new contestability problem:
who verifies a translated warning under time pressure, and who is responsible
when a locally important instruction is mistranslated or omitted? This is a
bounded design question, not evidence that AI translation is inherently unsafe
or beneficial.

## Counterexamples and limits

- English/Spanish dual delivery may be effective for many recipients; a
  language-availability gap is not proof of a failed warning.
- A recipient may understand an alert but be unable to evacuate because of
  disability, transport, care, housing or financial constraints.
- A multi-channel system can increase redundancy while still producing
  inconsistent local messages.
- Public alert records generally measure issued messages and system capability,
  not recipient comprehension, action or safe outcome.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: alert delivery and warning protection are
separate stages. Keep the warning-to-safe-outcome claim in Tier 2 until a named
event ledger links message content, recipient group, receipt, comprehension,
feasible action and outcome.

## Next test

Select one multilingual flash-flood or wildfire event and one disability-access
case. Compare the official message, language and format, device/channel,
community relay, transport/shelter constraints, observed protective action and
post-event outcome. Preserve cases where the warning worked as well as cases
where the barrier was operational rather than informational.
