# Rotation DZ — Ambient air pollution, place exposure and environmental-health justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** public health / geography / infrastructure / labour / environmental justice

## Why this rotation

The atlas already covers clean cooking, wildfire smoke, extreme heat and urban
public space. This rotation isolates chronic ambient exposure from traffic,
industry, ports, power generation, construction, waste burning and household
heating. It asks whether clean-air rules, monitoring, low-emission zones,
industrial transition, public transport and community data actually change
exposure and health for named places and groups.

Air-quality improvement at a regional average can coexist with persistent
hotspots, unequal exposure, displaced emissions, more expensive mobility or
limited ability to relocate. The meaningful unit is not a citywide annual mean
alone; it is the person, school, workplace, route, dwelling and neighbourhood
encountering a source over time.

## Research question

When a government or company introduces an emissions rule, industrial retrofit,
port or road project, low-emission zone, public-transport intervention, air
monitoring network or community-health programme, under what conditions does it
reduce actual exposure and health burden without shifting pollution, mobility
cost, employment risk or enforcement burden to less powerful communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
emission source / traffic / industry / fuel / geography / vulnerability
  -> regulation, investment, monitoring, enforcement and mobility policy
  -> household, school, workplace, route and neighbourhood exposure
  -> inhaled dose, symptoms, care use, missed activity, cost and adaptation
  -> health, attendance, work capacity, trust and place legitimacy
  -> correction, accountability, relocation choice, bargaining and durable equity
```

The unit must be a named source, corridor, facility, school, workplace,
neighbourhood, subgroup or household, with pollutant, time, geography and
intervention clearly identified. A sensor deployment, emission reduction or
citywide average is an intermediate signal, not proof of safer lived exposure.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Ambient pollution is a large urban-health pathway

WHO reports that an estimated 91% of urban residents breathe polluted air and
that poorly designed transport systems create air and noise pollution alongside
injury and barriers to safe activity. Transport, power, industry, construction,
waste burning and heating can produce different mixtures and exposure patterns.

This closes the broad health-risk mechanism but does not close a named source-to-
person result. The atlas must retain pollutant, route, time and source proximity.

Sources: [WHO urban-health fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/urban-health)
and [WHO air-quality monitoring guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240074446).

### 2. Exposure and vulnerability are geographically unequal

WHO notes that ambient exposure inequalities are often associated with deprived
populations living near high-traffic roads and industrial areas. The EEA finds
that PM2.5 concentrations in the poorest EU regions remained around one-third
higher than in the richest regions from 2007–2022, despite improvement in both.
The EEA also cautions that regional GDP is an imperfect proxy for individual or
neighbourhood exposure.

This creates two separate tests: whether average pollution falls, and whether
the exposure gap closes for children, older people, workers, migrants, low-income
households and people with limited ability to move or access care.

Sources: [WHO health-equity evidence](https://www.who.int/teams/environment-climate-change-and-health/air-quality-energy-and-health/sectoral-interventions/ambient-air-pollution/health-equity)
and [EEA environmental-health inequalities](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/europe-environment-2025/thematic-briefings/environment-and-human-health/environmental-health-inequalities-related-to-air-pollution).

### 3. Improvement does not mean justice has improved

The EEA reports that air quality improved in both richer and poorer EU regions,
but the relative inequality in PM2.5 exposure did not significantly decline.
Its data are population-weighted regional estimates and do not establish direct
causality between poverty and pollution. That limitation is itself important:
large spatial units can hide a school beside a road, a port-adjacent settlement,
or a worker spending hours in a high-exposure corridor.

The correct promotion claim is therefore “unequal exposure remains a live
mechanism,” not “a particular policy caused a particular health disparity”
without a named comparison and outcome bridge.

Source: [EEA income-related exposure indicator](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/indicators/income-related-environmental-inequalities-associated).

### 4. Monitoring is a governance capability, not a health outcome

WHO’s city-monitoring work supports air-quality measurement and reporting for
progress toward SDG 11.6. The EEA environmental-health atlas presents local maps
of PM2.5, NO2 and ozone and associated health indicators, while warning that
communication products are not automatically analytical or causal tools.

Community sensors, satellite data and official stations can reveal spatial
patterns, but they do not by themselves produce enforcement, cleaner air, access
to care or remedy. Measurement can also be uneven: low-income or informal areas
may have fewer stations, weaker data rights or less institutional capacity to
turn evidence into action.

Sources: [WHO city air-quality reporting guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240074446)
and [EEA pollution and health atlas](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/european-zero-pollution-dashboards/indicators/pollution-and-the-european-environment-and-health-atlas-signal-1).

### 5. Transport policy creates a mobility-and-exposure trade-off

WHO identifies poorly designed transport systems as a combined source of air
pollution, noise, injury and restricted physical activity. A low-emission zone,
road pricing, diesel restriction or bus redesign may reduce pollution in one
place while increasing travel cost, reducing job access, diverting traffic to
another neighbourhood or burdening workers whose schedules and routes cannot
change.

The exposure outcome and the mobility outcome must be measured together. A
cleaner central corridor is not a complete justice result if exposure or travel
burden is displaced.

Source: [WHO urban-health evidence](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/urban-health).

### 6. Health and economic burden are substantial, but attribution needs care

World Bank analysis describes ambient pollution from transport, industry, power
and biomass burning as a major source of health damage and economic loss. It
also emphasizes that exposure and health impacts interact with geography,
income, housing and access to services. Global burden estimates are useful for
priority-setting, but they do not establish the effect of one local intervention
on one community without a design that connects source, exposure and outcome.

Source: [World Bank global air-pollution data work](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/tracking-air-pollution-across-more-than-32-000-cities-with-new-g).

## Bounded proposition 131

**An air-quality standard, industrial retrofit, emissions reduction, low-emission
zone, public-transport project, monitoring network, port or road intervention,
clean-fuel programme or community air-quality campaign is not by itself cleaner
air, equitable health protection, safer mobility, lower care burden or
environmental justice.** Emission totals, sensor coverage, modeled annual means,
enforcement notices, cleaner vehicles, public-health messaging or citywide
compliance do not establish that named schools, workers, households and
neighbourhoods experienced lower exposure, fewer symptoms, affordable mobility,
better attendance or work continuity, equal enforcement, effective care, or
remedy when pollution was displaced or data were contested.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Clean air is a place-based right.** A city average can conceal a school,
   market, port settlement or worker route that remains exposed.
2. **Exposure is also time.** Shift work, commuting, outdoor labour, school hours
   and cooking/heating schedules create different lived doses within one address.
3. **Monitoring changes who can make a claim.** A sensor can make a hidden
   burden visible, but only institutions with standing, resources and remedy can
   turn visibility into correction.
4. **Transition redistributes cost.** Cleaner fleets and industrial rules may
   protect health while imposing vehicle, fare, job or compliance costs on groups
   with the least flexibility.
5. **Environmental identity is political.** Repeated exposure can become a story
   about neglect, sacrifice zones, race/class/geography, and whether authorities
   recognize residents as entitled to protection.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Citywide PM2.5 can decline while a port, road or industrial hotspot remains or
  worsens.
- A low-emission zone can improve central air while traffic and pollution move
  to outer neighbourhoods.
- More monitoring can increase visibility without changing emissions or health.
- An industrial closure can reduce exposure while causing concentrated job loss
  and fiscal stress; a retrofit may protect both, but only if verified.
- Regional inequality is not individual causality; poor regions can contain
  cleaner rural areas and rich regions can contain severe local hotspots.
- Health messaging can help people adapt but cannot substitute for source control,
  affordable mobility, housing quality, clinical access and enforcement.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, EEA and
World Bank sources support the ambient-health, inequality, monitoring, mobility
and burden mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because public
sources rarely follow the same source, place and subgroup through exposure,
health, mobility cost, enforcement, care and remedy.

## Next test

Compare one traffic corridor, one industrial or port-adjacent community and one
lower-exposure comparison area before and after a named intervention. Track at
least one warm and one cold season, and include schools, outdoor workers,
commuters, older people and households with limited relocation capacity.

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | standard, permit, retrofit, road/port project, sensor deployment, complaint or health alert |
| Mechanism | source, pollutant, route, wind, enforcement, fare, vehicle, job, housing or care pathway |
| Operation | named facility/corridor/school/workplace/household/subgroup and dates |
| Material consequence | PM2.5/NO2/ozone, dose, symptoms, care, attendance, work time, fare, travel and relocation |
| Meaning | fairness, sacrifice-zone identity, trust, stigma, dignity and attribution |
| Power | enforcement, data access, complaint, correction, participation, compensation and ability to refuse or relocate |

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the ambient-health-risk, spatial-inequality, monitoring,
mobility and burden mechanisms. It does not close named source-to-exposure,
exposure-to-health, displacement, affordability, enforcement or remedy outcomes.
Those remain acquisition targets.
