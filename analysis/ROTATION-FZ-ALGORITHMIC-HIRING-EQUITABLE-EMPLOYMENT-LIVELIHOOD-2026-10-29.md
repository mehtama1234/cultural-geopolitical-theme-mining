# Rotation FZ — algorithmic hiring, equitable employment and livelihood access

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological, labour, social, cultural and geographical systems

## Why this rotation exists

AI-assisted recruitment is moving from search and administration into
advertising, CV filtering, candidate ranking, video interviewing, matching and
employment decisions. The visible promise is speed, scale and consistency;
the lived outcome depends on the objective, training data, interface,
accommodation, human authority, feedback and remedy. A system can remove one
human bottleneck while reproducing historical exclusion, narrowing who sees an
opportunity, or making a decision difficult to contest.

## Research question

When an employer, recruiter, platform or public employment service uses AI to
advertise, match, screen or rank people, under what conditions does it widen
fair access to decent work and livelihood security rather than automate
exclusion, inaccessible assessment, opaque rejection, privacy loss or
unremedied discrimination?

## Conversion chain

```text
job demand / advertisement / profile / CV / interview / model score
  -> candidate encounters, understands and can access the process
  -> screening, ranking, human review, accommodation and contestability
  -> interview, offer, fair terms, retention, earnings and job quality
  -> agency, mobility, livelihood security, trust or institutional learning
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The EU AI Act treats recruitment and selection systems as high-risk because
   they can affect career prospects, livelihoods and workers' rights, and may
   perpetuate historical patterns against women, older people, disabled people
   and racial or ethnic groups. This is a legal-risk classification, not proof
   of actual bias or fair employment outcomes in a particular deployment.
2. The European Commission's employment guidance identifies targeted job
   advertising, application filtering, candidate evaluation and job matching as
   in-scope examples. A human recruiter retaining discretion does not remove
   risk when algorithmic rankings are a primary input.
3. The ILO's 2025 HRM paper argues that unclear objectives, biased or incomplete
   data and opaque programming can undermine recruitment, pay, scheduling and
   performance systems. Its practical unit is the tool's objective, data and
   programming—not the marketing claim that AI is objective.
4. The ILO's 2025 cross-continent social-dialogue case studies find that worker
   representatives can influence AI decisions in employment and working
   conditions, with stronger results where worker voice, collective capacity
   and inclusive solidarity constrain unilateral deployment.
5. The ILO's 2026 workplace discussion emphasizes that outcomes depend on the
   purpose, design and control of AI, and that worker dialogue should address
   safeguards, privacy and feedback mechanisms from design through deployment.
6. EEOC guidance confirms that existing US employment-discrimination law still
   applies when employers use AI for recruiting, screening, hiring, interview
   evaluation or workplace monitoring, including disability accommodation and
   protected-class discrimination. Legal coverage is an avenue for remedy, not
   evidence that every affected applicant can detect, challenge or reverse a
   decision.

## Bounded proposition

**An AI job advertisement, resume parser, matching engine, interview analyser,
candidate score, human-review requirement, bias audit or compliance label is
not by itself equitable employment access, fair selection, accommodation,
decent work, livelihood security or institutional trust.** A model score,
shortlist, interview, offer or rejection does not establish that named
candidates saw the opportunity, could participate on equal terms, were judged
on job-relevant information, received understandable notice, obtained human
review or had an effective remedy.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The system changes what “qualified” means in a place. Language, accent,
schooling, migration history, disability, care responsibilities, gender,
race/caste/ethnicity, internet quality, device access and local labour-market
norms can be encoded as apparently neutral proxies. A candidate may be excluded
before an interaction with a person, while an employer may treat local
informality or non-linear work history as a defect. In lower-resource or
multilingual settings, vendor data and imported benchmarks may not represent
the available workforce. Meaningful fairness therefore includes whether people
can understand the process, request accommodation, contest a decision and
retain dignity—not only whether aggregate selection rates look balanced.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Algorithmic ranking can improve consistency or reduce arbitrary discretion in
  some settings; a risk finding is not a universal failure finding.
- A group-level adverse-impact test can miss intersectional, small-group,
  proxy, sequential or accessibility harms; passing a statistical test does not
  prove job relevance or explainability.
- Removing demographic variables does not remove proxies such as postcode,
  school, employment gaps, language, name, device or browsing behaviour.
- Human review may correct errors, or may simply rubber-stamp a score without
  authority, time, evidence or training; “human in the loop” is not a closure
  measure.
- Candidate consent, notice and privacy do not guarantee the ability to refuse
  an AI interview without losing access to the job.
- Selection fairness is not job quality: an equitable offer can still produce
  unsafe work, low pay, unstable hours, discrimination or no livelihood
  security.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: AI hiring is a high-consequence access-to-work
mechanism requiring objective, data, programming, accessibility, human
authority, social dialogue and remedy controls. Keep the candidate-to-equitable
employment-and-livelihood chain as **Tier 2** until named applicant outcomes
and longitudinal job-quality evidence are available across places and groups.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | adoption, job ads, model use, legal classification and public concern | strong policy and mechanism evidence |
| Mechanism | objective, data, proxies, interface, language, accommodation, privacy and oversight | strong bounded evidence; deployment details often opaque |
| Operation | named candidate sees, accesses, completes and is scored in a hiring process | partial; examples and rights guidance exist |
| Material consequence | interview, offer, terms, earnings, retention and job safety | not closed for a general AI-hiring claim |
| Meaning | dignity, fairness, intelligibility, belonging and trust | not closed; requires candidate-informed research |
| Power | contestation, collective voice, remedy, model change and labour-market mobility | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

For a named employer or public employment service, obtain the system card,
job-ad distribution, candidate flow, structured and free-text inputs, subgroup
and intersectional selection rates, accommodation requests, human overrides,
appeals, reasons, offers, retention and job-quality follow-up. Compare people
who encountered AI screening with a credible non-AI or pre-deployment route,
while preserving privacy and not using demographic data as a proxy for a
person's identity without safeguards.

## Source boundary

The public record closes the high-consequence classification, risk mechanisms,
legal rights and worker-voice design stages. It does not establish a universal
candidate-level effect on fair access, job quality, earnings, retention,
livelihood security or durable institutional trust. Those remain acquisition
targets.
