# What appears to be changing across the 14 themes

**Checked:** 2026-09-24  
**Purpose:** plain-language orientation to the current Cultural, Social, and
Geopolitical Theme Mining atlas  
**Evidence boundary:** these are broad interpretations of diverse evidence.
They are not one survey, one population, or one universal causal theory.

For the shorter plain-language answer about what appears to be happening in
American society, see the [current societal, cultural, and political
readout](US-BROAD-CURRENT-SOCIETAL-CULTURAL-POLITICAL-READOUT_V1.md). For the
plain-language explanation of how the evidence is assembled, see the [broad
data-source map](US-BROAD-DATA-SOURCE-MAP_V1.md).

## The overall picture

The evidence points to a common tension:

> American systems are becoming more capable and more visible, but the ability
> to use them, challenge them, trust them, and leave them remains unequal.

That tension appears in household budgets, care, platforms, work, housing,
public benefits, politics, firms, infrastructure, and geopolitics. The details
are different in each theme; the recurring question is who gets a usable
option and who absorbs the cost when the formal option fails.

## What the source material is showing now

These are not broad labels imposed on the corpus. They are recurring patterns
that survive when the atlas keeps the observed stage of a process separate from
the outcome people are assumed to receive.

The detailed source-led maps below retain the counterexamples, population
boundaries, and unresolved outcomes behind each pattern. This readout is an
entry point, not a claim that every source tells the same story.

## The 14-theme readout

### 1. Household room and consumption

**What appears to be happening:** households are adapting through cheaper
goods, delayed purchases, borrowing, outside help, benefit use, and reduced
consumption. Aggregate income or spending can improve while some households
still have very little room.

**Subthemes:** prices and fees; food and energy; medical costs; debt and
liquidity; outside help; quality reduction; delayed or forgone needs.

**Key question:** which households are protecting one need by sacrificing
another, and does the adaptation last?

### 2. Time, care, and social reproduction

**What appears to be happening:** care, commuting, waiting, paperwork, and
family coordination act like hidden prices. Households and workers supply much
of the time needed to make formal services function.

**Subthemes:** eldercare; childcare; unpaid work; travel; waiting; schedule
control; multiple jobs; time for social and political participation.

**Key question:** who gets time back from convenience or public capacity, and
who quietly supplies the missing labor?

### 3. Consumer power and recourse

**What appears to be happening:** complaint routes, refunds, repairs, and
regulatory responses are becoming more visible, but formal response often
stops before verified correction, adequate payment, or a workable alternative.

**Subthemes:** hidden fees; fraud; refunds; recalls; complaint systems;
identity checks; human contact; switching; practical exit.

**Key question:** does a consumer actually recover money, access, safety, or
choice—or only receive an administrative status?

The [source-led consumer-power map](US-BROAD-CONSUMER-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates comparison friction, payment interfaces, response labels,
refund/cancellation routes, and data-driven screening from verified recovery.

### 4. Platforms, data, and attention

**What appears to be happening:** digital systems now organize work,
information, relationships, emotional support, privacy, and civic attention.
People may gain convenience while becoming more dependent on systems whose
rules they cannot control.

**Subthemes:** AI assistance; companionship; recommendation; privacy;
moderation; platform safety; attention capture; data deletion; cancellation;
digital exit.

**Key question:** can people correct, replace, or leave the system without
losing essential work, information, relationships, or safety?

The [source-led platform subtheme map](US-BROAD-PLATFORM-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates multiple evidence pathways often collapsed into generic claims about
technology: privacy control, attention and renewal friction, platform-mediated
fraud, AI choice and exit, algorithmic work, platform-dependent public life,
and the distinct stages of reporting, remedy, payment, recurrence, switching,
and practical exit.

### 5. Work, control, and bargaining

**What appears to be happening:** firms are adopting AI, measurement, and new
management tools faster than workers gain voice over how those tools affect
pay, schedules, evaluation, privacy, and job security.

**Subthemes:** AI adoption; algorithmic management; monitoring; worker voice;
representation; deactivation; wage remedies; ownership; bargaining.

**Key question:** who receives the productivity gain, and who carries the
risk of surveillance, replacement, or loss of control?

The [source-led work, control, and bargaining map](US-BROAD-WORK-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates entry, job security, automation, temporary-work credit, workplace
requests, representation, and care-shock resilience.

The [AI concern, workplace meaning, and political interpretation synthesis](projects/ai-work-control/ai-concern-management-meaning-cross-source-synthesis-v1.md)
joins public expectations with workplace meaning while keeping realized job
loss and political action open.

### 6. Care, health, and social reproduction

**What appears to be happening:** coverage and formal capacity can coexist
with delayed care, inaccessible services, prior-authorization friction,
provider shortages, medical debt, and unpaid family substitution.

**Subthemes:** disability access; health costs; treatment continuity; prior
authorization; mental health; eldercare; childcare; family care; care-worker
supply.

**Key question:** does a person receive continuous, usable care, or does the
family absorb the gap through time, money, work, and risk?

The [source-led care subtheme map](US-BROAD-CARE-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
keeps five distinct structures visible: affordability and delayed care,
gendered family-care work paths, childcare and household room,
intergenerational care capacity, and mixed health authority.

The MEPS 2024 event screen gives this interface a bounded measure: among
people with a dated office event, reported denial or prior-authorization delay
coexisted with more care delay (15.6% versus 5.8%) and medical debt (24.9%
versus 13.3%). The comparison is descriptive—the annual friction measure is
not time-linked to the event and does not show appeal, completed care, or
recovery—but it demonstrates why coverage or an encounter cannot stand in for
usable care. See the [MEPS institutional-friction record](records/us-meps-2024-event-institutional-friction.json).

### 7. Housing, place, and mobility

**What appears to be happening:** being housed is not always the same as
having secure shelter. Rent, insurance, energy, hazards, transportation, and
local service capacity combine to shape whether people can stay or move.

**Subthemes:** vouchers; lease-up; rent arrears; insurance; nonrenewal;
disaster recovery; transport; energy; neighborhood change; belonging.

**Key question:** can a household find a safe, affordable, durable place—or
only remain temporarily housed through increasingly costly adaptation?

The [source-led housing, place, and mobility map](US-BROAD-HOUSING-PLACE-MOBILITY-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates rent payment from stability, court procedure from affordability,
constrained staying from choice, risk reduction from equal relocation, and
neighborhood association from causal place effects.

### 8. Unequal exposure and status

**What appears to be happening:** the same formal rule reaches people
unequally because of income, race, disability, age, family structure, language,
documentation, geography, mobility, and institutional history.

**Subthemes:** status; discrimination; language; disability; migration;
administrative legibility; credibility; ownership; unequal recovery.

**Key question:** who has the time, money, information, safety, and status
needed to turn a formal option into a real one?

### 9. Trust, identity, and cultural meaning

**What appears to be happening:** people can experience family progress,
identity, community, and institutional distrust at the same time. Material
conditions become meaningful through dignity, blame, fairness, belonging, and
hope.

**Subthemes:** dignity; humiliation; identity; belonging; reciprocity;
religion; community support; national promise; cultural pessimism; legitimacy.

**Key question:** when conditions change, do people interpret the change as
fairness, betrayal, opportunity, exclusion, or evidence that action is useless?

The [source-led cultural and political subtheme map](US-BROAD-CULTURAL-POLITICAL-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
keeps institutional confidence, procedural fairness, belonging, cultural
distinction, and participation capacity separate rather than treating them as
one measure of polarization.

### 10. Public systems and policy feedback

**What appears to be happening:** governments can expand eligibility,
processing, data systems, and administrative reach without guaranteeing
notice, receipt, adequacy, correction, or durable security.

**Subthemes:** SNAP; Medicaid; eligibility; churn; recertification; data
matching; administrative burden; appeals; fraud controls; federalism;
policy learning.

**Key question:** does public administration turn a rule into usable
protection, and does the person’s experience change future policy or avoidance?

The [source-led public-systems map](US-BROAD-PUBLIC-SYSTEMS-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates route design, security controls, verification, correction, formal
eligibility, and disaster assistance from usable, durable protection.

The [public-help, household-room, and political-meaning synthesis](projects/us-safety-net-access/public-help-household-room-political-meaning-cross-source-synthesis-v1.md)
finds a wider pattern across administrative, household, and opinion data:
public help can be real while the route remains difficult, hardship continues,
and institutional judgment changes unevenly.

### 11. Political judgment and collective action

**What appears to be happening:** political participation is a menu rather
than a ladder. People may vote, search, complain, contact, organize, switch,
withdraw, or remain silent for different reasons.

**Subthemes:** economic judgment; party identity; efficacy; voting; local
action; protest; civic groups; information; political homes; non-use.

**Key question:** when does private pressure become political action, and when
does limited time, trust, safety, or efficacy prevent that conversion?

### 12. Firm, sector, and market power

**What appears to be happening:** firms can grow, invest, and recover while
customers, workers, suppliers, or communities receive uneven benefits and
carry different risks.

**Subthemes:** concentration; pricing; fees; ownership; AI adoption; supplier
dependence; bankruptcy; public subsidy; worker/customer trade-offs; local
business turnover.

**Key question:** when a firm succeeds, who actually gains room, security, or
choice—and who becomes more dependent on the firm?

### 13. Infrastructure, technology, and dependency

**What appears to be happening:** infrastructure is expanding through grids,
data centers, energy projects, AI capacity, logistics, and supply chains, but
public value is negotiated through cost allocation, local burden, reliability,
and replaceability.

**Subthemes:** electricity; large loads; transmission; data centers; energy
credits; critical minerals; chips; maintenance; public voice; resilience.

**Key question:** does new capacity become an affordable, reliable, and
replaceable service, or simply a larger system that creates new dependence?

The [infrastructure bargains synthesis](US-BROAD-INFRASTRUCTURE-BARGAIN-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
shows why construction, public voice, cost allocation, operation, household
benefit, and durable acceptance must be counted as separate stages.

### 14. Geopolitical and state consequences

**What appears to be happening:** states and alliances are building and
operating more capabilities, but procurement, readiness, public legitimacy,
and actual influence over other actors remain separate stages.

**Subthemes:** defense production; procurement; alliance support; host-country
consent; staffing; exercises; local security; foreign reputation; deterrence;
strategic autonomy.

**Key question:** does a capability change another actor’s choices, or does it
remain an expensive formal capacity whose strategic effect is still assumed?

The [source-led state and geopolitical subtheme map](US-BROAD-GEOPOLITICAL-SOURCE-LED-SUBTHEME-MAP_V1.md)
separates industrial capacity, dependence and bargaining, oversight, alliance
coordination, and public/partner commitment from delivery and strategic effect.

The [geopolitical and state-power synthesis](US-BROAD-GEOPOLITICAL-STATE-SUBTHEME-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
keeps procurement, readiness, legitimacy, alliance behavior, and external
response separate.

## Where the evidence comes from

The map is built from different kinds of evidence, each used for the question
it can actually answer:

| Area | Main source families | What they help measure |
|---|---|---|
| Household room | SIPP, SHED, BLS Consumer Expenditure data, MEPS, Census, Federal Reserve | spending, hardship, debt, benefits, health costs, buffers, and adaptation |
| Time and care | ATUS, NHTS, Census, BLS, HHS, GAO | caregiving, travel, waiting, work-time trade-offs, and care capacity |
| Consumer and digital life | CFPB, FTC, CPSC, platform records, privacy records, Pew, Gallup | complaints, refunds, safety, data control, attention, trust, and exit routes |
| Work and firms | BLS, Census BDS/BFS, NBER, ILO, OECD, company filings, worker cases | jobs, firm entry and exit, AI adoption, monitoring, bargaining, and market power |
| Housing and place | HUD, FEMA, ACS, insurance records, energy records, HRSA, PLACES | vouchers, disasters, housing risk, mobility, local capacity, and service access |
| Public systems and politics | SNAP/SIPP, CMS, HHS/OIG, GAO, ANES, CCES, Pew, CIRCLE | eligibility, administration, trust, identity, voting, civic action, and policy meaning |
| Infrastructure and energy | DOE, EIA, FERC, NERC, PJM, state utility records, NYPA, NYSERDA | construction, operation, rates, reliability, cost allocation, and public burden |
| Geopolitics and state power | NATO, allied government records, USAspending, SIPRI, IMF, Pew, defense records | procurement, readiness, alliance legitimacy, dependence, and external response |

Research studies from NBER, universities, the World Bank, BIS, ILO, OECD, and
other institutions provide comparison and mechanism evidence. International
records are used to explain a US exposure or test a mechanism; they are not
silently treated as US household results.

The source families are intentionally complementary. A survey can show belief
or behavior, an administrative record can show a program route, a company or
platform record can show a rule or remedy, and an infrastructure or defense
record can show operation. None of those automatically proves the next stage.

## The cross-theme subtheme

Across all 14 areas, the deepest recurring pattern is **unequal practical
optionality**.

An option becomes real only when a person, organization, community, or state
can:

1. recognize it;
2. reach it;
3. afford the effort or cost;
4. use it successfully;
5. correct a failure;
6. continue using it safely; and
7. switch or leave if necessary.

The atlas is increasingly good at measuring formal capacity, exposure,
participation, and institutional response. It is still weaker at measuring
adequacy, durability, trust, practical exit, and changed external behavior.

That gap is not a reason to narrow the project to one longitudinal dataset. It
is the central broad finding to keep testing across different people, places,
institutions, firms, and states.
