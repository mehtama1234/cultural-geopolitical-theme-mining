# Rotation EK — Community mental health, recovery, crisis response and agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** mental health / social determinants / community / work / housing / rights / peer support / crisis response

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes health information, social infrastructure, faith and trusted
intermediaries, AI healthcare, AI companions, care infrastructure and public
space. This rotation isolates the mental-health system as a cultural and place
system: how people move from distress or crisis to safety, support, recovery,
meaning, work, relationships and participation without coercion or exclusion.

Mental-health outcomes are often reduced to prevalence, service contacts,
diagnoses, beds, helpline calls or awareness campaigns. Those signals can
coexist with unaffordable care, long waits, stigma, unsafe work, housing loss,
institutional confinement, coercion, failed referral, family exhaustion or a
person’s inability to shape treatment. The unit is a person’s lived route
through a local support ecology, not an aggregate service count.

## Research question

When a government, health system, employer, school, housing provider, community
organization, crisis service, peer network or technology provider introduces
community care, prevention, workplace support, crisis response, deinstitutional-
ization, peer support or digital mental-health tools, under what conditions does
it produce safety, recovery, autonomy, participation and belonging for named
people while protecting families, workers and communities from transferred
burden and harm?

## Conversion chain

```text
distress / discrimination / violence / poverty / isolation / crisis
  -> housing, work, school, health, social-protection and mental-health policy
  -> primary care, peer, family, crisis, digital and community encounter
  -> reachable support, safety, continuity, consent and person-shaped care
  -> recovery, meaning, relationships, learning, work and civic participation
  -> agency, legal capacity, non-coercion, accountability and remedy
```

The unit must be a named person, household, peer worker, clinician, employer,
school, service, crisis episode or community with dates and geography. A budget,
bed reduction, trained worker, screening score, hotline volume, app download,
referral or awareness metric is an intermediate signal—not proof of recovery,
safety, dignity, participation or agency.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Community care is a rights, relationship and social-determinants system

WHO’s community mental-health guidance calls for person-centred and
rights-based services that address social determinants and help people regain
meaning and purpose across relationships, work, family, education, spirituality,
artistic life and politics. It emphasizes legal capacity, choices about
treatment, peer workers and lived-experience participation, and support without
coercion.

This closes the mechanism that care must be embedded in ordinary life. It does
not close whether a named person received accessible, trusted, culturally safe
and continuous support or achieved a later outcome.

Source: [WHO guidance on community mental-health services](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240025707).

### 2. Deinstitutionalization is a system transition, not a bed-reduction target

WHO’s 2026 practical guide says that deinstitutionalization requires aligned
policy, law and funding; community services; housing support; changed care
practices; supported discharge; continuity; and community participation. Its
central warning is operational: reducing institutional capacity without local
supports can separate people from communities or expose them to rights harms.

The conversion test must follow a named person before and after discharge or
service redesign, including housing, income, medication, relationships,
emergency access, choice and readmission. Bed counts alone cannot establish
community inclusion.

Source: [WHO guide to transition toward community-based mental-health and social care](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240124134).

### 3. Inequality changes both mental-health exposure and the care route

OECD’s 2025 review finds differences in mental-health status, access, care
experience and outcomes by gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, indigeneity,
migration and socioeconomic status. It examines how unequal exposure to risks,
opportunities and resources is compounded by unequal access and quality of care.

The relevant unit is therefore not a population average. It is a route audit:
who recognizes distress, who can pay or travel, whose language and identity are
legible, who is believed, who receives a culturally safe option, and who can
appeal or change providers.

Source: [OECD Understanding and addressing inequalities in mental health](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/understanding-and-addressing-inequalities-in-mental-health_56adb10f-en.html).

### 4. Work can create risk, protection and a route back to participation

WHO’s 2025 employment-sector guidance and mental-health-at-work fact sheet
recommend organizational interventions to reduce psychosocial risks, worker
participation, manager and worker mental-health literacy, reasonable
accommodations, return-to-work support and supported employment. The guidance
also stresses the meaningful involvement of workers, representatives and people
with lived experience.

This makes work a conversion site rather than just an outcome. A wellbeing
campaign or counselling benefit may coexist with unsafe workload, harassment,
precarity or retaliation. The test must observe working conditions, disclosure
choice, accommodation, absence, return, retention and remedy.

Sources: [WHO mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work?hl=en-US)
and [WHO employment-sector policy guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/b/80915).

### 5. Peer support changes authority and cultural legibility

WHO’s 2026 Thailand case documents a policy milestone recognizing people with
lived experience as peer-recovery participants in service delivery. The example
shows how lived experience can move from consultation to a formal role in
recovery-oriented care, while also illustrating that a standard or policy does
not prove broad service coverage or later wellbeing.

Peer support should therefore be evaluated for pay, training, influence, safety,
confidentiality, representation and whether it changes clinical decisions—not
just whether peer workers are counted.

Source: [WHO Thailand peer-support and recovery-oriented care](https://www.who.int/thailand/news/feature-stories/detail/transforming-mental-health-care-in-thailand-through-peer-support-by-people-with-lived-experience).

### 6. Crisis response can protect or reproduce coercion and mistrust

WHO reports that coercive restraint and seclusion can cause injury, trauma,
mistrust and damage to recovery, and connects reduction of coercion to
communication, staffing, environment, person-centred care and human-rights
practice. A crisis line, emergency department, police response or mobile team
must therefore be assessed for immediate safety and for later trust, continuity,
choice and willingness to seek help again.

Source: [WHO on moving away from coercive measures](https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/feature-stories/item/countries-move-away-from-using-coercive-measures-in-mental-health-care).

## Bounded proposition 142

**A mental-health strategy, community team, psychiatric-bed reduction, crisis
line, screening campaign, workplace programme, peer-support standard, digital
tool, referral, discharge plan or anti-stigma campaign is not by itself safety,
recovery, autonomy, dignity, participation, decent work, family resilience or
community belonging.** Budgets, beds, contacts, screenings, referrals, trained
workers, app downloads, hotline volume, reduced restraint or declared peer
roles do not establish that a named person reached culturally safe and
continuous support, retained housing and relationships, exercised legal
capacity, returned to learning or work, avoided preventable harm, or obtained
voice and remedy when the system failed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Distress is interpreted through culture.** Family, faith, language,
   gender, caste, race, disability and migration histories shape whether a
   person is believed, stigmatized, protected or criminalized.
2. **Community is an operating infrastructure.** Housing, transport, work,
   schools, peer networks, primary care and crisis response determine whether
   recovery can occur outside a clinic.
3. **Rights are operational, not decorative.** Choice, legal capacity,
   privacy, consent and freedom from coercion change the quality of the care
   route and the willingness to seek help again.
4. **Place changes crisis risk.** Rural distance, conflict, displacement,
   informal settlements and urban anonymity create different routes through
   emergency, family and community support.
5. **Recovery is participation.** Symptom change matters, but meaning,
   relationships, education, work, culture and civic life are part of the
   outcome rather than optional extras.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A community team can expand access while remaining understaffed, coercive,
  unaffordable or unavailable outside business hours.
- Closing beds can produce apparent deinstitutionalization while shifting risk
  to homelessness, family confinement, emergency departments or prisons.
- A workplace counselling benefit can coexist with workloads, harassment,
  insecure contracts or retaliation that make disclosure unsafe.
- Peer workers can improve trust in one service while being underpaid, tokenized
  or excluded from actual decisions.
- A digital mental-health tool can reach remote users while excluding people
  without devices, privacy, language support, data control or reliable internet.
- Reduced restraint can reflect changed recording or case mix rather than safer
  care; crisis outcomes require named episodes and later follow-up.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, OECD and
country evidence support the community, rights, inequality, work, peer and
crisis mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because public
sources rarely follow the same person across distress, help-seeking, crisis,
housing, treatment, work/education, relationships, recovery and remedy.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the community-care, rights, deinstitutionalization,
inequality, work, peer and crisis mechanisms. It does not close named recovery,
continuous access, safety, housing retention, family burden, return to work or
education, trust after crisis, technology equity, or remedy outcomes. Those
remain acquisition targets.
