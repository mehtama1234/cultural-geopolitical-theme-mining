# Rotation F: time, care and hidden social infrastructure

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** unpaid care, work-family time, social reproduction, hidden prices and participation  
**Status:** Tier 2 mechanism packet; time and monetary scale are observed, later-life conversion remains open

## Why this source matters

GAO's September 2026 analysis of 2021–2024 American Time Use Survey data gives
unpaid household work an explicit economic and institutional status. It estimates
that 87.3% of people age 15 and older performed at least one type of unpaid
household work on a typical day, spending about 3.72 hours; including secondary
child care raises the estimate to 88.4% and 4.74 hours. GAO estimates the 2024
market value of unpaid household work at $5.6–$6.0 trillion, or at least
19.2–20.2% of GDP.

A 2026 BLS analysis adds a different lens: work-family burden is not only the
total amount of work and caregiving, but also fragmentation across episodes and
multitasking, including secondary caregiving during paid work.

The conversion is:

```text
care/household need
  -> unpaid time, fragmentation and coordination
  -> paid-work, health, mobility or social-participation trade-off
  -> alternative care, withdrawal, continuity or exhaustion
  -> household security, belonging and institutional meaning
```

The data establish scale and distribution, not a universal causal effect on
employment, care quality or civic life.

## New trend interpretation

The stronger trend is not merely “caregiving is increasing” or “unpaid work is
valuable.” It is:

> Social systems are increasingly dependent on unpaid time while the scarce
> resource is not only hours, but predictable, non-fragmented and socially
> supported time.

This reframes time as infrastructure. A person with the same nominal service,
wage or public entitlement may have less usable agency if care is fragmented,
travel is long, work is inflexible, or the household lacks a substitute.

## Cultural and geographical meaning

Care is culturally interpreted through responsibility, reciprocity, gender,
family obligation, dignity and belonging. A policy that counts a service as
available may still be experienced as abandonment if the person must coordinate
multiple providers, miss paid work, travel long distances or rely on fragile
family networks.

Geography changes the conversion through provider density, transport, broadband,
housing, school schedules, employment structure, rurality and migration. The
same care hour has different consequences in a high-cost city, a rural service
desert, a multilingual household or a place with strong but exhausted mutual
aid.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A large time burden does not automatically imply low wellbeing; care can also
  express meaning, reciprocity and agency.
- The GAO market value is an estimate based on valuation assumptions, not wages
  actually paid or a claim that all care should be commodified.
- A diary day is not a full longitudinal record of need, quality or recovery.
- Secondary caregiving captures attention while doing another activity but does
  not by itself establish reduced work performance or harm.
- Aggregate sex, age, income or employment differences do not identify the
  causal role of geography, race, disability, culture or policy.

## Promotion decision

Promote the following Tier 2 mechanism:

**Time is social infrastructure, and its quality depends on predictability,
fragmentation, alternatives and support—not only on the number of hours.**

The next qualifying evidence is a panel, matched employer/service record or
place-based study that links care-time fragmentation to a documented
alternative, work/health/participation outcome and later continuity or exit.
