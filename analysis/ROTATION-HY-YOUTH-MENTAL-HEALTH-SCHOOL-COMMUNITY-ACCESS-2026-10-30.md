# Rotation HY — Youth mental-health access, school support and community continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When youth mental-health awareness, school counsellors, helplines, digital platforms and community services are introduced, do young people reach trusted, confidential, culturally safe and continuous support—or does the chain stop at screening, training, campaign reach, referral or hotline use?

## Why this packet matters

Youth mental health is not converted by awareness alone. A young person needs a route that fits age, language, gender, disability, family context, school, work, conflict and stigma; a trained adult or service; confidentiality and safety; referral capacity; and continuity when the young person changes school, moves, or loses routine.

```text
distress / risk / social pressure
  -> recognition and help-seeking
  -> trusted first contact
  -> confidential assessment and culturally safe support
  -> referral / specialist care / peer or family support
  -> continuity and functioning in school, work and relationships
  -> safety, agency and durable belonging
```

School and digital channels expand reach, but can also expose privacy, consent, safeguarding, language, device and professional-capacity risks. The unit of analysis must therefore move beyond awareness impressions and service contacts to a young person’s usable path through care.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| HY1 | WHO’s 2024 service guidance says very few children and young people receive the mental-health services they need and calls for community-level service design adaptable to context. | Global access gap and service-design frame. | Person-level help-seeking, receipt, therapeutic fit, continuity or outcome. |
| HY2 | WHO notes adolescent mental-health experiences can impair daily life and education, and emphasizes rights, non-pharmacological approaches and avoiding over-medicalization. | Participation, rights and care-quality frame. | Whether a named service preserves confidentiality, agency and functioning. |
| HY3 | UNICEF reports mental-health services scaled in 45 countries in 2024, reaching 5.28 million children/adolescents through primary care, schools and digital platforms; Viet Nam integrated MHPSS into national education and reached over 2 million adolescents. | System reach and national education integration. | Unique young-person receipt, quality, referral, safety, functioning or long-term outcome. |
| HY4 | UNICEF’s Türkiye example reports 41,942 school counsellors trained, reaching approximately 1.5 million students including 384,209 refugee children. | Workforce training and reach across host/refugee populations. | Counsellor availability, confidential contact, referral, refugee-specific fit or recovery. |
| HY5 | UNICEF’s Brazil example reports the Pode Falar digital platform connected 114,140 unique users with trained providers as a national adolescent support system. | Digital contact and provider connection. | Crisis resolution, repeat care, safety, privacy, access for excluded users or functioning. |
| HY6 | UNICEF Nepal reports a severe specialist scarcity, with one child psychiatrist in the country at the time described, while training and tele-mental-health outreach reached caregivers, children and frontline workers. | Scarcity, task-sharing, training and telehealth mechanism. | Clinical receipt, wait time, diagnosis, continuity, referral completion or outcomes. |
| HY7 | WHO describes a school-based family support centre in North Macedonia, and its country profile notes that teacher training, parental engagement, community participation and sustained reinforcement remain barriers. | Named place-based service and implementation barriers. | Student-level use, confidentiality, symptom change, family safety or durable behaviour change. |
| HY8 | UNICEF’s Eastern and Southern Africa analysis used 21 country offices, interviews and focus groups and reports persistent policy, workforce and school-based MHPSS gaps. | Regional system gap and youth/stakeholder evidence route. | Comparable country-level service receipt, cultural fit, referral, functioning or outcome. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Help-seeking is shaped by stigma, family authority, school discipline, religion, language, gender norms, migration, conflict and the perceived consequences of disclosure. Refugee inclusion in a counsellor count does not prove refugee-safe care. Digital services may reduce travel and stigma for some users while excluding young people without privacy, devices, connectivity or safe payment. Specialist scarcity makes community, school and peer routes important, but also raises safeguarding and quality-assurance demands.

## Counterexample and source boundary

The reach figures are deliberately not treated as outcomes. A trained counsellor, a platform user, an outreach participant or a national policy can coexist with unmet clinical need, unsafe disclosure, referral failure and limited specialist capacity. Nepal and Eastern/Southern Africa provide clear capacity boundaries; North Macedonia provides an implementation example with sustained-behaviour-change barriers.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Awareness, screening, counsellor training, platform users, hotline contacts, referrals, school integration or a national plan is not by itself trusted care, confidentiality, therapeutic receipt, safety, continuity, functioning or recovery for young people.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **youth-mental-health-access-to-trusted-continuous-functioning**. The strongest next acquisition is a named school, community or digital service chain linking distress, help-seeking, first contact, confidentiality, assessment, referral, treatment/support, repeat contact, school/work functioning, safety and youth-defined agency, disaggregated by gender, disability, language, displacement and geography.
