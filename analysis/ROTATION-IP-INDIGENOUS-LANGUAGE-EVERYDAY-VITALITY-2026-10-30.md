# Rotation IP — Indigenous-language vitality, digital access and everyday cultural continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When states, schools, international agencies and technology builders support Indigenous and minority languages, do they create everyday speaking, learning, service access, media and intergenerational transmission—or mainly produce plans, resources, events and digitized language artifacts without community control or daily use?

## Why this packet matters

Language is simultaneously infrastructure, identity, memory, knowledge, service access and political power. A language can be officially recognized yet absent from a clinic, court, school assessment, keyboard, public alert or AI system. Digitization can support revitalization, but it can also extract knowledge or reproduce errors if communities do not control data, representation and access.

```text
language status / endangerment / access gap
  -> policy, school, media, public service and digital investment
  -> teacher/speaker/community production and governance
  -> comprehension, use, learning, safety, identity and participation
  -> family transmission, institutional presence and cultural continuity
```

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| IP1 | UNESCO says nearly 40% of the world’s languages, most of them Indigenous, are endangered and the International Decade of Indigenous Languages promotes language rights, education, digital inclusion and intergenerational transmission. | Global risk and policy architecture. | Speaker-level vitality, home use, service access, control or durable transmission. |
| IP2 | UNESCO reports that 40% of people globally lack education in a language they speak and understand fluently; only 351 of roughly 7,000 languages are used as media of instruction. | Education-language access and institutional scarcity baseline. | Actual classroom comprehension, teacher capacity, progression, identity or family use. |
| IP3 | UNESCO’s 2025 multilingual-education guidance recommends sociolinguistic data, legislation, mother-tongue materials, aligned assessment, bilingual teachers and meaningful parent/community engagement. | Policy and implementation design. | Funding, teacher availability, local language variation, classroom quality or learner outcome. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Language policy is a geography of who can be understood. A national language may enable mobility while displacing local knowledge; a mother tongue may support conceptual learning while lacking textbooks, trained teachers or official status. In Indigenous territories, language and land, ecological knowledge, ceremony and governance can be inseparable. For migrants and displaced communities, language continuity can preserve belonging while also affecting access to health, justice, education and social protection.

Digital tools introduce a new power layer. Speech recognition, translation and generative AI can lower barriers, but low-resource languages may be underrepresented, mistranslated or mined for training data without consent. Community radio and Indigenous media may be more socially usable than a technically sophisticated application, yet they often face financing, safety and infrastructure constraints.

## Counterexample and source boundary

An official-language policy, language list, school programme, bilingual textbook, translation, AI model, media event, digitized archive or number of resources is not by itself everyday vitality, comprehension, cultural authority, safe service access, speaker agency or intergenerational transmission. The evidence closes global framing, education scarcity, policy design, named classroom and country mechanisms, digital policy and media barriers, but no public source here closes a comparable speaker/family/service chain through daily use, learning, consent, data governance and durable continuity.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** A language-rights declaration, multilingual-education policy, bilingual material, digital archive, AI tool, translation service, media programme or cultural event is not by itself language vitality, accurate public service, learner success, data sovereignty, speaker agency or intergenerational continuity.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **Indigenous-language-policy-to-everyday-use-service-access-and-intergenerational-vitality**. The strongest next acquisition is a named language/community/family/school/service/media chain linking speaker status, home use, teacher/content, digital tool, consent, comprehension, service encounter, learning, safety, participation, media reach, youth uptake and repeated intergenerational transmission.
