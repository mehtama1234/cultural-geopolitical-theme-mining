# Rotation EW — Climate relocation, durable settlement and place continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 climate-hazard-to-durable-relocation-and-place-continuity conversion question  
**Research lane:** climate mobility / planned relocation / managed retreat / displacement / livelihoods / land / housing / culture / host communities / agency / durable solutions

## Why this rotation

The atlas already covers climate risk, disaster recovery, migration and local
capacity. This deepening packet isolates a difficult adaptation claim: whether
planned relocation or managed retreat makes people safer while preserving
housing, livelihoods, land rights, social networks, cultural identity and the
agency to decide whether, when and where to move.

Relocation counts, new houses, land acquisition, a resettlement plan, a safer
site, compensation, an early-warning system or a mobility programme are
important signals. They do not by themselves show that a named household has
secure tenure, restored income, access to services, social belonging, cultural
continuity, host-community acceptance or a viable choice to stay, move or
return.

## Research question

When climate hazards make in-place adaptation insufficient, under what
conditions does planned relocation or managed retreat produce safer and more
durable lives without transferring risk, livelihood loss, land insecurity,
social fragmentation, cultural erasure or decision-making power away from
affected people and host communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
hazard / exposure / loss of habitability / adaptation limit / displacement risk
  -> stay, adapt, evacuate, migrate, relocate or retreat decision
  -> land, tenure, housing, infrastructure, services, livelihoods and host settlement
  -> safety, income, health, education, food, belonging, cultural continuity and agency
  -> reduced risk, durable solution, return/retention, social cohesion and legitimacy
```

The minimum unit is a named household, community, host group, settlement,
relocation site, land parcel, livelihood, service and time period. People moved
is an intermediate operation signal; it is not proof of a durable solution.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Climate mobility requires a portfolio, not a relocation default

The World Bank’s Groundswell work treats climate mobility as one part of a
broader adaptation portfolio: adapt in place where possible, enable movement
when necessary, and support people after movement. It emphasizes safe and
dignified planned relocation and preparation of destination services such as
health, education and housing. This prevents “relocation” from becoming a
single output disconnected from origin and destination systems.

Source: [World Bank, Groundswell: Acting on Internal Climate Migration](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/837771631204427139/pdf/Groundswell-Part-II-Acting-on-Internal-Climate-Migration.pdf).

### 2. Forced displacement and climate risk are layered

UNHCR’s 2024 climate report finds that climate shocks interact with conflict and
forced displacement, while noting that only a minority of national climate
plans contained concrete provisions for planned relocation. A relocation test
must therefore retain legal status, protection, conflict, disability, gender,
host-community and repeated-displacement conditions rather than treat climate
exposure as an isolated variable.

Source: [UNHCR, No Escape: climate change, conflict and forced displacement](https://www.unhcr.org/publications/no-escape-frontlines-climate-change-conflict-and-forced-displacement).

### 3. Community experience shows that relocation is a social process

IOM’s 2025 Solomon Islands research used community consultations and key
informant interviews across communities with direct relocation experience. It
was designed to inform planned-relocation guidelines, underscoring that
relocation decisions require local knowledge, consent, governance and attention
to the lived consequences of moving—not merely engineering a safer site.

Source: [IOM, Climate Mobility and Relocation in Solomon Islands](https://publications.iom.int/books/climate-mobility-and-relocation-solomon-islands-insights-community-experiences-and-key-stakeholders).

### 4. Livelihood restoration remains a major failure point

IOM’s planned-relocation manual states that outcomes are mixed and that
establishing sustainable livelihoods is often difficult. It records that
relocation can reduce risk and increase access to services while still failing
to restore incomes because household livelihoods depend on many resources and
relationships. Post-relocation livelihood support and the possibility of
continued coping mobility therefore belong in the conversion chain.

Source: [IOM, Planned Relocation for Communities](https://publications.iom.int/system/files/pdf/training_manual_on_planned_relocation_eng.pdf).

### 5. Disaster data often disappear after the first movement

UNDRR reports that evacuation and arrival numbers are often recorded
immediately after an event, while data on people remaining in shelters,
returning or moving onward become scarce weeks and months later. A count of
evacuees or arrivals therefore cannot establish durable settlement, return,
integration, safety or place continuity.

Source: [UNDRR, Understanding disaster displacement and assessing future risk](https://www.undrr.org/resource/case-study/understanding-disaster-displacement-and-assessing-future-risk).

### 6. Safer land can still be culturally and economically unviable

World Bank analysis of Pacific atoll countries presents protect, accommodate
and retreat as context-dependent pathways and recommends investments in
education, health and governance that have long-term value across scenarios.
This is a useful caution: a technically safer site may still fail if it lacks
livelihood access, social networks, public services, governance or the cultural
relationship to land and sea that makes the place viable.

Source: [World Bank, adaptation pathways for Pacific atoll countries](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2024/11/14/protect-accommodate-or-retreat-new-world-bank-research-outlines-adaptation-pathways-for-pacific-atoll-countries).

## Bounded proposition 154

**A climate-relocation plan, managed-retreat programme, resettlement site,
evacuation, compensation package, new house, land purchase, adaptation project,
mobility pathway or “safer” destination is not by itself durable safety,
secure tenure, restored livelihood, household resilience, cultural continuity,
social belonging, host-community legitimacy or agency.** People moved, homes
built, parcels acquired, funds disbursed, services planned or displacement
recorded do not establish that named households can remain or move by choice,
retain land and social ties, recover income and food security, access services,
avoid repeated displacement, or obtain remedy when relocation damages their
rights, culture or livelihood.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Place is more than a hazard map.** Land contains memory, kinship, burial,
   livelihood, language, identity and political belonging.
2. **Safety has a destination test.** A site is not safe if it lacks water,
   schools, health care, transport, work, tenure or protection from host conflict.
3. **Movement can be unequal within a household.** Women, older people,
   disabled people, children, migrants and livelihood-dependent workers may face
   different costs and bargaining power.
4. **Consent is temporal.** Emergency acceptance, compensated agreement and
   long-term ability to return, remain or contest are different forms of agency.
5. **Hosts are affected people too.** New arrivals can increase pressure on
   land, services, jobs and social relations; host participation is part of
   legitimacy rather than an externality.
6. **Cultural continuity can be operational.** Language, collective practices,
   access to customary land and sea, ritual sites and reciprocal networks can be
   tracked as material conditions of a durable solution.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A safer house can be far from work, farms, fishing grounds, markets or care
  networks, increasing debt and livelihood loss.
- Compensation can be paid while tenure remains insecure or land valuation
  excludes customary, informal or collective rights.
- A relocation site can receive infrastructure while services, teachers,
  clinicians, transport or maintenance fail after project funding ends.
- Evacuation can reduce immediate mortality while producing prolonged shelter,
  debt, schooling, health and protection harms.
- People may move repeatedly after relocation because the destination remains
  exposed, unaffordable or socially unviable.
- A participatory meeting can occur without community consent, representative
  inclusion, decision power or a meaningful veto over alternatives.
- Remaining in place can be a rational, culturally grounded choice when
  mobility would destroy livelihood or social protection; immobility can also
  be forced by poverty, disability, insecurity or lack of documents.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. World Bank,
UNHCR, IOM and UNDRR evidence supports the separate mobility, displacement,
livelihood, destination, data and participation mechanisms. Keep the integrated
climate-hazard-to-durable-relocation-and-place-continuity outcome at **Tier 2**
because public sources rarely follow the same household through origin loss,
decision, tenure, livelihood, service access, social belonging and long-term
agency.

## Next test

Select one completed or active relocation and follow households at origin,
transit, destination and after one seasonal or livelihood cycle. Record hazard,
choice, consent, land, tenure, housing, income, food, services, travel, social
ties, culture, host relations, return or onward movement, complaint and remedy.
Compare households who stayed, moved voluntarily, moved under pressure and
remained unable to move.

## Source boundary

The packet closes the adaptation-portfolio, layered-displacement, community-
experience, livelihood-restoration, post-event-data and destination-safety
mechanisms. It does not close named consent, secure tenure, income recovery,
service continuity, cultural practice, host-community effects, repeated movement,
return, agency or remedy. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open for
most climate-mobility sources. Relocation is therefore treated as a negotiated
place-and-livelihood transition, not as a unit count.
