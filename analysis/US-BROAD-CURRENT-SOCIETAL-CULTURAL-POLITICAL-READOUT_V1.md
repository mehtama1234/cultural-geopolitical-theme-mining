# What appears to be happening in American society

**Checked:** 2026-09-25  
**Purpose:** plain-language synthesis of the broad Cultural, Social, and
Geopolitical Theme Mining program  
**Evidence boundary:** this is a cross-source interpretation, not one survey,
one population, or one causal model.

## The short answer

American systems are becoming more capable and more visible, but people do
not receive equal practical power from them.

The important divide is increasingly between:

- an option that exists on paper and one that can actually be used;
- support that keeps life going and security that lasts;
- belonging that feels meaningful and support that is delivered;
- a complaint or request that is recorded and a problem that is corrected;
- a project that operates and a public benefit that reaches people; and
- a capability that is real and one that changes another actor’s choices.

Safety infrastructure shows the same pattern. GAO found that the number of
flight simulators grew by more than 500% while FAA simulator-program staffing
stayed roughly steady. FAA kept evaluation activity fairly steady partly by
allowing longer intervals for some simulators. That may be an efficient
risk-based choice, but unclear decision rules, sponsor communication problems,
and open skills gaps mean that stable inspection counts do not by themselves
show stable protection. This is a bounded oversight finding, not evidence of
unsafe simulators or greater accident risk.

Federal benefits and grants add a delivery-chain version. GAO reviewed 20
large state- and locally administered programs representing about $1.1
trillion in FY2025 obligations. Money may pass through states, Tribes, local
governments, subrecipients, and contractors. That can make delivery fit local
needs, but it can also make responsibility and records harder to see: only 5
of the 20 programs had documented evidence of identifying and assessing fraud
risks for action, while 15 did not. This does not prove fraud or service
failure. It suggests that public trust depends on whether a person can see who
holds the record and who can fix an error after the money leaves the federal
office.

National-security acquisitions add a planning-and-credibility version. In a
GAO review of 27 DHS programs, 18 of 19 programs with approved baselines met
their latest goals, but 15 had revised those baselines and projected costs had
risen $11.4 billion from the originals. Eight programs also had major staff
reductions, while acquisition oversight was reorganized. The bounded signal is
not that the programs failed. It is that public confidence can attach to a
current “on track” label even when the original promise and the institution
watching it have changed.

The aging workforce adds a human-scale version. In six selected federal
workforce programs, people 55 and older made up about 22% of participants, but
their post-exit employment was lower than that of younger participants and they
used training less often. Interviews showed real digital-literacy and job
benefits as well as application friction, limited openings, and possible age
discrimination. The broader signal is that being counted in a public program is
not the same as having a usable route into decent, lasting work—especially when
care, health, money, and retirement timing compete with training.

Unemployment insurance adds a firm-power version of the same question. A
Census study of Great Recession extensions finds about one extra week of
nonemployment; the overall earnings effect was not statistically significant,
but the earnings response was weaker in more concentrated local labor markets.
The broader signal is that time to search has different value depending on how
many employers and alternatives a worker can realistically reach. This is a
historical working-paper result, not a current household-welfare estimate.

AI diffusion adds an important internal split. A Census firm survey found AI in
18% of firms but 32% employment-weighted, while worker task use appeared in 23%
of firms and 41% employment-weighted. Formal firm adoption and worker use did
not always coincide. Most users described augmentation, and employment
decreases were rare, but broader functional deployment and operational
investment were the parts associated with decreases. The social question is
therefore not simply whether AI exists; it is who controls the deployment and
who absorbs the adjustment.

Health information adds a trust-and-meaning version. Pew finds that Americans
still turn most often to clinicians, but many also use people with similar
health problems, social media, and AI chatbots. Half have at least some trouble
judging accuracy, and 54% have difficulty deciding what to trust when claims
conflict. The broader signal is not that experts have been replaced; it is that
people now assemble health guidance across institutions, peers, platforms, and
AI, while access and trust do not always point to the same source.

Influencers add a more personal version of that authority problem. Pew found
that many large health accounts present themselves through a mix of professional
credentials, coaching, entrepreneurship, parenthood, and lived experience.
People often trust only part of what they hear, yet many still say the content
helps them understand health. The broader cultural signal is that relatability
can create real influence before expertise, sponsorship, accuracy, or downstream
health effects are settled.

Partisan identity adds another layer to the political picture. Pew's 2026
National Public Opinion Reference Survey found a narrow national difference—47%
Democratic or Democratic-leaning versus 43% Republican or Republican-leaning—
but much wider and uneven social boundaries: men leaned Republican, women
Democratic, and Black and Hispanic adults leaned Democratic, while college
graduates leaned Democratic and adults without a degree were close to even.
Forty-four percent called themselves independent or something else before
leaners were assigned. The broader signal is that party identity is a shared
political home for many people, but it is assembled from different social
positions rather than one uniform experience. This is repeated cross-sectional
affiliation data, not evidence of switching, voting, causation, or durable
realignment.

There is another version underneath these divides: a person may be willing to
answer a public question, but the available categories may not match how their
household actually works. Recent Census cognitive interviews found uncertainty
about other household members' income, missing time anchors for moving, and
response lists that were too long or repetitive. The broader signal is that
institutional visibility is partly built by translation: what cannot be named,
remembered, or placed in the right box may disappear from the record. This is a
bounded qualitative finding, not evidence that national estimates are wrong.

The same split appears in benefit data. In the 2021 Child Tax Credit study,
91% of eligible taxpayers participated but 85% of eligible dollars were paid.
That means a program can look broadly reached by counting people while still
leaving money unclaimed or only partly claimed. It is evidence of a conversion
gap, not yet an explanation of why it happened or proof that a household became
secure.

Family care reveals another practical inequality. The Census SIPP study finds
that about half of recent first-time parents used paid leave, but the usable
option differs by race/ethnicity and sex, and some parents returned to work
within a month. The broad point is not that race alone caused the difference;
it is that pay, job type, employer policy, household resources, and care needs
can turn the same formal family transition into very different amounts of time.

The firm side shows a parallel pattern. Census LEHD evidence suggests that new
firms often hire workers socially similar to their founders, even after basic
industry, place, and cohort comparisons. So job creation can coexist with
unequal access to the new jobs themselves. The evidence points toward firm
demand, but it does not by itself prove discrimination or explain every group's
path.

The technology-and-generation side adds a sharper entry problem. A Census study
of new college graduates finds that the most AI-exposed majors had lower initial
employment and about 13% lower early earnings after ChatGPT's release. About
half of the earnings loss came from moving into lower-paying industries, not
only from earning less inside the same sector. Many graduates later adjusted,
but the most exposed group remained weaker around two years out. The broad
signal is not simply “AI eliminates jobs.” It is that a person can stay attached
to work while the first career rung becomes lower-paid or harder to reach. The
study does not yet show long-run scarring, actual individual AI use, or a
national result.

Public employment adds a counterexample to the idea that temporary help is only
temporary. In the 2010 Census hiring study, applicants just around the hiring
threshold who received a Census job were much more likely to have W-2 work in
2010 and still more likely to have it in 2023. The long-run gain was concentrated
among men; across the broader applicant pool, the gain faded, and low-score
hires had more misconduct or performance terminations. The broader cultural
signal is that a public institution can create a work bridge for people near
the edge of employment, but the bridge is not equally usable or equally
beneficial for everyone. This is a specific recession-era Census setting, not a
general job-guarantee result.

Environmental finance adds a different kind of institutional power. A Census
study of the 1996 Lender Liability Act finds that when lenders became less
exposed to cleanup costs, affected firms polluted more and reduced abatement
investment, while loan maturity lengthened. The broader social signal is that
public risk can move through private contracts: a firm may preserve production
and employment while environmental accountability weakens. The study does not
measure residents' exposure, health, cleanup, or present-day finance.

Health infrastructure shows the same capacity-versus-access split. Census data
show fewer but larger hospitals and rising hospital employment, while more
counties—especially rural Southern counties—have no study-defined hospital.
That does not prove care disappeared, but it means local protection depends more
on travel, transport, insurance, emergency capacity, and usable broadband.

Disaster recovery adds a further complication: public money can improve
aggregate productivity by helping new and young firms replace or acquire capital
after a flood, while workers and communities attached to closing firms still
face disruption. “The economy recovered” and “the same people recovered” are
separate claims.

## The recurring societal subthemes

### 1. Formal help is separating from usable help

Vouchers, SNAP, health coverage, refunds, privacy rights, energy credits, and
care services can all exist while people still face paperwork, waiting,
landlord or provider refusal, missing notices, transportation problems, bill
timing, or difficult appeals.

The broad pattern is not that public systems are fake. It is that the route to
using them has become part of the benefit itself. Time, information, location,
language, status, and the ability to correct an error determine who gets the
protection in practice.

The SNAP evidence illustrates the gap: state application-timeliness rates
range from about 61% to 97%, while separate recipient evidence reports
interruption, insufficient notice, and paperwork barriers. Those figures are
different denominators, not one national success rate, but together show why
participation cannot stand in for route quality.

Housing assistance adds a sharper status dimension. HUD's 2026 announcement
reported audit matches involving nearly 200,000 tenants requiring eligibility
verification, nearly 25,000 deceased tenants, and nearly 6,000 tenants the
agency described as ineligible non-Americans. The related PHA process requires
verification, possible secondary review, and an appeal route; mixed-status
households may receive continued, deferred, or prorated assistance. But the
public materials do not show match accuracy, notice, appeal resolution,
proration, termination, displacement, or later housing stability. The broader
point is that a public benefit can become a test of whether a household is
correctly recognized by the system before it becomes reliable shelter. The
counts are agency-reported administrative figures, not independently audited
prevalence estimates or a household outcome sample. See the [HUD
verification record](records/us-hud-housing-eligibility-verification-2026.json).

SNAP's EBT modernization shows the same tension in a more everyday form.
Chip-and-tap cards, retailer upgrades, and possible mobile payments may improve
security, but use still depends on PINs, compatible stores, fallback systems,
and a working replacement route. USDA's proposed fourth-replacement threshold
can deter misuse while also adding notice, explanation, delay, or false-positive
risk for households whose cards were stolen, damaged, or repeatedly lost. The
public status pages do not show whether theft fell, replacement arrived in
time, or food access was restored. Security for the account is not automatically
usable security for the recipient. See the [SNAP EBT modernization
record](records/us-usda-snap-ebt-modernization-control-2026.json).

SNAP recertification data show the paperwork route itself becoming a source of
food-insecurity risk. In Urban Institute's December 2024 survey of working-age
adults in SNAP families, 24% reported an involuntary stop or interruption in
the prior year; among the smaller group who said they could not recertify on
time, 40% said they did not have enough time after notice, 32% did not receive
the notice, 22% found the paperwork too difficult, and 16% could not complete
the interview. These are retrospective self-reports, not linked case files or
food-security effects, and the reason categories can overlap. They show why a
benefit can be legally available while renewal time, notice, work schedules,
and paperwork determine whether it continues. See the [Urban SNAP
recertification record](records/us-urban-wbns-snap-recertification-interruption-2024.json).

USDA's FY2025 state tables make the route-performance distinction visible.
Across 50 states and D.C., application timeliness ranged from 61.2% to 97.2%,
while recertification timeliness ranged from 25.2% to 99.9%. Those measures did
not simply track participation: Georgia reported 15.6% participation with
61.2% application timeliness, while Wisconsin reported 11.6% participation
with 97.2% application timeliness. These are state aggregates with different
case mixes and quality-control universes, not household outcomes or causal
comparisons. They show why participation, processing speed, renewal
continuity, and actual food security must remain separate stages. See the
[USDA SNAP route-performance record](records/usda-snap-state-route-performance-2026.json).

The broader SNAP route synthesis shows why “participation” is not a single
success measure. In separate study settings, flexible interviews increased
approvals by about 6 percentage points, office closures reduced tract
participation by 7%–9% over two years, and work requirements increased exits
without increasing employment in the reported populations. SIPP transitions
also show that continued receipt can coexist with following-month rent or
utility hardship, while charitable food can supplement public help and still
be difficult to reach. These studies use different people, places, dates, and
designs; they are not one causal chain. Together they show that route design
can change who receives or keeps a benefit before anyone can claim improved
food security, household room, or political trust. See the [SNAP route and
security triangulation](records/us-public-system-route-security-triangulation-2026.json).

ACA Medicaid expansion adds a cross-program mechanism. Border-county and
restricted-ACS comparisons found increases in SNAP and TANF participation,
with a less precise possible increase in EITC participation, apparently
concentrated among people already eligible for those programs. The official
Census CES version estimates that a typical 0-to-138% expansion reduced
uninsurance by about 2.2 percentage points and increased SNAP by about 0.6
people per 100; roughly one in five adults induced into public insurance was
also induced into SNAP. The interpretation is that Medicaid eligibility or
enrollment may lower the marginal effort of applying for other help—through
information, reduced transaction costs, or a more legible route. The study
does not follow one household through notice, application, benefit amount,
food security, or recovery, so expanded participation is not the same as
expanded security. See the [Medicaid cross-program spillover record](records/us-medicaid-expansion-safety-net-spillovers-2014-2019.json) and the [detailed finding](findings/us-medicaid-expansion-safety-net-spillover-001.md).

The GAO Tribal-federal shared-decision review adds a sovereignty-and-usable-
voice subtheme. Five illustrative agreements and interviews identify 11
features that can strengthen shared management, but legal authority, staffing,
information, dispute routes, and the time required for Tribal advocacy still
shape whether formal consultation becomes practical control. The report does
not measure which decisions changed, community consent, cultural outcomes, or
durable trust. See the [shared-control finding](findings/us-gao-tribal-federal-shared-decision-making-agreements-2026-matched-evidence-001.md).

### 2. Search and waiting are hidden prices

Households search for food, housing, work, doctors, benefits, loans, refunds,
and safer digital or consumer options. The search may protect one need while
using up time, money, care capacity, attention, dignity, or political energy.

Finding an option is not the same as finding a good option. A person may remain
housed, employed, connected, or enrolled because the alternative is worse—not
because the current arrangement is secure.

### 2A. Staying afloat often means trading one resource for another

The household evidence adds a more concrete version of that pattern. In the
Federal Reserve's 2024 SHED, 63% of adults reported switching to cheaper
products, 61% using less or stopping something, 46% delaying a major purchase,
43% reducing savings, 16% increasing borrowing, and 18% working more or taking
another job in response to higher prices. These answers could overlap, so they
are not a single hardship score. They show a broad adaptation menu: protect
today's food, bills, or routine by spending less on quality or quantity, using
future savings, taking on debt, or giving more time to work.

BLS's Consumer Expenditure Survey shows the budget structure beneath that
adaptation menu. In 2024, average annual spending was $78,535 per consumer
unit and housing accounted for 33.4% of the average budget. The published
income-quintile comparison reports different income and expenditure changes,
with the lowest-quintile spending increase concentrated in housing. This does
not prove that a specific family could not pay rent or that added spending was
only price pressure: consumer-unit averages cannot show a bill's timing,
quantity, liquidity, debt, substitution, or what was sacrificed. It does show
why the same price or payment change lands differently before hardship is
visible. See the [BLS consumer-expenditures record](records/us-bls-consumer-expenditures-income-quintile-2024.json).

Transaction evidence gives that difference a more concrete consumer route.
An NBER study of large-bank NSF-fee eliminations and overdraft-policy changes
finds the targeted fees fell immediately, but later reductions in related fees,
interest, and alternative-financial-service use appeared only among more liquid
households; the study did not find a substantial reduction in financial stress
among more vulnerable households. Removing a fee can therefore be real relief
without restoring the cash buffer that determines what happens next. The study
does not identify each original bill, protected need, family support,
merchant/service result, or complete household welfare. See the [bank-fee and
financial-room record](records/us-nber-bank-fees-household-financial-wellbeing-2017-2022.json).

The same source family shows why this is unequal. Food insufficiency was 19%
among adults in families earning under $25,000 in 2024, compared with 2% among
those at $100,000 or more; in 2025 the reported figures were 21% and 1%.
Separately, 23% of adults reported receiving help from outside their household
with a listed expense, rising to 47% among ages 18–29 and falling to 13% among
those 45 and older. That help may be generous and effective, but it can also
show who has a family or social buffer and who must absorb the same pressure
alone.

The broad cultural and political meaning is important: visible stability may
be produced by quiet sacrifice. A household can keep paying, eating, working,
or caring while losing savings, time, quality, health margin, independence, or
future room to respond. These sources are separate population snapshots and a
paired survey comparison, not one complete household story; they support a
recurring pattern of adaptation, not a claim that every trade-off happened in
the same people or had the same cause.

The Federal Reserve's 2025 SHED adds a timing and margin layer. Thirty percent
of adults said their monthly income varied at least sometimes, and 11% said
that variation made it difficult to pay bills. The figures were higher among
self-employed adults—58% reported variation and 22% reported bill difficulty—
than among employees, at 28% and 10%. Only 41% of adults said money was left
over always or often; the figure was 19% below $25,000 in family income and 59%
at $100,000 or more. These are separate self-reports, not a household panel or
causal path, but they show why income amount, income timing, outside help, and
budget margin are different forms of room. See the [SHED income-volatility and
support record](records/us-federal-reserve-income-volatility-support-2025.json).

EIA's 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey adds a concrete cost
gradient. Using each income band's midpoint as a bounded affordability proxy,
mean modeled energy expenditure was about 28.7% of the midpoint for households
below $15,000, compared with 1.7% for households at $75,000 or more. Only 5.3%
of households reported ever receiving home-energy assistance in the retained
valid universe. The proxy is not a household's observed bill-to-income ratio,
and assistance receipt is not proof that heat, cooling, arrears, or energy
security were restored. It does show why the same energy system can consume
very different amounts of household room. See the [RECS energy-burden record](records/us-recs-energy-burden-income-assistance-2020.json).

The Urban Institute's longer food-security series shows why temporary relief
and durable security must be separated. Among working-age adults, reported
food insecurity fell from 24.0% in 2019 to about 20% in 2020 and 2021, then
rose to 24.7% in 2022, 27.2% in 2023, 27.0% in 2024, and 27.7% in 2025. In
2025 it was 32% among adults in families with children, 51% below 200% of the
poverty line, and 52.1% among adults with disabilities. These are weighted,
cross-sectional survey estimates; the 2025 method changed, and the series
does not identify whether policy, prices, work, or household composition caused
the movement. It does show that hardship can remain elevated after a support
cycle changes. See the [Urban food-insecurity persistence record](records/us-urban-wbns-food-insecurity-persistence-2019-2025.json).

A separate SIPP adjacent-month screen shows how these pressures can stack. In
selected renter reference-parent pairs reporting utility-payment difficulty,
following-month mortgage hardship was about 58.0% when no child-care work
prevention was reported and 72.8% when it was reported; following-month food
insecurity was about 50.2% versus 64.6%. The second group contains only 12
valid pairs, the utility measure is not a dated bill or shutoff, and the care
measure is an annual reference-period field. This is therefore a small,
descriptive joint-constraint signal—not a utility effect or a household
prevalence estimate—but it shows how housing, utilities, care, and food can
appear as one lived problem even when surveys measure them in separate
questions. See the [SIPP utility/care following-outcomes record](records/us-sipp-utility-care-following-outcomes-2024.json).

The 2008 stimulus evidence adds a consumer-finance conversion: households
spent about 80% of the payment on durable goods within three months, mostly on
vehicles, and borrowed about 40 cents per stimulus dollar for those purchases.
That may have created mobility or replaced a failing essential, but the source
does not show loan terms, repayment, repairs, insurance, or whether the vehicle
protected work and care. A temporary payment can become both immediate use and
a future claim on household income. See the [durable-purchase and financing
record](records/us-nber-transitory-income-durable-purchases-vehicle-financing-2008.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-durable-purchase-financing/findings/us-durable-purchase-financing-001.md).

### 3. Support is becoming a shared social infrastructure

Family networks, neighbors, religious groups, public benefits, employers,
platforms, AI tools, hospitals, and alliances can all keep people or systems
functioning. CIRCLE's political-home evidence, Pew family and public-life
surveys, SNAP records, AI-companion surveys, and NATO medical-readiness records
show different versions of this pattern.

But support is not free. Someone supplies the money, care, transport,
information, privacy, emotional labor, staffing, or public resources. The
central question is who carries that hidden work and whether the support is
adequate and repeatable.

Pew's survey of 4,271 Black adults adds a cultural definition of the support
network itself. Among Black adults who had the relevant relationship, 73% said
they were close to a nonrelative they considered family, 48% were close to a
grandparent, 42% to a cousin, and 36% to an aunt or uncle; the grandparent,
cousin, and aunt/uncle figures were higher than among non-Black adults in the
comparison. This shows that family can extend through choice, kinship, and
community beyond the nuclear household. It does not show money, care, housing,
transport, or emotional labor being delivered. A wide support map can coexist
with obligation, conflict, distance, or limited capacity. See the [Black family
networks record](records/us-pew-black-family-networks-support-2025-2026.json).

Pew's neighborhood survey adds a different social-capacity pattern. In March
2025, 44% of adults said they trusted all or most of their neighbors, compared
with 34% who said most people generally can be trusted. People were more likely
to say they would help a neighbor or friend than to say a neighbor or friend
would help them—for example, 76% said they would bring in mail or water plants,
but 52% thought a neighbor would do the same. This suggests that local trust
and willingness can exist alongside a weaker expectation of dependable
reciprocity. The survey records stated capacity, not help actually delivered,
its burden, adequacy, or later household security. See the [Pew neighborhood
trust and mutual-aid layer](projects/us-cost-trust-politics/pew-2025-neighbor-trust-help-community-capacity-layer-v1.md).

Gallup's March 2026 Values in Action survey adds a kindness-conversion layer.
Sixty percent of adults often or very often see people treating others with
kindness and respect, and 65% experienced a community kindness many or a few
times in the prior week. But only 52% are very comfortable initiating a kind
act toward a stranger, ranging from 64% among adults 65 and older to 35% among
adults 18–29. Recent receipt is associated with initiation comfort: roughly
80% of those receiving kindness many times are very comfortable initiating it,
versus 54% among those receiving it a few times and under 40% among those
receiving it once or not at all. This suggests that interpersonal exposure may
help convert social capacity into willingness, while age and exposure gaps
show that the capacity is uneven. It remains a cross-sectional association:
the survey does not observe actual aid, adequacy, safety cost, institutional
response, or later civic action. See the [Gallup kindness and reciprocity
record](records/us-gallup-kindness-observation-reciprocity-2026.json).

Pew's intergenerational synthesis adds a different family-support clock. In
2024, 66% of U.S. adults ages 65 and older were grandparents; 70% of
grandparents in that age group reported at least monthly contact, while 36% of
adults with a living grandparent described the relationship as extremely or
very close. In a separate two-year HRS window, 21% of grandparents with a
caregiving response reported at least 100 hours of grandchild or
great-grandchild care. These are different populations and measures: status,
contact, closeness, and care do not prove support quality, burden, or household
security. See the [grandparenthood record](records/us-pew-grandparenthood-intergenerational-connection-2024-2025.json).

Religious institutions show another version of the distinction. In Pew's
2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, 80% of adults said religious organizations
help bring people together and strengthen community bonds, and 78% said they
play an important role in helping poor and needy people. Yet only 37% reported
belonging to a house of worship and 40% participated in services at least
monthly. An institution can therefore retain public-service legitimacy beyond
its regular members without proving that services reach people adequately or
that the public accepts its political influence. This is a cross-sectional
judgment, not a measure of delivery, trust over time, or political action. See
the [Pew religious-institutions record](records/us-pew-religious-institutions-community-bonds-2025.json).

The March 2026 Census household-pulse data show why contact and support should
not be treated as the same thing. Frequent loneliness rose from 1.8% among
respondents reporting no household expense difficulty to 30.2% among those
reporting that expenses were very difficult; low social or emotional support
rose from 14.0% to 40.9%. Yet frequent phone or video contact was actually
lower in the very-difficult group, 21.8% versus 28.3%. Food insufficiency also
rose from 0.7% to 33.6%. These are descriptive same-round comparisons, not
proof that expense pressure caused loneliness or isolation, but they suggest
that social infrastructure is about perceived dependable support—not merely
the number of contacts a person has. Material strain may therefore weaken the
quality, availability, or usefulness of connection even when people remain
reachable. See the [Census expense, loneliness, and support record](records/us-census-htops-social-connection-expense-march-2026.json).

The CDC's 26-state BRFSS analysis adds an identity and life-stage boundary:
loneliness and lack of needed support were higher among younger, low-income,
bisexual, and transgender adults, and were associated with stress, frequent
mental distress, and depression history. This makes social connection a
stratified cultural resource rather than a single community score. The study
does not show causation, support received, later recovery, belonging, or civic
action. See the [CDC social-connection finding](projects/us-cdc-loneliness-social-support-2022/findings/us-cdc-loneliness-social-support-2022-001.md).

Charitable food shows that community support can be both real and hard to use.
In the Urban Institute's 2025 survey, 16.1% of adults reported receiving
free groceries or meals, rising to 39.5% among adults in food-insecure
households. Yet 51.6% of charitable-food recipients reported at least one
access difficulty. Among people who needed help but did not get it, common
reasons included limited hours, not knowing where to go, discomfort asking,
transportation, long travel or waiting, and not feeling safe. This is not one
stigma or provider-failure estimate: the reasons overlap and the survey does
not measure food adequacy or later security. It does show that emergency
support depends on time, transport, dignity, information, and treatment—not
just whether a pantry or food bank exists. See the [charitable-food access
record](records/us-urban-wbns-charitable-food-access-2019-2025.json).

An income experiment adds a household-level version of shared support. When one
person received $1,000 per month, other household members were more likely to
enter education or training, while income outside the recipient fell by about
$1,700 per year and partners were less likely to be promoted or move to a better
job. Relationship trust improved suggestively, and reported forgone healthcare
increased in a way that may reflect newly visible unmet need rather than a
simple health harm. Cash can therefore create room, redistribute work, and
expose need at the same time. See the [intrahousehold guaranteed-income
record](records/us-nber-guaranteed-income-intrahousehold-dynamics-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-guaranteed-income-household-dynamics/findings/us-guaranteed-income-household-dynamics-001.md).

### 4. Institutions are more visible without automatically becoming trusted

Dashboards, case statuses, complaint portals, processing records, privacy
request counts, public comments, exercise reports, and payment records make
institutional activity easier to see. That is useful, but visibility does not
prove fairness, correction, adequate payment, or a better final outcome.

Perceived corruption adds a legitimacy version of the same distinction.
Gallup's September 2026 World Poll release reports that 89% of U.S. adults say
corruption is widespread in government, up from 79% in 2025 and 73% in 2024;
the estimate is 91% among Democrats, 90% among independents, and 83% among
Republicans. Seventy-one percent also see corruption as widespread in business,
so the government perception exceeds the business perception by 18 points. In
the closest comparable 2025 cross-national comparison, the U.S. estimate was
79% against a 59% OECD median. This is a powerful public-legitimacy signal,
but it is not an audit of misconduct, an individual exposure measure, or proof
that people stop using public systems. The [Gallup government-corruption
perception record](records/us-gallup-government-corruption-perception-2026.json)
keeps the perception, party, government/business, and OECD boundaries
separate.

The Census Household Panel adds a measurement-and-contact version. Text
messages appeared to bring some survey responses faster, but access and consent
varied across demographic groups and only about half of panelists consented to
text invitations. A faster response route can therefore improve institutional
visibility without proving equal participation, lower burden, greater trust, or
policy responsiveness. See the [text-contact finding](findings/us-census-household-panel-text-consent-response-2024.md).

The Census date-entry experiment adds an interface version. Calendar fields
improved objective accuracy and speed for many near-date tasks even though
participants generally described all three designs as extremely easy. Public
systems can therefore be available and apparently simple while still producing
different completion results for different users. See the [date-entry finding](findings/us-census-date-entry-design-usability-near-dates-2026.md).

The Census formal-privacy paper adds a data-visibility version. A proposed
mechanism can protect confidential records by releasing controlled noisy data,
but the same noise can make small groups or local problems harder to see. A
formal privacy guarantee is therefore not the same as useful accuracy,
representative visibility, or public trust. See the [formal-privacy finding](findings/us-census-formal-privacy-boltzmann-mechanism-2026.md).

The Census incentive-preference study adds a consumer-access version. Most
people preferred digital gift cards or bank transfers, but the small group that
preferred cash was mostly young, low-income, and without a college degree. A
digital default can therefore increase convenience for many while making
receipt harder for people with fewer banking, device, or trust resources. See
the [digital-cash finding](findings/us-census-incentive-payment-preferences-digital-cash-2026.md).

The federal reentry system shows the same problem in a justice setting. GAO
found that earned-time eligibility did not reliably become a timely move to a
halfway house or home confinement: eligibility data were incomplete, capacity
was not fully known, and providers were often paid late. The institution can
therefore offer a formal route while the practical bridge to housing, work,
treatment, and community reconnection remains delayed. This does not establish
what happened to each person after release; it identifies the missing middle
between eligibility and durable return. See the [BOP reentry-timing finding](projects/us-gao-bop-reentry-timing-2026/findings/us-gao-bop-reentry-timing-2026-001.md).

BJS adds a different justice layer: 3.68 million adults were still under
probation or parole at the end of 2024 even though the comparable-agency
population had fallen 24% over the prior decade. A shrinking supervision system
is still a large system of everyday state contact. Its administrative exits do
not tell us whether conditions were workable, services were useful, or people
found durable housing, work, safety, or belonging. See the [community
supervision finding](projects/us-bjs-community-supervision-2024/findings/us-bjs-community-supervision-2024-001.md).

People can become better at reading institutional signals while becoming less
confident that the institution will protect them. Trust therefore attaches to
a specific actor, problem, route, and expected result; one general trust score
cannot explain legitimacy.

This also applies to consumer remedies. A refund, reissue, recall, wage
recovery, privacy completion, or appeal reversal can be a real institutional
response while the person still has to find the route, prove the loss, wait,
cash the payment, repair the account, or find another provider. The [formal
access and usable-control synthesis](US-BROAD-FORMAL-ACCESS-CONTROL-CONVERSION-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
calls this the difference between formal response and practical control.

Walmart's California privacy metrics make the digital version concrete. For
2025, the company reported 1,914 access requests, 3,199 deletion requests,
and 17,367 sale opt-outs, with separate completed and denied counts and
median completion times. That makes administrative capacity visible, but it
does not show whether a completed deletion removed all relevant copies,
whether data later reappeared, or whether the person gained more practical
control. An opt-out completion is also not the same thing as deletion. See
the [Walmart privacy-request record](records/us-walmart-ccpa-privacy-request-completion-2025-2026.json).

California's DROP system adds a broader public route. CalPrivacy reports more
than 325,000 consumers using one verifiable request to reach more than 600
participating data brokers, with hashed matching, a 45-day broker-processing
clock, and statuses such as deleted, exempted, not found, opted out of sale,
or pending. That can reduce repeated effort and make partial failure easier to
see. But the public record does not show how many brokers matched each person,
which records were deleted, whether exemptions were appropriate, whether data
returned later, or whether any consumer gained durable privacy or trust. A
centralized route can therefore improve reach and observability while leaving
practical control unequal. See the [California DROP record](records/us-california-drop-data-broker-deletion-control-2026.json).

The FTC's GM/OnStar order adds a firm-enforcement version of the same problem.
The 20-year order requires routes for consumers to request connected-vehicle
data, seek deletion, opt out of collection, and—where the technology allows—
disable precise location collection. GM's published implementation still makes
those controls conditional on model year, verification, request channels, and
safety or emergency exceptions; using them may also affect valued connected
services. The public record does not show requests, completed deletion,
residual collection, or whether anyone switched vehicles or services. A legal
right can therefore improve contestability without becoming usable, verified,
and costless privacy. See the [GM/OnStar privacy-order record](records/us-ftc-gm-onstar-data-control-order-2026.json).

The data also show that correction is selective. A high appeal-success rate
only describes people who noticed the problem, knew how to contest it, had the
documents and time, and completed the route. People who could not appeal may
disappear from the success count. This is why [administrative legibility](US-BROAD-ADMINISTRATIVE-LEGIBILITY-LAYER_V1.md)
and [unequal contestability](US-BROAD-CONTESTABILITY-CAPACITY-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
are social and political issues, not merely paperwork problems.

GAO adds the system-level version. In its review of nine selected federal
eligibility data sources, all nine had some data-quality issue and seven had
cross-source inconsistencies; more than 100 possible federal sources exist,
but no government-wide interoperability authority had been designated when
the report was checked. A matching record can identify a question without
proving an improper payment or a person's ineligibility. Administrative
legibility therefore has two sides: the household must be recognizable to the
system, and the systems must be coherent enough to recognize it correctly.
See the [GAO eligibility-data record](records/us-gao-federal-eligibility-data-interoperability-2026.json).

GAO's September 2026 audit adds a government-wide knowledge version of the
same split. Treasury corrected errors in the final FY2025 consolidated
financial statements and closed three older recommendations, but GAO also
identified three new control deficiencies and six older recommendations that
remained open. The federal government can therefore publish a visible account
of itself while the procedures behind consolidation, disclosure, and access
control are still being repaired. This is not proof that every number is wrong
or that the state has collapsed; it is evidence that public visibility and
dependable public knowledge can recover on different clocks. The report does
not measure whether citizens changed trust, political judgment, or behavior.
See the [federal financial-reporting control record](records/us-gao-federal-financial-reporting-controls-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-federal-financial-reporting-control/findings/us-federal-financial-reporting-control-001.md).

GAO's broader federal-program review adds an outcome-measurement version of
the same problem. Programs may report money spent or people served while
lacking clear goals or data showing whether lives improved. In one selected
set of 15 programs serving pregnant women, young children, and their families,
12 had performance-management processes and three did not; the report also
says about one-third of federal managers in a 2020 survey had access to robust
evaluations. This is not proof that unmeasured programs failed. It shows that
public accountability can stop at activity unless the state can measure
results. The DHS Securing the Cities example is a counterpoint: after earlier
oversight, it developed goals, measures, milestones, and quarterly financial
assessments, though preparedness impact was still not measured here. See the
[federal program-effectiveness record](records/us-gao-federal-program-effectiveness-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-federal-program-effectiveness/findings/us-federal-program-effectiveness-001.md).

GAO's tax-rulemaking review adds a political-voice version of the same route.
Treasury and IRS generally opened comment periods, but hearing participation
still relied on dial-in technology, hearing comments were not always recorded,
and agencies lacked a policy for handling mass or AI-assisted comments. In a
sample of 10 proposed rules, comment periods had a median length of 61.5 days,
but comment volume ranged from five or fewer to 11,050. A formal invitation to
comment therefore does not show equal ability to participate, that comments
were legible to the agency, or that the final rule changed because of them.
The counterpoint is that alternatives analysis was used consistently in four
selected economically significant rules. See the [tax-rulemaking public
engagement record](records/us-gao-tax-rulemaking-public-engagement-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-tax-rulemaking-public-engagement/findings/us-tax-rulemaking-public-engagement-001.md).

GAO's Opportunity Zone review adds a place-and-firm version of the same
boundary. Qualified Opportunity Funds held more than $108 billion in assets at
the end of 2024, and nearly 9,000 designated Zones covered about 10% of the
population. Yet states and selected funds generally were not tracking specific
jobs, housing, or community outcomes. Among 24 states aware of investment, 14
said it affected jobs, housing, or both; that is a reported perception, not a
measured effect. The selected funds show real implementation—12 of 16 had
completed all planned projects by June 2026—but completion does not establish
affordability, job quality, displacement, or durable community security. See
the [Opportunity Zone record](records/us-gao-opportunity-zones-community-outcomes-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-opportunity-zones-community-outcomes/findings/us-opportunity-zones-community-outcomes-001.md).

The consumer-remedy records make the gap measurable without pretending it is
a recovery rate. In the FTC's September 2026 Amazon Prime update, about $845.2
million was reported as issued but about $235.5 million as accepted across
different payment phases. The figures do not reveal how many eligible
customers received money, how much each received, or whether the original
loss, time, or distrust was repaired. The CFPB Ombudsman's FY2025 report adds
the recipient-side friction: among 2,584 inquiries, people asked for address
updates, redress-list inclusion, and reissued payments, including one example
in which an expired check required escalation. A Credit Karma case similarly
shows that a refund can be issued after wasted application time and possible
credit-score harm without public evidence that the individual's credit access
or lost opportunity was restored. The broad societal pattern is that remedy
has its own route, effort, delay, and failure points; “the institution paid”
is not the same as “the person recovered.” These are separate case and
inquiry records, not one pooled consumer population.

The CFPB's complaint system adds a time-series version of the visibility gap.
Across published complaint records, the coded share marked as an explanation
fell from 90.7% in 2020 to 49.0% in 2024, while non-monetary relief rose from
6.2% to 50.1%; in 2025 the mix shifted again to 58.7% explanation and 40.6%
non-monetary relief. Separately, the CFPB's 2025 annual report says 97% of
complaints sent to companies were sent within one day and more than 99% got a
timely company response. That is strong evidence of a visible and fast
response pipeline, not proof of adequate correction: published records are not
unique consumers, response categories and product mix can change, and the
report does not show whether the underlying account, loss, or household
condition was repaired. See the [CFPB complaint-response trend record](records/us-cfpb-annual-response-trend-2020-2025.json).

A separate CFPB API-vintage check shows why those public categories need care.
Across four products and 2024–2025 samples, routing to companies was usually
within the same day, but the shares with a public company response and the
labels for explanation, monetary relief, and non-monetary relief differed by
product and year. The returned 2025 sample also lacked a narrative field that
was present in the 2024 schema. CFPB later said flaws in the complaint system
had limited its practical utility and announced standardized response,
identity, and quality controls. This does not prove that the changes improved
consumer correction; it shows that a complaint database is not a neutral
window. Field definitions, filtering, automation, and submission friction can
shape what becomes visible before any company fixes an account. See the [CFPB
case-route vintage record](records/us-cfpb-case-route-vintage-comparison-2024-2025.json).

The FleetCor/Corpay case adds a small-business version of the same gap. A
federal court found the company liable over hidden or unauthorized fuel-card
fees, and an appeals court upheld the judgment. The proposed FTC order would
make $100 million available for redress and impose clearer billing controls,
but the public record does not yet show the customer denominator, claim route,
payment receipt, restored cash flow, or whether affected firms could switch
providers. A judicial finding and a large redress fund can therefore recognize
real harm without yet showing business-level recovery. See the [FleetCor
redress record](records/us-ftc-fleetcor-unauthorized-fees-redress-2026.json).

The FTC's Publishing.com order adds a work-opportunity version of the same
route. The company was accused of selling e-book and audiobook programs with
substantial-income promises while placing refund conditions in terms that could
make recourse difficult or impossible. The final order requires a $1.5 million
payment and prospective rules for earnings claims, refund disclosure, prompt
handling, and reviewer incentives. The public announcement does not show what
any buyer paid, earned, claimed, received, or did next. A marketed route to
economic independence can therefore create both hope and exit friction, while
formal enforcement improves future rules without proving past recovery. See
the [Publishing.com earnings/refund record](records/us-ftc-publishingcom-earnings-refund-2026.json).

The Handy/Angi worker-redress case adds a later but still incomplete endpoint.
The FTC reports more than $2.7 million in checks sent to 62,893 eligible
consumers after alleged earnings claims and undisclosed fees or fines withheld
money from workers. Recipients were told to cash the checks within 90 days,
but the public follow-up does not report cashed, returned, or expired counts.
It also does not show net wage restoration, continued platform access,
alternative work, bargaining power, or exit. An identified recipient pool and
a mailed check are stronger than a proposed fund, but they still do not equal
worker recovery. See the [Handy worker-redress record](records/us-ftc-handy-worker-earnings-redress-2026.json).

The FTC's 2026 subscription cases add a consumer-culture version of the same
problem. The Shutterstock complaint alleges that renewal terms, consent, and
cancellation were not presented or designed clearly enough; the related Uber
One case alleges a cancellation flow of as many as 23 screens and 32 actions.
These are allegations in pending enforcement matters, not a national harm
rate, and some customers may knowingly keep a useful subscription. But they
show how ordinary inattention and the wish for continuity can be converted
into a billing relationship whose exit takes more time and effort than entry.
The broader issue is not simply deceptive design: it is whether people can
keep, question, or leave a service without paying an attention tax. See the
[FTC subscription record](records/us-ftc-shutterstock-subscription-cancellation-2026.json).

The New York transmission-refund case shows why visibility matters even when
there is a formal correction. Regulators reported a $13 million refund plus
interest, and a later explanation described pro-rata credits to monthly
transmission charges. But individual bill posting, customer allocation, and
receipt were not visible; some utilities might not itemize the credit
separately. A remedy can therefore exist in law and accounting while remaining
hard for a household to see, verify, or connect to its own bill. See the [New
York transmission-refund record](records/us-ny-psc-transmission-ratepayer-refund-2025-2026.json).

A linked Census fraud panel adds a related counterexample. Respondents who
reported a fraud loss and reported it to an agency later showed much more
difficulty paying an energy bill than respondents with no reported exposure
(about 44.9% versus 16.8% in the selected linked comparison). The eleven-person
cell that reported loss, reporting, and agency recovery still had a very high
later energy-payment-difficulty share (78.8%), while the institutional measures
did not move in one clean direction. This does not show that fraud caused the
later hardship: the fraud questions refer to the prior year, the incident date
and remedy timing are unknown, and the groups are selected valid-answer cells.
It does show why reporting or reported recovery cannot be treated as restored
household room or trust. See the [linked Census fraud follow-up record](records/us-htops-fraud-loss-following-trust-2025.json).

### 5. Technology and infrastructure expand faster than control and exit

AI systems, platforms, data centers, grids, energy projects, logistics, and
defense systems are adding capacity. They can provide real convenience,
information, jobs, resilience, or security. They can also make people,
communities, firms, and states more dependent on systems whose rules they do
not set.

The key question is not only whether the system works. It is whether users can
understand it, challenge it, replace it, afford it, and leave it without losing
something essential.

Pew reports that 49% of U.S. adults had used an AI chatbot in early 2026, while
70% expected their personal information to become less secure and 67% had
little or no confidence that government could regulate AI effectively. Use,
concern, and confidence therefore coexist; none alone measures dependence or
control.

GAO adds a government-side control gap. Its expert panel identified 10 selected
privacy challenges in federal AI use, but OMB guidance fully addressed only 2:
workforce skills and scaling AI with privacy protections. The other 8 included
data-use transparency, secondary use, consent, privacy-impact assessment, and
privacy-performance tradeoffs. This does not prove a breach or agency failure;
it shows that public AI capability and complete public control are developing
on different clocks. See the [federal AI privacy-guidance record](records/us-gao-ai-privacy-guidance-gaps-2026.json).

GAO's cloud-security review adds an operating-control version. Across eight
selected federal cloud systems, continuous monitoring was fully performed for
three, service-level agreements were fully implemented for five, and the other
three did not consistently define how performance would be measured or
enforced. Twelve corrective recommendations remained open. This does not show
a breach or outage; it shows that cloud capacity and provider dependence can
advance before the government can demonstrate who detects failure, who can
force correction, or how recovery will be measured. See the [federal cloud
security record](records/us-gao-federal-cloud-security-control-gaps-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-139.md).

FAA aviation cybersecurity adds a higher-stakes version. GAO reports that FAA
has identified spoofing, jamming, and other spectrum threats, but has not
completed all risk assessments or established real-time monitoring for every
threat. Only two of eight collaboration practices were fully addressed, and
nine recommendations remained open. This does not show an attack, outage, or
accident; it shows that a safety system can name a threat before it can see and
coordinate around it continuously. See the [aviation cybersecurity
record](records/us-gao-aviation-spectrum-cybersecurity-monitoring-gaps-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-141.md).

The newer AI-assistant choice study adds a more specific version of that
problem. Among its 1,999 U.S. adult users, about 43% said they deliberately
used more than one assistant for different tasks. Yet one service still held a
58.2% primary share, and task-specific choice did not mean durable switching
or a real alternative. The study also found that more than 80% were concerned
about how conversation data might be used, while roughly 60% did not know
whether their assistant trained on conversations. Knowledge of the policy was
more closely associated with reported protective tools than concern alone.

A peer-reviewed Nature experiment adds the clearest encounter-to-action bridge
so far. Participants randomly assigned to converse with an AI model advocating
for a candidate changed stated candidate preference and voting likelihood in
the U.S. presidential-election experiment; a Massachusetts experiment also
changed support for a ballot measure. The study reports short-run stated
political choice, not verified voting, durable belief, or population-scale
election effects. It also found that right-leaning candidate-advocating models
made more inaccurate claims across the three country contexts. Influence and
truthfulness therefore remain separate clocks. See the [AI voter-persuasion
record](records/us-nature-ai-dialogue-voter-persuasion-2024-2025.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-142.md).

GAO's homebuying proptech review shows the same issue in housing. Online
search, automated valuation, underwriting, and electronic closing can reduce
time and cost, while data collection, algorithmic steering, historical bias,
and opaque model inputs can make a housing decision harder to understand or
challenge. The report found product-specific oversight concentrated at FHFA,
with other agencies generally not focusing on the products directly. This is a
selected risk and oversight finding, not a national discrimination or privacy-
loss rate. See the [homebuying proptech record](records/us-gao-homebuying-proptech-oversight-fair-lending-2025.json).
The rental side shifts the control problem from buying to staying housed. GAO
reports that screening tools can use inaccurate or outdated records, rent-setting
software can reduce bargaining power, and facial recognition can create privacy
and misidentification risks. All 10 public housing agencies interviewed wanted
more specific guidance, while HUD guidance remained high-level. These are
selected risk and oversight findings, not national denial, rent, eviction, or
privacy-loss rates. See the [rental proptech record](records/us-gao-rental-proptech-screening-pricing-privacy-2025.json).
This suggests a cultural pattern of conditional agency: people adapt their
behavior inside a system they may distrust because convenience, habit, task
fit, price, and limited alternatives still matter. It is a survey and
hypothetical-choice result, not telemetry, a causal privacy estimate, or proof
of actual exit. See the [AI choice, trust, and privacy record](records/us-ai-assistant-platform-choice-trust-privacy-2026.json).

A UK survey supplies a useful comparative control layer. Among 1,067 daily
social-media users surveyed in 2023, more than 80% had used at least one safety
tool; among people aware of them, more than 85% had used unfollow and more than
80% had used blocking. But only 18% said they were always satisfied with the
outcome of reporting harmful content, while satisfaction with feed controls
was about 30%–35%. This separates the ability to act from confidence that the
platform will respond. It is UK evidence, based on self-report and a 2023
survey, not a U.S. estimate, platform-log measure, causal harm-reduction test,
or evidence of durable exit. See the [comparative platform-safety control
record](records/us-uk-platform-safety-control-satisfaction-2026.json).

Google's explicit-image removal release shows the same gap in a more concrete
U.S. user route. People can submit a simpler removal request, track its status,
receive change notifications, and opt into filtering for similar results. But
the product account reports no request volume, completion time, confirmed
de-indexing, recurrence rate, removal across other services, or survivor safety
outcome. The route is therefore easier to see and use without proving that the
image is gone or that repeated exposure stops. See the [Google image-removal
record](records/us-google-search-explicit-image-removal-control-2026.json).

The federal Take It Down Act adds a legal escalation layer. Covered platforms
must provide a clear process for nonconsensual intimate-image removal, act
within 48 hours of a valid request, and address known identical copies; the
FTC also provides a complaint route for platforms that fail to offer the
process or act. Guidance calls for an identifying number so the person,
platform, and law enforcement can refer to the same request. These rules make
recourse more contestable and traceable than a product button alone, but the
public record does not show request validity, platform compliance, complete
removal, recurrence, survivor safety, or exit. A legal deadline is therefore a
stronger route, not a verified lived outcome. See the [Take It Down Act
record](records/us-ftc-take-it-down-act-platform-removal-2026.json).

Pew's smartphone survey adds a personal self-control version of the same
problem. Fifty-three percent say they spend too much time on their phone, and
45% tried to cut back in the prior year. Among those who tried, only 25% say
they were highly successful. Yet 73% say the phone helps them stay informed and
51% say it helps them feel connected. The phone is therefore not simply a bad
technology people should abandon: its usefulness can be part of why reducing
use is difficult. These are self-reports, not device logs or proof of addiction,
health harm, durable behavior change, or exit. See the [smartphone
self-regulation record](records/us-pew-smartphone-self-regulation-2026.json).

A randomized customer-service experiment adds a different platform-control
boundary. AI suggestions helped agents respond faster and improved measured
customer sentiment, especially for less-experienced agents. The gains were
smaller for repeat complaints because the underlying delivery problem was still
there, and a fast AI-assisted human reply could feel worse after a customer had
already struggled with a chatbot. Better conversation is therefore not the
same as correction, refund, replacement, or a usable exit. See the [AI
customer-service record](records/us-hbs-ai-customer-service-contextual-recourse-2020-2021.json).

The wider cultural shift is that digital systems are becoming mixed social
infrastructure: people use them for information, work, news, companionship,
identity, and civic connection. That can help someone who lacks time,
transport, privacy, or a nearby institution. It can also move the work of
checking accuracy, protecting personal information, managing attention, and
finding a human alternative onto the user. The real political argument is
therefore less “technology or no technology” than who sets the terms, who
absorbs the error, who can challenge a decision, and whether leaving costs the
user something essential. The current sources compare these pieces across
different populations; they do not prove that platform use caused a change in
trust, isolation, persuasion, or political action. See the [platform and
digital-life subtheme synthesis](US-BROAD-PLATFORM-DIGITAL-SUBTHEME-SYNTHESIS_V1.md).

The World Happiness Report adds a collective-exit version. In a randomized
Facebook study, four weeks away was linked to better reported wellbeing and
lower later use, but also to less measured political knowledge. A separate
U.S. college-student study found that willingness to leave Instagram or TikTok
changed when everyone else was asked to leave too. People can therefore want
less platform use and still stay because connection, information, or fear of
missing out makes individual exit costly. The studies are separate and do not
show durable deletion, population-wide harm, or coordinated real-world exit.
See the [social-exit record](records/us-whr-social-media-deactivation-collective-exit-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-digital-habits-attention/findings/us-digital-habits-attention-016.md).

The FTC adds a material-risk version of the digital story. In 2025, people
reported $2.1 billion lost in scams where social media was the contact method,
with shopping ads, investment groups, romance contacts, job offers, and rental
listings using different forms of trust and targeting. These are reports, not
the full amount of fraud or proof that a platform caused each loss. Payment,
reporting, recovery, platform correction, and later trust remain separate.
See the [FTC social-scam record](records/us-ftc-social-media-scam-losses-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-digital-habits-attention/findings/us-digital-habits-attention-017.md).

AP-NORC's 2026 *State of the Facts* survey adds a more direct verification
problem. Trust in federal information fell from 2024 levels on topics including
cost of living, health care, and elections; 37% said it was difficult to know
whether information they encountered was true, and 43% said it was difficult
to judge whether candidates' statements were true. AI does not simply solve
that problem: 46% thought it could make complicated information easier to
understand, but only 20% said it was easy to verify AI-generated information
and 9% were highly confident that AI or chatbot information was reliable. This
is a cross-sectional survey of trust and perceived difficulty, not an accuracy
audit or a measured path to persuasion, voting, correction, or withdrawal. See
the [AP-NORC information-trust record](records/us-apnorc-state-of-facts-information-trust-2026.json).

Financial guidance shows the same conditional-use pattern outside general AI
chat. Among U.S. adults who sought financial guidance, 73% used internet
research, 35% used family, 32% used professional advisors, and 18% used AI.
Financially stressed guidance-seekers relied more on family than fulfilled
ones (43% versus 19%) and somewhat more on AI (21% versus 15%), even though no
more than 30% of U.S. adults reported at least some confidence in AI's
financial expertise. Need can widen the source mix before trust is settled.
This is a descriptive survey: it does not show advice quality, the decision
made, financial improvement, or human substitution. See the [financial
guidance record](records/us-gallup-financial-guidance-ai-trust-2026.json).

Among U.S. teens, AI use also separates practical help from emerging social
use. Pew reports information search for 57% of teens, schoolwork help for 54%,
casual conversation for 16%, and emotional support or advice for 12%. The
important cultural shift is not that every teen has an AI relationship; it is
that the same tool can sit beside school and information systems while also
becoming a low-friction place for conversation or advice. The survey does not
show dependence, quality, mental-health effects, privacy loss, or replacement
of human support. See the [Pew teen-AI record](records/us-pew-teens-ai-purpose-support-2026.json).

The companion 2025 Pew teen survey adds the exposure context. Among 1,458
U.S. teens ages 13–17, 64% had ever used a chatbot and 28% used one daily,
while 36% said they used at least one of five measured social platforms almost
constantly. Chatbot use was 68% among 15- to 17-year-olds and 57% among 13- to
14-year-olds; daily chatbot use was 35% among Black teens, 33% among Hispanic
teens, and 22% among White teens. These are self-reports, not logged minutes
or a dependence measure, and subgroup differences may reflect school, device,
family, language, or reporting conditions. The added cultural point is that
AI adoption is entering an already dense platform environment, with unequal
exposure and access routes. See the [teen social-AI exposure record](records/us-pew-teens-social-ai-2025.json).

An adult companion survey adds a deeper meaning layer. Among reported AI
companion users, 31% called an AI a friend, 59% said it gave them needed
support, 53% turned to it for advice about difficult people, 39% sometimes
told it things they would not tell others, and 38% said they would feel a
personal loss if the interaction ended. These are conditional self-reports
among a selected user group: they do not show loneliness reduction, human
relationship change, dependence, privacy consequences, or durable use. They do
show that AI is becoming a social-support surface whose cultural meaning can
matter before its longer-term effects are known. See the [AI companion social-
meaning record](records/us-ai-companion-social-meaning-support-2026.json).

The World Bank's 2026 AI framework adds a wider institutional lens: countries
may first adopt imported tools, then adapt them to local language, skills,
data, infrastructure, and institutions, and only later advance their own
capabilities. This explains why access can expand without producing local
control. Hardware, data centers, models, and applications remain concentrated
in a few countries and firms, while public agencies may use AI behind the
scenes without proving that front-end services became easier, fairer, or more
trusted. The report identifies a mechanism and policy questions, not a U.S.
productivity, welfare, or geopolitical-leverage estimate. See the [World Bank
AI capability record](records/us-world-bank-wdr2026-ai-capability-governance.json).

The PJM large-load record shows the infrastructure version in operation. A
reported July 2026 event disconnected about 4,000 MW of data-center load;
facilities switched to backup generation while operators managed the resulting
imbalance and voltage/frequency effects. PJM then proposed ride-through rules
for large loads. This moves data-center dependence from forecast risk into an
observed operating response and a rule-making response. It still does not show
which households experienced an outage, who paid, what emissions or local
burden resulted, or whether the proposed rules were adopted and worked. See
the [PJM large-load record](records/us-pjm-large-load-disconnection-response-2026.json).

The DOE-AEP Texas transmission loan shows the upstream version of the same
split. A closed financing commitment of up to $3.26 billion covers about 2,800
miles of transmission work and forecasts $685 million in electricity-cost
savings over 30 years for 1.1 million customers. Those are real financing and
project-planning stages, but the source does not show completed construction,
actual capacity, a retail-bill change, who receives the savings, reliability,
or whether the benefit lasts. Public investment can therefore make a future
benefit more likely without yet making that benefit visible to a household.
See the [DOE-AEP transmission record](records/us-doe-aep-texas-transmission-loan-customer-savings-2026.json).

The Champlain Hudson Power Express adds a stronger operating-stage example.
New York reports that the 339-mile cross-border line reached commercial
operation, with Hydro-Québec power expected to deliver 10.4 terawatt-hours a
year into the New York metro area—about 20% of New York City's expected energy
needs. The announcement also projects emissions reductions and more than 1,400
construction and operations jobs. Commercial operation is real infrastructure
delivery, but the public record does not show a customer's bill, rate-class
allocation, monthly delivery pattern, reliability change, host-community
acceptance, or durable cross-border leverage. A project can therefore be
operating and strategically important before its household bargain is visible.
See the [Champlain Hudson operating record](records/us-nyserda-champlain-hudson-power-express-operation-2026.json).

EPA and CDC's drinking-water records add an essential-service version of this
problem. About 90% of people receive water from community systems, while about
one in eight use private wells; CDC reports that about one in five sampled
private wells had a chemical at a potentially health-affecting level. EPA also
estimates about four million lead service lines and $625 billion in long-term
drinking-water infrastructure needs. These numbers describe coverage,
responsibility, inventory, and assessed need—not a household exposure,
replacement, bill, health, or safe-service outcome. A water system can be
formally available while the practical burden of testing, repair, or replacement
falls unevenly across places and households. See the [drinking-water
infrastructure record](records/us-epa-cdc-drinking-water-coverage-replacement-risk-2026.json).

The NBER *Water Works* study adds a measured place-to-health step: across 266
million readings from 48 states, pollution above current standards fell by
about half from 2003 to 2019, and the study estimates that Safe Drinking Water
Act loans reduced both pollution and mortality among older Americans. This is
stronger than an infrastructure-need count, but it still does not show which
household received cleaner water, paid for private protection, or changed its
trust or political behavior. See the [NBER water and health
record](records/us-nber-water-works-safe-drinking-water-health-2026.json).

California's wildfire-insurance record adds a housing-recovery version of the
same split. The state reports large claim payments, while a sampled State Farm
investigation found claim-handling violations and California enacted smoke-
damage standards. Payment and legal correction are real stages, but the public
record still does not show a property's completed repair, safe return, renewed
coverage, or a family's stay-or-move decision. See the [wildfire claims and
smoke-recovery record](records/us-california-wildfire-claims-smoke-recovery-2026.json).

The Census/NBER eviction study adds a measured housing-to-child route. In
linked Chicago and New York records, eviction orders increased moves,
homelessness, school absences, and school changes, and reduced high-school
graduation. The estimates are causal for the study's judge-assignment design,
but they are not a national effect or a finding about every informal move.
They show how a housing court event can become an education and family-life
shock. See the [eviction-to-child-outcomes record](records/us-census-nber-eviction-children-housing-school-outcomes-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-housing-insurance-risk/findings/us-housing-insurance-risk-010.md).

The emergency-rental-assistance lottery study supplies the counter-side. Five
programs in four cities increased rent payment and reduced eviction concerns,
with suggestive self-reported health improvements, but produced little measured
change in housing stability or financial distress during the pandemic-era
follow-up. Immediate relief and durable housing security therefore need
separate measures. See the [rental-assistance intervention
record](records/us-nber-emergency-rental-assistance-lottery-four-cities-2024-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-housing-insurance-risk/findings/us-housing-insurance-risk-011.md).

The New York housing-court study adds a process-side intervention. Free tenant
lawyers reduced possession judgments, money judgments, and eviction warrants in
the study's rollout design; the effect on warrants actually carried out was
smaller and less consistent. Legal help can therefore change a person's
position inside an institution without proving that rent became affordable or
that the same tenant stayed housed later. See the [tenant legal-representation
record](records/us-nber-nyc-tenant-legal-representation-housing-court-2016-2019.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-housing-insurance-risk/findings/us-housing-insurance-risk-012.md).

Rent-guarantee insurance adds a risk-transfer version of the housing problem.
An NBER model finds that rent guarantees could improve welfare, reduce large
deposits, and reduce homelessness, but says unrestricted access is not
financially viable. Private insurers would need to target better-off renters,
while public insurance would focus on households at highest risk. A provider's
public terms show the practical boundary: the renter pays an upfront,
non-refundable premium, the landlord is protected, and a claim paid to the
landlord can still leave the renter owing the provider. Lease access and
household protection therefore run on different tracks. See the
[rent-guarantee record](records/us-rent-guarantee-insurance-protection-repayment-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-rent-guarantee-insurance/findings/us-rent-guarantee-insurance-001.md).

### 6. Place decides whether a formal option becomes real

The same benefit, service, job, energy project, insurance route, medical
provider, or security policy can be usable in one place and costly or
unreachable in another. Housing supply, transport, local staffing, land,
utility rates, hazards, and political authority shape the available choices.

Place is therefore not background context. It distributes waiting, travel,
exposure, cost, safety, belonging, and the ability to stay or move.

The housing evidence makes this concrete. HUD estimates that voucher
lease-ups fell from about 65% of searches in 2018–2020 to about 58% in
2021–2022 while search times increased. California FAIR Plan policies grew
from 242,440 in September 2021 to 642,010 in September 2025, while insured
exposure grew even faster. Those figures show a real assistance and insurance
backstop, but not that households found affordable, safe, durable, or movable
housing. See the [housing and insurance lane](projects/us-housing-insurance-risk/README.md).

HUD's worst-case housing report adds the stock-side explanation. For every 100
extremely low-income renter households in 2023, about 66 units were affordable,
but only 38 were affordable and available and about 34 were also physically
adequate. A housing option can therefore exist in the national stock without
being vacant, reachable, safe, or usable by the household that needs it. These
ratios do not show a particular family's search, lease, stay, or move. See the
[HUD housing-stock record](records/us-hud-worst-case-housing-affordability-availability-adequacy-2023.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-housing-insurance-risk/findings/us-housing-insurance-risk-013.md).

GAO's preliminary Native housing testimony adds a federal-legibility layer.
Thirty federal programs can support Native housing, but nearly all Tribes and
Tribal Designated Housing Entities contacted said funding did not keep pace
with construction, rehabilitation, or maintenance needs. “Doubling up”—
temporarily staying with friends or family—may be widespread yet fail to fit
some federal homelessness definitions. Shared housing can be culturally
protective and still crowded or unstable; the report does not measure which
experience applies to which household. See the [Native housing finding](projects/us-gao-native-housing-homelessness/findings/us-gao-native-housing-homelessness-001.md).

Public safety adds another visibility boundary. BJS estimated 23.3 violent
victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older in 2024, but 11.2 per 1,000
were reported to police; about 48% of violent victimizations and 30% of
property victimizations were reported. Experience, reporting, police response,
remedy, safety, trust, and later action are separate stages. Nonreporting can
reflect fear, privacy, doubt that police can help, or a belief that the incident
is personal or too minor. See the [NCVS public-safety record](records/us-bjs-ncvs-victimization-police-reporting-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-safety-net-access/findings/us-safety-net-access-025.md).

The NCVS/NIBRS comparison adds a second measurement boundary. For 2023, the
comparable violent-crime-excluding-simple-assault rate was 4.42 per 1,000 in
NCVS victimizations reported to police and 3.88 per 1,000 in NIBRS recorded
incidents. Property crime was 30.61 per 1,000 households in NCVS and 20.15 per
1,000 persons in NIBRS, so those numbers cannot be subtracted. The two systems
answer different questions: one asks people what happened, while the other
counts incidents entered by participating law-enforcement agencies. See the
[NCVS/NIBRS record](records/us-bjs-ncvs-nibrs-complementary-crime-measures-2023.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-public-safety-measurement/findings/us-public-safety-measurement-001.md).

Campaign-finance records add a political-resource boundary. In the first 15
months of the 2025–2026 cycle, congressional candidates reported $2.1274
billion in receipts, parties $1.1208 billion, and PACs $6.3004 billion; these
are committee-level financial flows, not counts of donors, voters, persuasion,
or policy influence. See the [FEC campaign-finance record](records/us-fec-campaign-finance-2025-2026-15-month.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-political-participation/findings/us-political-participation-001.md).

Election administration adds the practical-participation side. EAC reported
nearly 65% of the citizen voting-age population participated in 2024 and more
than 158 million counted ballots, while states reported 1.736 million
provisional ballots, of which 25.1% were rejected; more than 98% of
jurisdictions used equipment with a paper or auditable record. Participation,
ballot conversion, access, trust, and legitimacy remain separate. See the
[EAVS record](records/us-eac-election-administration-voting-2024.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/us-election-administration/findings/us-election-administration-001.md).

The NLRB adds the worker-voice administration side. In FY 2025, the agency
received 19,754 unfair-labor-practice charges and 2,743 representation
petitions, including 2,633 petitions for secret-ballot elections. It found
32.8% of charges meritorious and settled 96.8% of meritorious cases, while the
Board lacked a quorum for nearly three-quarters of the year. Filing, merit,
settlement, decision, remedy, bargaining, and durable worker power are separate
stages. See the [NLRB record](records/us-nlrb-worker-voice-administration-fy2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-worker-voice-administration/findings/us-worker-voice-administration-001.md).

The EEOC adds a civil-rights recourse route distinct from labor bargaining. In
FY 2025, it received 88,201 new discrimination charges, resolved 90,743, and
reported more than $528 million in pre-litigation monetary relief; 49,807
charges remained pending at year end. Those outputs do not show individual
receipt, employer compliance, workplace change, retaliation, or later job
security. See the [EEOC record](records/us-eeoc-employment-discrimination-recourse-fy2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-employment-discrimination-recourse/findings/us-employment-discrimination-recourse-001.md).

GAO's disability-workforce audit adds an earlier public-employment gate. In
the reviewed programs, participants reporting disabilities had lower employment
and earnings after exit, while selected job centers reported difficulty with
interpreters and accessible outside training and DOL did not routinely
aggregate monitoring reports. This does not prove inaccessible services caused
the outcome gap; it shows that formal workforce eligibility, usable
accommodation, program completion, and durable job security are separate
stages. See the [disability workforce finding](projects/us-safety-net-access/findings/us-gao-disability-workforce-program-access-001.md).

GAO's working-adults analysis adds a household/employer version. About 13.8
million Medicaid-enrolled adults and 10.6 million adults living in SNAP-
receiving households worked at some point in 2024. Selected-state records also
showed recurring overlap between program participants and large employers. Work
and public-benefit reliance therefore belong in the same labor system, but the
source does not show inadequate wages, employer cost shifting, schedule
control, benefit churn, or worker choice. See the [working-adults finding](projects/us-safety-net-access/findings/us-gao-working-adults-medicaid-snap-employer-reliance-001.md).

The New York Fed's small-business survey adds a firm-capacity split: smaller
firms reported weaker revenue paths and pricing power while employment growth,
profitability, and debt moved on different measures and time windows. A firm
can keep or add workers without showing durable revenue, low debt, strong
pricing power, worker security, or local prosperity. See the [small-business
capacity finding](projects/us-local-business-place/findings/us-nyfed-small-business-capacity-001.md).

BJS jail data add a justice-exposure layer. At midyear 2024, local jails held
658,700 people; they reported 7.9 million admissions over the prior year, and
68% of people held were unconvicted. The Black jail incarceration rate was 566
per 100,000 residents versus 149 for white residents, even as the aggregate
jail population and admissions were below 2014 levels. Custody, conviction,
release, reentry, and later safety are separate. See the [BJS jail record](records/us-bjs-jail-pretrial-exposure-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-jail-pretrial-exposure/findings/us-jail-pretrial-exposure-001.md).

Transit data add a realized-infrastructure layer. FTA reports nearly 3,000
agencies provided 7.6 billion passenger trips in 2024, up 9.8%, and carried
passengers about 38.1 billion miles, up 8.7%; service depended on more than
380,000 workers and 177,000 vehicles. National service and use are real, but
local reach, fare, reliability, safety, and household mobility remain separate.
See the [FTA transit record](records/us-fta-ntd-transit-service-realization-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-transit-service-realization/findings/us-transit-service-realization-001.md).

The DOT SMART grant review adds an earlier transportation-technology stage.
The program received 1,073 applications and had announced 135 projects worth
about $289 million by September 2025, but most projects were not yet complete;
recipients reported procurement delays and DOT had not fully built a process for
learning from results. A public technology program can therefore create local
experimentation before it creates dependable service. The report does not show
who used a named project, whether it improved safety or reliability, or whether
residents could afford and reach the service. See the [SMART transportation
finding](projects/us-dot-smart-transportation-demonstration/findings/us-gao-smart-transportation-demonstration-2026-001.md).

The ports-of-entry review adds a state-capacity and public-private layer. CBP
handled a daily system of more than 1.15 million passengers and pedestrians and
88,500 containers through 328 ports in FY2024, while partners reimbursed
overtime or donated infrastructure and equipment. These arrangements can add
capacity without proving shorter waits, better inspections, lower costs, equal
access, or stronger public trust. The partnership route changes who helps fund
the margin of state service, but the report does not show who received the
benefit or carried the cost. See the [CBP port-partnership
finding](projects/us-cbp-port-public-private-partnerships/findings/us-gao-cbp-public-private-port-partnerships-2026-001.md).

The water-resilience review adds a place and public-health layer. Federal
agencies provided about $35 billion in grants and $29 billion in direct loans
for water infrastructure across 14 programs from 2014 to 2023, yet selected
utilities still faced local rate resistance, loan repayment, maintenance, and
public-visibility problems. A project can be funded without showing that a
household received affordable, continuous water during a disruption. See the
[water-resilience finding](projects/us-water-infrastructure-resilience/findings/us-gao-water-infrastructure-resilience-vulnerable-communities-2025-001.md).

The defense-technology release review adds a geopolitical permission layer.
Foreign partners can request sensitive U.S. technology, but decisions move
through specialized military and interagency authorities. Existing precedent
can speed most cases, while new requests may take days to a year; urgent
Ukraine cases moved faster by using overtime and pushing other work back. The
permission system is part of alliance capacity, but the report does not show
whether a named partner received, used, or trusted the resulting capability.
See the [technology-release finding](projects/us-technology-release-foreign-disclosure/findings/us-gao-technology-release-foreign-disclosure-2026-001.md).

The State priority-recommendations review adds a foreign-assistance control
layer. More than $30 billion in direct budget support to Ukraine came under
State oversight while GAO still highlighted unusual expenditure changes and
inconsistent investigation and reporting of potential security-assistance
violations. This is an oversight gap, not proof of misuse or battlefield
failure: delivery, safeguarding, investigation, correction, congressional
visibility, and strategic effect remain separate stages. See the
[foreign-assistance oversight finding](findings/us-gao-state-priority-recommendations-security-assistance-ukraine-2026-matched-evidence-001.md).

The security-cooperation workforce review adds the people behind geopolitical
capacity. DOD had completed many formal workforce provisions and identified
19,379 positions, but training-center vacancies, hiring constraints, and work
continuing through FY2028 remained. A commitment can therefore have policy,
equipment, and institutional language before it has enough trained and stable
people to sustain partner capability. This report does not show whether any
named partner became more capable or trusting. See the [workforce
finding](projects/us-security-cooperation-workforce/findings/us-gao-security-cooperation-workforce-2026-001.md).

The Navy robotic-autonomy review adds a capability-realization layer. Strategic
urgency and planned billions in enabling-technology investment can coexist with
leadership turnover, platform-centered acquisition, siloed domains, and
unclear portfolio roles. The public snapshot does not establish fielded RAS,
readiness, deterrence, or adversary response; it shows the organizational
middle that must be crossed first. See the [RAS organizational-capacity
finding](findings/us-gao-navy-robotic-autonomous-systems-organizational-capacity-2026-matched-evidence-001.md).

The GAO medical-device review adds a health-infrastructure version: accredited
private organizations can expand initial review capacity while FDA retains the
final market decision and the audit/correction clock remains incomplete. The
617 delegated reviews, concentration among two organizations, and audits taking
more than six months show an oversight dependency, not a device-safety or
patient-outcome estimate. See the [medical-device oversight finding](findings/us-gao-fda-third-party-device-review-audit-2026-matched-evidence-001.md).

The Coast Guard review adds a named-operation version of state-capacity
tradeoffs. During a maritime migration surge, migrant-interdiction hours rose
while drug-interdiction hours fell; foreign partners partly filled the gap in
another mission. That is not proof of failure or success: migrant interdiction
can be life-saving, partner substitution can be burden sharing, and the public
measure can miss actions completed by another agency. The broader point is that
emergency capacity is often moved from somewhere else. See the [Operation
Vigilant Sentry finding](projects/us-coast-guard-migrant-interdiction-tradeoffs/findings/us-gao-coast-guard-migrant-interdiction-tradeoffs-2026-001.md).

The NBER SNAP-office study adds a causal place mechanism to public-benefit
access. In one state's administrative data, SNAP participation in a census
tract fell about 7–9% over two years after a nearby enrollment office closed;
openings showed suggestive increases. A benefit can therefore remain legally
available while the local route to it changes participation. This does not show
national effects, food security, or household adequacy. See the [SNAP place-
access finding](projects/us-snap-enrollment-office-place-effect/findings/us-nber-snap-office-closure-participation-2025-001.md).

SIPRI adds a geopolitical-dependence layer. In 2021–25, the US supplied 42%
of global major-arms exports to 99 states; European imports rose 210%, with
48% supplied by the US, while European NATO imports rose 143% and 58% came
from the US. Transfer volume shows material dependence, not yet delivery,
operation, sustainment, political consent, or strategic control. See the
[SIPRI record](records/us-sipri-arms-transfer-dependence-2021-2025.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/us-arms-transfer-dependence/findings/us-arms-transfer-dependence-001.md).

The FTC's platform-data review adds a deeper digital-control boundary. Its
6(b) study of nine large social and video platforms found extensive collection
and retention involving users and non-users, broad sharing, and cases where not
all data were deleted after a request. A visible privacy route is therefore not
proof of actual deletion, downstream control, safety, or exit. See the [FTC
record](records/us-ftc-social-media-data-retention-control-2024.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/us-platform-data-retention-control/findings/us-platform-data-retention-control-001.md).

GAO adds an operational version of the alliance question. DOD has real
mechanisms and goals for integrating allies and partners into space operations,
but staffing gaps, overlapping responsibilities, difficult points of contact,
and missing implementation milestones can still limit usable coordination.
This means formal alliance strength and day-to-day shared capability are not
the same thing. See the [GAO space-coordination record](records/us-gao-space-allied-coordination-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-space-allied-coordination/findings/us-space-allied-coordination-001.md).

DOE's Transmission Facilitation Program adds an infrastructure-realization
layer. Federal capacity support and planned megawatts can be visible while the
current project page records later terminations or withdrawals for several
selected projects. Selection is not construction, energization, service, cost
recovery, reliability, or community benefit. See the [DOE transmission record](records/us-doe-transmission-facilitation-realization-2023-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-transmission-facilitation-realization/findings/us-transmission-facilitation-realization-001.md).

California's DROP system adds a digital-contestability layer. A resident gets
one request, an eight-digit tracking ID, broker-level statuses, and a recurring
processing route. That makes formal control more visible without proving
complete deletion, downstream contractor action, reappearance control, safety,
trust, or durable exit. See the [DROP record](records/us-cppa-drop-status-contestability-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-drop-status-contestability/findings/us-drop-status-contestability-001.md).

The Census CES volunteering study adds a material-place layer to civic life.
Across more than 990,000 CPS observations from 2002–2015, local disadvantage
and inequality were associated with lower volunteering; the Great Recession
dampened volunteering, and the earlier rural advantage disappeared afterward.
This does not mean community life vanished: informal help, belonging, political
action, and trust remain separate. See the [Census civic-capacity record](records/us-census-economic-place-civic-capacity-2002-2015.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-economic-place-civic-capacity/findings/us-economic-place-civic-capacity-001.md).

The USMCA labor case adds a cross-border remedy layer. A worker petition at a
Mexican facility moved through U.S.-Mexico review to reported reinstatement,
backpay, union access, facility rules, training, monitoring, and resumed tariff
liquidation. This shows geopolitical leverage operating through workplace
remedy, while receipt, non-retaliation, durable bargaining power, and wider
compliance remain open. See the [USMCA record](records/usmca-thyssenkrupp-rapid-response-labor-remedy-2025-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/usmca-rapid-response-labor-remedy/findings/usmca-rapid-response-labor-remedy-001.md).

OFAC adds a named-firm financial-infrastructure layer. A foreign company's
apparent Iran-sanctions violations moved through U.S.-dollar payment channels
to a $275 million settlement and a five-year compliance program. This shows
regulatory reach and correction, not yet later compliance, deterrence, changed
state behavior, or who absorbed the cost. See the [OFAC record](records/us-ofac-adani-sanctions-compliance-2023-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-ofac-adani-sanctions-compliance/findings/us-ofac-adani-sanctions-compliance-001.md).

School safety adds a youth-specific version of the same boundary. The joint
NCES/BJS report says at-school nonfatal victimization fell from 52 per 1,000
students ages 12–18 in 2012 to 22 per 1,000 in 2022. Yet in 2021–22, 3.8% of
students reported fear of attack or harm at school, 3.6% avoided activities or
classes because of that fear, and 2.2% stayed home. Aggregate improvement does
not prove equal lived safety or continued participation. See the [school-safety
record](records/us-nces-bjs-school-safety-fear-avoidance-2021-2022.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/us-school-safety/findings/us-school-safety-001.md).

GAO adds the building-infrastructure layer. In 2024–25, an estimated 19% of
public school districts canceled school for a facility issue, affecting about
2 million students and 3.5 million days of learning; 47% of districts had a
chronic facility issue in at least one school. Plumbing emergencies and extreme
classroom temperatures were among the reported causes. A closure can protect
students from unsafe conditions, so canceled days are not automatically lost
learning—but the report makes clear that the school building itself can become
the access bottleneck before student recovery, family work disruption, health,
or later opportunity are measured. See the [school-facility finding](projects/us-gao-school-facility-disruption/findings/us-gao-school-facility-disruption-001.md).

Broadband infrastructure adds a digital version of the same “available versus
usable” split. FCC reported that at year-end 2023 about 53% of households
subscribed to 100/20 Mbps fixed broadband when it was available; the denominator
already excludes households without qualifying availability. In its 2022
provider-choice comparison, 7.9% had no provider at that speed, while 37.4% had
one and 36.6% had two. Price, device access, installation, reliability, and
meaningful use remain open. See the [FCC broadband record](records/us-fcc-broadband-availability-adoption-choice-2022-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-digital-access/findings/us-digital-access-001.md).

The [place, public-systems, and political-feedback synthesis](US-BROAD-PLACE-PUBLIC-SYSTEMS-POLITICAL-FEEDBACK-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
connects this place effect to public routes and political capacity: the same
formal service can require different travel, waiting, documents, money, and
social support in different places, and the resulting burden may produce
contact, switching, staying, or non-use rather than one predictable political
reaction.

Transit adds a useful measurement boundary. DOT is developing a public
family-friendly dashboard around safety, cleanliness, accessibility, real-time
information, and reliability, while requesting public input. A separate
nine-intersection signal-priority prototype reports technical operation and
one-to-two-cycle recovery, but had not yet collected rider feedback. A system
can therefore become more measurable and technically responsive before anyone
has shown that families completed more trips, waited less, paid less, or found
the service accessible in practice. See the [DOT transit usability
record](records/us-dot-family-friendly-transit-dashboard-measurement-2026.json).

Solar for All supplies a useful counterexample and a useful warning. D.C.
program materials report nearly 10,000 households served and an average annual
savings figure, while a project-level filing reports every listed subscriber
receiving a monthly credit on four selected project rows. That is stronger than
a promise or a capacity announcement. But the public record still does not
show each household's bill, net savings, or continued participation. A related
billing audit could not resolve customer-level crediting because required data
were not provided in time, and a proposed error-rate correction is not the same
as a verified later bill credit. The same public program can therefore produce
real implementation evidence and still leave correction and durability partly
unseen. See the [Solar for All household-savings record](records/us-doee-solar-for-all-household-bill-savings-2025-2026.json),
[monthly-credit record](records/us-dcpsc-solar-for-all-monthly-credit-receipt-2025.json),
and [billing-correction record](records/us-dcpsc-solar-credit-error-rate-remedy-2025.json).

Virginia's data-center debate shows a different household-facing stage. An
official company proposal described $10 monthly residential credits for four
years, while the State Corporation Commission described an approved allocation
framework in which a typical 1,000-kWh customer's modeled monthly increase fell
from $2.90 to $0.94. This makes cost sharing and political legitimacy more
visible than a capacity forecast. But the record does not show a posted bill,
customer eligibility, credit receipt, net savings, reliability improvement, or
durable affordability. A proposed credit and a modeled charge are therefore
routes toward a public bargain, not proof that households received one. See
the [Virginia data-center customer-relief record](records/us-virginia-data-center-residential-bill-relief-package-2026.json).

Georgia's large-load docket adds a further realization stage. After the state
created special terms, minimum-billing, and cost-protection rules for very
large customers, the PSC docket showed multiple executed large-load contract
filings and an active review process in 2026. That proves the governance route
reached real contracts, but not the contract terms, payment, energization,
residential bills, reliability, or public acceptance. See the [Georgia
large-load contract record](records/us-georgia-psc-large-load-contract-realization-2026.json).

Federal telecommunications adds a direct service-continuity test. GSA managed
the transition of 221 agencies from old contracts to a replacement contract,
but the transition ran more than three years late and old contracts had to be
extended. For six selected agencies, legacy-contract billing rose 206% from
February to March 2025. GAO also found that GSA did not know which agencies
had experienced disruptions. A replacement route and a continuity extension
are therefore visible before actual outage, quality, mission, or user effects
are known. See the [federal telecommunications transition record](records/us-gao-federal-telecom-transition-service-disruption-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-138.md).

DOE's Clean Energy Connector shows an earlier household-benefit stage. The
program links LIHEAP eligibility to an opt-in community-solar subscription,
uses a subscription manager to complete enrollment, and requires the
household's financial benefit to exceed participation cost by at least 20%.
That is a more concrete protection than a general promise, but the public page
does not report eligible households, completed opt-ins, actual bill credits,
subscription fees, persistence, or energy-burden change. Eligibility and a
minimum-savings rule can improve the terms of access without proving that a
household received usable savings. See the [Clean Energy Connector
record](records/us-doe-clean-energy-connector-low-income-bill-credit-2024-2026.json).

The place comparison adds a warning against treating growth as a civic ladder.
Across county groups sorted by population change, federal trust ranged from
41.9% in the lowest-growth group to 35.3% in the highest-growth group, while
reported civic action was nearly unchanged at 38.1% and 37.3%. A separate set
of county cells combining health/social-assistance establishment density with
zero-vehicle-household share also showed ranges rather than a simple better-
place pattern. These are context-linked descriptive comparisons, not evidence
that growth, service capacity, or mobility caused trust or action; county
capacity is not the same as a respondent's usable service. See the [place
capacity and belonging record](records/us-place-capacity-growth-belonging-action-crosssource-2024-2026.json).

### Status and belonging are not the same as presence

People can be present in a place, economy, neighborhood, or institution while
still facing uncertain recognition, safety, language access, or political
voice. In Pew's 2025 National Survey of Latinos, 61% said Hispanic identity was
very or extremely important, while 33% said it hurt their ability to get ahead,
26% said it helped, and 34% reported at least one selected identity-based
unfair treatment in the prior year. The same identity can therefore be a
source of connection and a source of exposure.

The Muslim-American record shows a parallel institutional pattern: population,
mosques, and congressional representation have grown over time, while public
suspicion and reported discrimination remain. ANES immigration data likewise
show that people can favor a legal citizenship pathway while preferring lower
immigration levels. Inclusion, scale, threat, identity, and political action
are separate judgments.

The broad insight is that membership has to be measured through treatment,
usable services, safety, voice, and the ability to participate—not inferred
from population presence or formal representation. See the [migration and
place synthesis](projects/us-immigration-local-demand/migration-place-cultural-conversion-synthesis-v1.md),
[Hispanic identity record](records/us-pew-hispanic-identity-advantage-barrier-2026.json),
and [Muslim-American record](records/us-pew-muslim-americans-25-years-2026.json).

AAPI Data/AP-NORC adds a separate national-identity and exposure surface. In
its April 2026 probability-panel survey, AAPI adults strongly valued the
American Dream (80%), a democratically elected government (79%), and mixing
cultures and values from around the world (73%), yet 64% said the United States
used to be a great place for immigrants but is not anymore. Fifty-one percent
said they or someone they know had carried documentation, changed travel
plans, altered daily routines, or faced detention/deportation because of
immigration status. This is a bounded finding about pluralist ideals coexisting
with status-related caution—not evidence of legal-status composition,
discrimination prevalence, policy causation, migration, or one AAPI political
bloc. See the [AAPI Data/AP-NORC record](records/us-apnorc-aapi-immigration-status-identity-2026.json).

The Urban Institute's 2025 Well-Being and Basic Needs Survey adds the missing
nonuse surface. Among 2,234 adults in immigrant families, 18% reported that
they or someone in the family avoided at least one listed essential activity
because of immigration concerns. The reported routes include work, driving,
health care, and religious or community participation. Mixed-status families
also reported substantially more worry about doctors, work, children's school
or child care, and police contact than immigrant families without undocumented
members; 12% reported avoiding safety-net programs over green-card concerns and
9% over data-sharing concerns.

This supports a stronger but still bounded chain: **status-related concern can
translate into family-level avoidance of work, care, community, and benefits**.
The source does not identify the exact encounter, legal status, agency action,
forgone benefit, or recovery, so it remains a conversion surface rather than a
closed causal episode. See the [Urban immigrant-family record](records/us-urban-wbns-immigrant-family-disengagement-services-2025.json).

A Census Center for Economic Studies working paper adds a causal public-safety
boundary. Studying Secure Communities, it reports reduced crime reporting by
Hispanic victims and increased offending against Hispanics, while aggregate
reported crime stayed unchanged because the two movements offset one another;
the paper estimates a 7.9% increase in offending for a 10% decline in reporting.
This means a flat administrative crime total can conceal a reporting-and-safety
change. The result is historical and policy-specific, not a universal estimate
of enforcement, victimization, trust, or political behavior. See the [Census
enforcement-reporting record](records/us-census-immigration-enforcement-victim-reporting-public-safety-2026.json).

The economic side of migration adds a useful counterweight to identity-only
accounts. An older Census study estimates that each immigrant created about 1.2
local jobs for local workers, mostly in non-traded services. A newer study of
the 2021-2024 surge models labor-supply and consumer-demand effects as roughly
offsetting at the national inflation level. Neither result tells us whether a
particular place has enough homes, schools, clinics, transit, or social trust.
Local demand, national prices, and lived belonging therefore remain separate
questions. See the [immigration local-demand record](records/us-nber-immigration-local-demand-supply-place-1980-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-immigration-local-demand/findings/us-immigration-local-demand-001.md).

Working-class identity adds a status-and-meaning layer that should not be
collapsed into income or occupation. Pew found that 60% of adults said the
label described them well in 2026, up from 54% in 2024; the share was 67% among
Republicans and Republican leaners, 55% among Democrats and Democratic leaners,
and 66% among adults without a college degree. It was also common among middle-
income adults (67%) and among people reporting high financial stress (61%), but
the survey shows that the identity crosses comfortable and strained positions.
Blue-collar workers were more likely than other workers to use the label (77%
versus 61%), yet occupation does not establish that identity caused political
views or that the label measures hardship. The cultural pattern is that class
language can organize dignity, belonging, and political interpretation across
different material positions. See the [working-class identity record](records/us-pew-working-class-identity-2026.json).

### 7. Political action is a menu, not a ladder

Material pressure and institutional encounters can lead to voting, contacting,
searching, complaining, organizing, switching, protest, silence, non-use, or
withdrawal. People can distrust an institution and still act, or participate
socially without taking direct political action.

The newest external-data rotation sharpens this into a source-led subtheme:
**plural authority under weak shared national confidence**. Gallup's 2026
institution survey puts average confidence in 14 major institutions at 27%,
while Pew's public-life study finds four distinct engagement profiles rather
than one ladder from engaged to disengaged. Mobilizers and Connectors report
87% voting in 2024, compared with 49% among Spectators and 41% among
Outsiders; contacting elected officials ranges from 65% to 3%. Federal-
government trust remains low across the groups even as news trust varies more.

Census HTOPS adds a social-capacity layer: always/usually obtaining social-
emotional support rises from 47.8% among adults ages 18–34 to 76.1% among
adults 65+, and regular religious and club participation also rises with age.
Gallup/Kettering adds an entry-route counterexample: among would-be community
participants with ample free time, 54% are unsure how to get involved and 51%
say they were not invited or encouraged. These are separate descriptive
surfaces, not one causal pathway. They suggest that civic action depends on
authority, social support, information, invitation, and efficacy—not only on
available hours.

National democratic frustration is also not identical to local judgment. In
the 2026 Gallup/Kettering survey, 56% rated U.S. democracy poorly and only 19%
well, while 34% rated democracy in their own city or town well and 21% poorly.
The comparison does not prove local institutional performance or a transfer of
legitimacy, but it makes scale a necessary part of the political-meaning
analysis. See the [external-data first-pass memo](US-BROAD-EXTERNAL-DATA-FIRST-PASS_2026-09-25.md)
and its linked [HTOPS](records/us-census-htops-social-support-community-authority-2026.json),
[Pew engagement](records/us-pew-public-life-engagement-groups-2025.json),
[Gallup confidence](records/us-gallup-institutional-confidence-2026.json),
[Gallup/Kettering democracy](records/us-gallup-kettering-democracy-local-national-legitimacy-2026.json),
and [community-involvement](records/us-gallup-community-involvement-information-invitation-2026.json)
records. Pew's same-survey neighbor study adds a small conversion bridge:
trusters were more likely than distrusters to say they would help a neighbor
(82% versus 67%) and conserve water or electricity after a public official's
request (78% versus 64%). These are stated intentions, not observed action or
institutional performance. See the [neighbor-trust/public-request
record](records/us-pew-neighbor-trust-public-request-2025.json).

The Ohio State IDEA constituent-engagement survey adds a congressional version
of the same gap. While 72.1% said people like them would like to discuss
important issues with Congress, 21.7% reported contacting or trying to contact
a member in the prior year. Among noncontact respondents, 40.5% said members
do not care about concerns like theirs, 37.1% cited difficulty, 34.7% expected
members would not change their minds, and 21.7% cited time cost. Perceived
representative interest (64.9%) and trust in Congress (14%) are separate
judgments; the survey does not observe receipt, substantive response, issue
resolution, or later action. See the [IDEA constituent-engagement
record](records/us-osu-idea-constituent-engagement-responsiveness-2025.json).

The historical Ohio State town-hall experiments provide a bounded causal
counterexample to treating low trust as an immutable barrier. Randomized
invitations to real-time online interaction with Members of Congress increased
trust and approval; in the second study, vote intention rose 10.5 points and
reported post-election vote rose 13.1 points, though the latter estimate was
weaker statistically. These effects apply to selected historical participants
and do not establish ordinary constituent-service response, issue resolution,
or present-day national generalization. See the [congressional town-hall
field-experiment record](records/us-osu-congressional-townhall-field-experiment-trust-vote-2006-2008.json).

A related telephone-town-hall experiment separates the forum from the
representative. Among 1,005 enrollees, only 222 completed both waves; attendance
improved platform evaluations by about 6% of the scale and Member trust/approval
by about 4%. A single-topic, briefing-supported design improved evaluations of
the forum by about 5% relative to the standard design, but did not materially
improve Member attitudes. This suggests that procedural quality can be a real
outcome without being equivalent to policy responsiveness or durable trust. The
study is local, highly attrited, selected, and lacks a true no-event control.
See the [telephone-town-hall field-experiment record](records/us-osu-telephone-townhall-field-experiment-platform-member-trust-2019.json).

The response layer is measurable but still incomplete. A randomized audit of
32,398 policy emails sent to state legislators found replies to 17.3% of
human-written messages and 15.4% of GPT-3-generated messages. The small gap
shows that synthetic correspondence can reach the office-response stage at
nearly the same rate as human text, but response rate and word count do not
show authentic constituent receipt, substantive staff work, policy change, or
issue resolution. This is a state-legislator policy-email audit from 2020, not
a present-day congressional or service-request estimate. See the [AI
constituent-email record](records/us-cornell-ai-constituent-email-legislative-response-2020.json).

A necessary counterexample is that class inequality is not uniform across the
whole route. Four randomized correspondence experiments covering 4,492 routine
casework messages found replies to 16.6% of less-affluent-coded senders and
15.8% of more-affluent-coded senders, a statistically insignificant difference
with a tight 1.32-point pooled bound. This does not erase the donor-status access
effect above: equal reply rates after a routine request are not equal ability to
ask, equal seniority of access, equal service quality, or equal policy
representation. See the [casework-class record](records/us-carnes-holbein-casework-social-class-response-2012-2013.json).

Access quality can diverge before a response is even given. In a randomized
experiment across 191 House offices, otherwise identical meeting requests
produced Member-or-Chief-of-Staff access in 2.4% of cases when attendees were
described as constituents and 12.5% when they were described as campaign
donors. Any senior-staff access was 5.5% versus 18.8%, and scheduled meetings
were verified as held. This is strong evidence for a resource-cue-to-access
effect in an organized advocacy setting, but it does not establish a policy
result, quid pro quo, or current ordinary-constituent baseline. See the
[congressional-access record](records/us-kalla-broockman-campaign-donor-congressional-access-2013.json).

The strongest policy-stage bridge in this pass is an information experiment. A
survey of 10,690 New Mexico voters produced district-specific opinion estimates;
half of the 70-member House was randomly given its district results before a
tax-rebate vote. Treated legislators' votes tracked district opinion, while the
control relationship was near zero; 84.4% of controls voted yes versus 68.6% of
treated legislators overall, with the contrast concentrated in low-support
districts. This demonstrates information-to-vote responsiveness, not ordinary
constituent contact, implementation, material benefit, or durable trust. See
the [constituency-information record](records/us-butler-nickerson-constituency-information-legislative-vote-2008.json).

The New Mexico episode can be followed one step further, but only to formal
program design. Official bill text enacted a refundable personal-income-tax
credit for eligible 2007 filers with income, filing-status, and dependent-based
amounts, while the Legislative Council Service summarized the package as less
than $60 million. This closes vote → enacted benefit rule for a named episode;
it does not show that the information treatment caused enactment, that people
claimed or received the credit, or that the payment was timely or adequate. See
the [SB24 vote-to-implementation record](records/us-new-mexico-sb24-vote-to-refundable-credit-implementation-2008.json).

The fiscal-impact report adds a planned administrative route after enactment. It
estimated 786,500 eligible returns, $87.6 million in total benefits, an average
$111 benefit, and roughly 20% of filers excluded by income thresholds. It also
specified refund or liability-reduction processing, checks, outreach, and a
highlighted tax form. These are projections and plans; they do not establish
claims, payment timing, take-up, adequacy, or household relief. See the [SB24
fiscal-impact record](records/us-new-mexico-sb24-fiscal-impact-planned-distribution-2008.json).

The firearm-safety study adds a cultural version of “same goal, different
route.” Owners and non-owners both describe safety as important, but they see
lethal firearms, non-lethal alternatives, and no weapon as different ways to
produce it. Information moved storage and alternative-tool preferences without
making current owners want to give up lethal firearms. This is a study of
reported beliefs and stated preferences, not proof of actual safety or violence
reduction. See the [firearm-safety finding](projects/us-firearm-safety-means/findings/us-nber-firearm-safety-means-2026-001.md).

The Pew Religious Landscape Study adds a community-institution layer. Latter-day
Saint adults reported more volunteering and generalized trust than U.S. adults
overall, but education and regional differences inside the group were large.
This suggests that community institutions can create real social resources
without giving every member the same time, role, access, or experience. The
survey does not show that membership caused the outcomes or that volunteering
was equally helpful to others. See the [religious-community finding](projects/us-religious-community-trust/findings/us-pew-latter-day-saints-communal-trust-volunteering-2026-001.md).

The Senate peer-proximity study adds a small but concrete social mechanism:
repeated physical contact can change legislative voting even when formal rules
remain the same. The paper reports the strongest effects among close assigned
peers and a larger cross-party effect on final votes. This is evidence about
Senators in a special institution, not proof that contact changes ordinary
voters, private beliefs, public trust, or lasting coalitions. See the [NBER
peer-proximity finding](projects/us-senate-peer-proximity-voting/findings/us-nber-senate-peer-proximity-voting-2026-001.md).

Information itself is not one shared pipeline. Pew finds that Americans name
different main sources for different topics: news media lead on foreign policy
(51%), immigration (46%), and the economy (44%); government sources are nearly
as prominent as news for Social Security (31% versus 31%); and friends, family,
school, or social media occupy different positions depending on the question.
This suggests that public judgment is assembled through topic-specific source
ecologies rather than one common information environment. The survey does not
show accuracy, trust quality, persuasion, or later action. See the [Pew
information-source record](records/us-pew-information-source-topic-diversity-2026.json).

Pew's 2026 electorate analysis adds a status-to-voice boundary. The projected
eligible electorate is becoming more racially and ethnically diverse: the
White share falls from 74% in 2006 to 61% in 2026, while the Hispanic share
rises from 9% to 16% and the Asian share from 3% to 5%. But eligibility is not
registration, voting, representation, or policy influence. In 2024, 52% of
Hispanic adults and 60% of Asian adults were eligible voters, compared with
81% of White adults and 74% of Black adults. These are age-and-citizenship
differences, not a measure of political commitment or exclusion. The broader
insight is that demographic presence changes the political field before it
proves usable political power. See the [electorate composition
record](records/us-pew-electorate-racial-ethnic-composition-2026.json) and
[bounded finding](projects/us-cost-trust-politics/findings/us-cost-trust-politics-067.md).

Pew's 2026 press-freedom study adds a legitimacy conflict inside that
information environment. Seventy-one percent of adults described the
government-news relationship as bad, but 55% also said news organizations have
too much political power; that view was held by 69% of Republicans and 43% of
Democrats. At the same time, 68% said journalists criticizing politicians was
acceptable, compared with 56% saying politicians criticizing journalists was
acceptable. The pattern is not one shared trust score: people can diagnose a
relationship as broken while assigning blame, power, and permissible criticism
differently. These are perceptions and norms, not measures of censorship,
accuracy, retaliation, or later political action. See the [press-government
legitimacy record](records/us-pew-press-government-media-legitimacy-2026.json).

The companion press-freedom study adds a second tension: 73% said press
freedom was extremely or very important, yet 53% preferred preventing
inaccurate reporting even if press freedom had to be limited, while 45%
preferred freedom even if some reporting was inaccurate. Perceived freedom
also fell from 33% in 2024 and 2025 to 28% in 2026, while high concern about
restrictions rose from 41% to 46%. The partisan pattern reversed with the
political context: 41% of Republicans and Republican leaners said the press was
completely free, compared with 18% of Democrats and Democratic leaners; high
concern was 29% versus 64%. These are normative perceptions, not a legal or
content audit. The broader insight is that support for a principle can coexist
with conditional limits and changing judgments about who currently threatens
it. See the [press-freedom limits record](records/us-pew-press-freedom-limits-2026.json).

Gallup's June 2026 confidence survey adds a firm-and-government comparison.
Average confidence across 14 institutions was only 27%, but confidence was
67% for small business and 20% for large technology companies. Republicans
also averaged 13 points more confidence than Democrats, with a 70-point gap on
the presidency. The useful pattern is not simply that Americans distrust
institutions: confidence is selective, tied to political control, and can
coexist with continued use or dependence. These are reported judgments, not
measures of performance, access, switching, compliance, or political action.
See the [Gallup institutional-confidence record](records/us-gallup-institutional-confidence-2026.json).

The NBER employer-power study adds a political-economy route. Workers at major
local employers were more likely to report employer political communication and
influence on voting behavior and career expectations. A separate shift-share
analysis reports that greater electoral monopsony power increased Republican
turnout. This does not mean a specific message changed a specific worker's
vote: job dependence, reported influence, modeled mechanism, and actual voting
remain different evidence stages. See the [employer political-influence
record](records/us-nber-employer-market-power-political-influence-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-employer-political-influence/findings/us-employer-political-influence-001.md).

Pew's public-life engagement analysis adds a different cultural distinction:
participation is not one ladder from inactive to active. Its four descriptive
groups—mobilizers, connectors, spectators, and outsiders—had very different
levels of high political interest: 58%, 43%, 28%, and 9%, respectively. Yet
trust in the federal government was low across all four groups, ranging from
17% to 22%, while trust in national news varied more, from 45% among outsiders
to 62% among mobilizers. The groups are built from selected activity and
attention measures, so they are not fixed identities or causal stages. The
bounded insight is that public participation, government trust, and news trust
can combine in several ways rather than forming one civic progression. See the
[public-life engagement record](records/us-pew-public-life-engagement-groups-2025.json).

Identity, information, safety, time, social support, and belief in efficacy
shape which response is possible. Hardship does not automatically become
anger, a party choice, or collective action.

Non-use deserves its own category. The migration and public-benefit evidence
reports families avoiding benefits, clinics, work, police, religious events,
or community routes because visibility may carry legal, social, financial, or
safety risk. Avoidance can protect immediate control while sacrificing care,
income, food, belonging, or future recourse. It is not automatically apathy,
distrust, or free choice. See the [protective non-use synthesis](US-BROAD-PROTECTIVE-NONUSE-CROSS-DOMAIN-SYNTHESIS_V1.md).

The Trust for Civic Life's 2025 rural community survey adds a local-national
counterexample. Among people reached through 24 Civic Hub networks, trust in
local organizations averaged about 70%, compared with about 25% for national
organizations; 90% said they could make a positive difference locally, while
only 11% said national leaders made their voice matter. Among the small
returning subgroup, national political trust fell sharply from 2024 to 2025
while local trust, agency, and belonging held steady. The report also links
agency more closely to taking meaningful responsibility in problem-solving
groups than to simply accumulating volunteer hours. This is a selected,
network-recruited rural sample, not a representative or causal estimate. It
shows that national political distance can coexist with local civic capacity.
See the [Trust for Civic Life record](records/us-trust-civic-life-rural-local-agency-2025.json).

The Census/AmeriCorps civic-engagement supplement adds a reach-versus-time
split. Formal volunteering rose to 28.3% in 2023 and informal neighbor help
reached 54.2%, but mean annual hours per formal volunteer fell from 96.5 in
2017 to 70 in 2023, with the median falling from 40 to 24. The simple story
"civic engagement recovered" is therefore incomplete: more people may touch
civic life while the typical time commitment is thinner. This does not show
durable organization, political efficacy, local repair, or later action. See
the [Census/AmeriCorps civic participation record](records/us-census-americorps-civic-reach-time-2023.json).

BLS's 2025 time-use release adds the everyday cultural layer beneath those
participation measures. On an average day, 30.4% of people age 15 and older
socialized or communicated, 9.4% took part in religious or spiritual activity,
and 5.3% volunteered. These categories have different weekday/weekend timing;
they should not be collapsed into one measure of community or civic health. See
the [cultural-time record](records/us-bls-atus-cultural-civic-time-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-aging-care-strain/findings/us-aging-care-strain-020.md).

The NEA's analysis of four 2024 Census Household Pulse cycles adds a cultural
infrastructure layer. About 25.2% of adults attended a live performance or art
exhibit in the previous month, 16.3% created or practiced art, and 62.2% agreed
their community had plenty of arts opportunities. Attendance was 16.2% among
adults earning under $25,000 versus 55.2% among those earning $200,000 or more;
arts attendees also reported needed social support more often. This is an
association, not proof that arts attendance causes connection. See the [NEA
arts record](records/us-nea-hps-arts-attendance-social-connectedness-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-cost-trust-politics/findings/us-cost-trust-politics-066.md).

Political action also needs a place, not just a grievance. CIRCLE reports that
young people with access to a “political home”—a setting that can provide
belonging, information, food, transportation, or compensation—showed civic
action and civic self-efficacy about 20 percentage points higher than peers
without that access. That is an association, not proof that the setting caused
participation, but it makes the social infrastructure visible. ANES provides a
different cultural lesson: respondents can favor a legal citizenship pathway
while preferring immigration levels to stay the same or fall. People are not
one-position political types; they combine inclusion, scale, economic judgment,
identity, trust, and perceived control. Action depends on whether a credible
route and meaningful efficacy exist, not on hardship or identity alone. See the
[CIRCLE civic-capacity record](records/us-circle-political-homes-youth-civic-capacity-2026.json)
and [ANES immigration-meaning record](records/us-anes-immigration-meaning-action-2024.json).

A Gallup/With Honor survey of planned 250th-anniversary recognition adds a
different cultural route to legitimacy. Among adults planning to celebrate,
66% intended to spend time with family or friends, 32% intended to attend a
neighborhood or community event, and 17% intended to attend an official
America250 program. Among the subgroup planning civic or educational
recognition, history learning and listening to personal stories were more
common routes than formal ceremony. This suggests that national meaning is
often made through ordinary relationships, local settings, and stories before
it becomes an official institutional experience. These are nonexclusive plans,
not observed attendance, political agreement, learning, or durable identity;
family celebration can also coexist with disagreement about the nation's
history and institutions. See the [Gallup anniversary meaning record](records/us-gallup-america250-social-ritual-meaning-2026.json).

AP-NORC's America 250 poll adds a principle-versus-threat split. Freedom of
speech, voting, and religion were widely described as important to American
identity, yet many respondents also saw those rights as threatened. The
American Dream was more divided: only one-third said it still holds, while
belief varied sharply by party and age. The flag was more often seen as
unifying than divisive, but half of adults said they never display it at home.
Shared ideals can therefore remain culturally important without producing one
shared account of present reality, national promise, or political action. These
are cross-sectional identity judgments, not legal, mobility, or civic-action
measures. See the [AP-NORC America 250 record](records/us-apnorc-america-250-identity-rights-dream-2026.json).

Pew's 2026 political typology adds why party labels are too blunt for this
meaning work. It sorts adults into nine value-based groups rather than one
left-right line: the Unconventional Right is 23% of adults, the Order and
Opportunity Left is 18%, and the low-attention Tuned-Out Middle is 9%. Even
within the right, support for making abortion illegal ranges from 83% in Faith
First Conservatives to 43% in the Unconventional Right. Foreign-policy values
also cross-cut: 71% say U.S. military primacy matters while 62% say the U.S.
should consider allies' interests even when compromise is required. This is a
cluster-derived descriptive map, not a causal account of votes or behavior. See
the [Pew coalition typology record](records/us-pew-political-typology-values-coalitions-2026.json).

Religion adds a related but distinct legitimacy boundary. Pew's April 2026
survey finds that 37% say religion is gaining influence in American life, up
from 18% in 2024, and 55% have a net positive view of religion's influence.
The partisan difference is large: 75% among Republicans and Republican leaners
versus 38% among Democrats and Democratic leaners. Yet 79% oppose houses of
worship endorsing candidates and 66% prefer that they stay out of day-to-day
political matters. Familiarity with Christian nationalism has increased, but
familiarity is not support, and public salience is not observed organizational
power. See the [Pew religion/public-life record](records/us-pew-religion-public-life-church-state-2026.json).

Gallup supplies a counterexample to reading this as uniform religious decline.
In its 2024–2025 biennial aggregates, 42% of men ages 18–29 said religion was
very important, up from 28% in 2022–2023; young women were at 29%. Monthly-or-
more attendance was nearly tied at 40% for young men and 39% for young women,
while specific affiliation was 63% versus 60%. Importance, attendance, and
affiliation therefore move as separate currencies. The result is repeated
cross-sectional evidence, not proof of a durable revival, congregational growth,
political mobilization, or civic action. See the [Gallup young-men religiosity
record](records/us-gallup-young-men-religiosity-gender-reversal-2024-2025.json).

Pew's democracy survey adds a performance-and-reform layer. Sixty-eight
percent said the United States used to be a good example of democracy but has
not been in recent years, and 69% were dissatisfied with how democracy is
working. Yet this does not mean one shared rejection of democracy:
dissatisfaction was 86% among Democrats and Democratic leaners versus 51% among
Republicans and Republican leaners, and the survey does not show whether
dissatisfied people withdraw, vote, organize, or seek reform. See the [Pew
democracy-evaluation record](records/us-pew-democracy-dissatisfaction-reform-2026.json).

Pew's later reform analysis adds a second split: wanting change is not the same
as believing change can happen. About 77% said the political system needs major
changes or complete reform, but only 27% were confident that such changes could
happen. Pew calls roughly half of Americans "pessimistic reformers"—people who
want major change but do not expect it to be achievable. Support for particular
ideas can still be broad: term limits, age limits, campaign-spending limits,
and a national popular vote each receive majority support in separate surveys.
These are not one shared reform plan, and they do not show organizing, voting,
protest, withdrawal, or successful institutional change. The deeper subtheme is
that democratic dissatisfaction may produce pressure for repair while perceived
feasibility blocks a common route to action. See the [Pew reform-demand and
feasibility record](records/us-pew-reform-demand-feasibility-2026.json).

CIRCLE's 2026 youth financial-strain survey gives that distinction a material
anchor. Sixty percent of respondents ages 18–29 said they sometimes or often
had trouble meeting basic needs. Among financially struggling youth, 64% said
their vote did not matter, compared with 53% of financially stable youth; life
satisfaction was 57% versus 88%. At the same time, struggling youth were more
likely to describe participation as worthwhile when their voice mattered,
their communities changed, and candidates followed through. The pattern is
not “hardship causes apathy.” It is that pressure can reduce efficacy and
well-being while a credible route to results can still turn concern into
action. This is a cross-sectional pre-election survey: it does not measure
final turnout, candidate follow-through, or a dated hardship-to-action path.
See the [CIRCLE financial-strain and political-engagement record](records/us-circle-youth-financial-strain-political-engagement-2026.json).

The CCES comparison supplies an important counterexample to any simple
withdrawal story. In 2024, respondents reporting both gig work and current
student-loan responsibility had a 30.3% joint share of reported federal trust
and at least one civic action, compared with 16.4% among respondents reporting
neither condition. At the same time, low federal trust did not mean no action:
the low-trust-and-action share was 16.1% in the joint gig-work/debt group and
18.7% in the neither group. These are descriptive proxy cells, not dated
shocks or causal effects, and the smallest cell has 665 respondents. The
useful lesson is that material pressure, distrust, and civic action can coexist
in different combinations; political response depends on identity, efficacy,
resources, and available routes, not hardship alone. See the [CCES trust and
action comparison](records/us-cces-trust-action-joint-material-proxies-2024.json).

### 8. State power depends on social capacity

Military and geopolitical capability depends on staffing, families, childcare,
maintenance, hospitals, police, local partners, public money, and legitimacy.
The same principle appears in civilian infrastructure and public programs.

A state can approve, fund, staff, and operate a capability without proving
local consent, durable public support, deterrence, or changed behavior by
another actor. Equipment and formal authority are only part of power.

The administrative middle matters too. GAO's public reviews of space
cooperation and the security-cooperation workforce find formal mechanisms
alongside staffing gaps, overlapping responsibilities, difficult points of
contact, incomplete workforce provisions, and missing implementation
milestones. The safe conclusion is not alliance failure. It is that cooperation
needs named owners, staffed contact routes, training, and a visible completion
clock before a signed arrangement becomes dependable shared action. See the
[space-coordination record](records/us-gao-space-allied-coordination-2025.json)
and [security-cooperation workforce record](records/us-gao-security-cooperation-workforce-2026.json).

Task Force X-Baltic makes the conversion stage visible. NATO reports a live
test of more than 70 autonomous systems in 2025, followed by an eight-Allied
commitment in 2026 to nationally owned systems that NATO can task and
integrate. That is evidence that an alliance can turn experimentation into
shared institutional capability. It is not evidence of continuous coverage,
deterrence, changed adversary behavior, fair burden sharing, or public consent.
See the [Task Force X-Baltic finding](projects/us-nato-task-force-x-baltic-2025-2026/findings/us-nato-task-force-x-baltic-2025-2026-001.md).

The geopolitical records show the same separation: the German brigade report
put volunteer staffing at about 80% while selected mandatory measures were
still needed, and NATO's medical exercise involved civilian health and
emergency institutions across 38 countries. These are real capacity stages,
not evidence by themselves of deterrence, consent, or crisis performance.

NATO's 2026 Resilience Baseline Requirements make the civil-military link
explicit at the policy level. Whole-of-government and whole-of-society
planning, communications, transport, food and water, health, movement of
people, border crossing, supply chains, and private-sector dependencies are
treated as part of resilience and Host Nation Support. The document also says
military and civil plans should mitigate effects on the population and maintain
essential services. This shows a formal strategic dependency, not country-level
compliance, fair cost sharing, public consent, or crisis performance. See the
[NATO resilience-baseline finding](projects/us-nato-resilience-baseline-requirements/findings/us-nato-resilience-baseline-requirements-001.md).

The Steadfast Discovery 26 account adds a practiced stage beneath that policy.
From 14–18 September 2026, NATO's Allied Reaction Force rehearsed liaison and
reconnaissance work with Denmark in Copenhagen and Herning, including logistics,
transport, movement, reception, and sustainment procedures. This is evidence
that host-nation coordination was practiced by named institutions, not proof
that a real crisis would move quickly, protect civilian services, share costs
fairly, or retain public support. See the [Steadfast Discovery
finding](projects/us-nato-host-nation-rehearsal-denmark/findings/us-nato-host-nation-rehearsal-denmark-001.md).

SIPRI's 2025 estimates add the resource-allocation scale. Global military
spending reached about $2.887 trillion, while U.S. spending was estimated at
$954 billion and European spending rose 14% in real terms. NATO accounted for
about 55% of global spending, with 23 members at or above 2% of GDP under
SIPRI's calculation. These figures show that states are putting more money
into security capacity, but they do not show readiness, usable equipment,
interoperability, household trade-offs, public legitimacy, or whether another
actor changed behavior. Spending is an input to power, not power itself. See
the [SIPRI 2025 military-expenditure record](records/us-sipri-military-expenditure-2025-state-capacity.json).

State fiscal reserves show the civilian version of the same capacity problem.
Across the 50 states in fiscal 2025, rainy-day funds totaled about $174.2
billion, with a median cushion equal to 47.8 operating days, down from 54.5
days the year before. The spread was large: Wyoming reported 320.2 days while
New Jersey reported zero, and 26 states saw their days of reserve capacity
decline. These figures show room to absorb a shock, not a common national fund
or a guarantee of better services. States differ in accounting, legal access,
spending needs, and willingness to draw reserves; the data do not show which
households receive protection when the cushion is used. See the [state fiscal
capacity record](records/us-pew-state-rainy-day-capacity-fy2025.json).

Disaster grants add a continuity problem: public capacity can be withdrawn
before it becomes usable protection. GAO found that DHS paused disbursement of
all obligated grant funding in February 2025, terminated 362 grants, and
deobligated about $1.002 billion; some disaster-mitigation projects were
delayed by nearly a year, and one FEMA funding route was later restored after a
court order. This does not show that every terminated grant was valuable or
that every delay caused harm. It does show that support, pause, correction,
and restoration are separate stages, and that local preparedness can depend on
administrative continuity as well as nominal funding. See the [DHS grant
continuity record](records/us-gao-dhs-grant-termination-pause-disruption-2025.json).

Disaster contracting adds a local-economy version. FEMA and the Army Corps had
policies favoring firms in the declared disaster area, but GAO found they did
not monitor the data well enough to know how often local vendors were used. In
FEMA's records for three selected disasters, about 3%—$85 million of $2.9
billion in obligations—was identified as local-vendor work. That is not a
national local-vendor rate or proof that outside firms were unnecessary; it is
evidence that recovery spending and local economic recovery are separate
stages, and that the agencies could not reliably see the connection. See the
[local-vendor disaster-contracting finding](projects/us-gao-disaster-local-vendor-use/findings/us-gao-disaster-local-vendor-use-001.md).

The same issue appears in civilian infrastructure. A data center, transmission
line, community-solar program, or cross-border power route can be built and
operated while the public still does not know who receives the savings, who
pays the extra cost, who carries the land or reliability burden, or whether a
household can switch to something else. Infrastructure is therefore a social
and political bargain, not just physical capacity. Public comments,
cost-allocation rules, and reported project benefits show that people have a
route into the decision; they do not prove that the route changed the decision
or that the final bargain was accepted. See the [infrastructure bargain
synthesis](US-BROAD-INFRASTRUCTURE-BARGAIN-SYNTHESIS_V1.md).

Puerto Rico's grid recovery makes the conversion problem concrete. FEMA, HUD,
and DOE had obligated about $14 billion since 2017, but only about $2.7 billion
of FEMA's $11.1 billion obligation had been disbursed. Nine large FEMA projects
were complete, while about 400 of 16,000 planned vegetation-clearing miles had
been completed with federal funds. DOE also redirected or canceled about $715
million initially aimed at community and low-income solar projects. These are
not proof that federal investment failed; they show that obligation,
disbursement, completion, household-facing benefit, and reliable service run on
different clocks. See the [Puerto Rico grid recovery record](records/us-gao-puerto-rico-grid-recovery-disbursement-stability-2017-2026.json).

Payment infrastructure adds an everyday version of the same dependence. An
NBER study finds that disasters and outages reduce spending, digital payments
become less available, and cash use rises; nearly half of surveyed consumers
could not use their preferred electronic payment during an outage. More cash
access was associated with more completed transactions, but a completed
transaction does not prove that food, medicine, fuel, work, or care needs were
met. See the [payment-resilience record](records/us-nber-payment-method-resilience-disaster-outages-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-payment-system-outage-resilience/findings/us-payment-system-outage-resilience-001.md).

At the international level, alliance support and trust in a particular partner
also move on different tracks. People may support an alliance in principle
while doubting whether the United States is reliable, respectful, or attentive
to other countries' interests. Formal cooperation can therefore continue while
the political permission for future cost, risk, or leadership remains
conditional. See the [geopolitical and state-power synthesis](US-BROAD-GEOPOLITICAL-STATE-SUBTHEME-SYNTHESIS_V1.md).

Pew's spring 2026 survey makes the external-reception gap measurable. Across
36 countries, the median favorable view of the United States was 37%, while
the median unfavorable view was 57%; only 32% median said the U.S. considered
countries like theirs' interests a great deal or fair amount. Formal alliance
ties and operational cooperation can therefore coexist with weaker public
confidence that the United States is a reliable or interest-respecting partner.
This is an opinion layer, not evidence that a government denied access,
changed a treaty position, or altered deterrence behavior. See the [Pew
international-reliability record](records/us-pew-us-reliability-international-2026.json).

The paired NATO survey makes the separation clearer. Across 13 surveyed
member states, NATO had a 65% median favorable rating and a 30% unfavorable
rating, while the separate cross-national survey found only 32% median saying
the United States considered countries like theirs' interests. In the U.S.,
NATO favorability was 75% among Democrats and Democratic leaners versus 42%
among Republicans and Republican leaners. Alliance legitimacy, trust in a
particular leading partner, and domestic partisan meaning are therefore three
different political resources. These are public-opinion measures, not evidence
of burden sharing, willingness to fight, readiness, or durable alliance
behavior. See the [Pew NATO-legitimacy record](records/us-pew-nato-public-legitimacy-2026.json).

The Germany-Lithuania record adds a public-permission layer to the posture
question. A 2025 German survey found broad support for higher defense spending,
more personnel, common European security policy, and European defense projects,
but only 37% trusted the United States as a reliable partner, 43% saw U.S.
policy as a risk to NATO cohesion, and only 10% felt well informed about
foreign missions. A separate Lithuanian poll reported 82% approval of a
permanent German brigade, while the later exercise and posture records still
do not show local experience or consent for every cost, escalation risk, or
future combat use. Defense support, alliance trust, mission knowledge, and
event-specific permission are therefore separate political resources. See the
[Germany-NATO posture record](records/us-nato-germany-hybrid-response-alliance-posture-2026.json).

The newer JEF operating records add a later capability stage without closing
the larger gap. A multinational escort rehearsal moved Danish military
equipment through the Baltic to Latvia, and a later exercise deployed a JEF
forward headquarters in northern Norway with host-nation support and NATO
coordination. That shows movement, command, and alliance interfaces operating
in practice. It does not show a real crisis response, adversary change, local
acceptance, community burden, or durable deterrence. See the [JEF operating
record](records/us-jef-lion-protector-baltic-express-2026.json).

Lithuania's Defence Ministry reports a later, live operating stage: on
September 15, NATO fighters identified, intercepted, and destroyed a drone
that entered Lithuanian airspace, with Italian crews conducting NATO air-
defence duties in Lithuania. A follow-up statement presented the response as
evidence of alliance unity and called for stronger European air defence and
critical-infrastructure protection. This is stronger evidence of a real
protective action than an exercise or procurement announcement. The public
record still does not independently establish the drone's origin or intent,
the recurring cost, civilian-risk reduction, public burden, deterrence, or any
changed behavior by an outside actor. See the [Lithuanian interception
record](records/us-nato-lithuania-drone-interception-alliance-response-2026.json).

A later NATO account adds a repeated-incident follow-up. After airspace
violations affecting Poland and Romania and other drone incidents, Allies
publicly expressed solidarity, attributed the violations to Russia, condemned
them, and described continued reinforcement of air and missile defenses and the
eastern flank. This makes the incident-to-alliance-response path visible beyond
a single interception. It still does not show independent attribution, reduced
future incidents, changed Russian choices, host-community burden, or public
acceptance. See the [NATO airspace-response follow-up
record](records/us-nato-airspace-incidents-response-followup-2026.json).

The EU's MARSEC EU 26 exercise adds a cross-border infrastructure-response
stage. Finnish and Polish authorities, EU agencies, defense and emergency
services, and commercial maritime actors practiced information sharing,
joint decision-making, vessel boarding, search, rescue, and securing a vessel
near suspected undersea-infrastructure damage. This is stronger than a policy
promise because the response chain was rehearsed, but it remains an exercise:
real-incident performance, repair, service continuity, public acceptance,
deterrence, and external behavior remain open. See the [MARSEC EU 26
record](records/us-eu-marsec-baltic-cross-border-infrastructure-response-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-140.md).

NATO's Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance Force shows another
operating stage: during 2026 activities it reports about 180 RQ-4D flight
hours, more than 500 intelligence products, and operations across Norway,
Finland, and Italy, with mission crews, specialists, infrastructure, and
host-nation capabilities working as one enterprise. Later accounts describe
decision support during Neptune Strike and a maritime surface picture during
Northern Viking. These records show repeated multinational operation and
host-support integration, not just a plan or exercise label. They do not show
what any product changed, whether targeting succeeded, the cost or burden to
host communities, public acceptance, or changed adversary behavior. See the
[NATO ISR operating record](records/us-nato-nisrf-agile-combat-employment-2026.json).

A separate NISRF account shows a Belgian national MQ-9B temporarily deployed
through NATO infrastructure at Sigonella, with more than 400 flight hours in
support of Operation Sea Guardian and Operation Noble Shield. This is evidence
of allied capability enablement and operational use, not proof of a changed
strategic outcome. Mission effect, host cost, public acceptance, replaceability,
and external response remain open. See the [Belgian MQ-9B deployment
record](records/us-nato-belgian-mq9b-nisrf-deployment-2026.json).

Poland's U.S.-linked defense relationship adds an industrial and political
stage beyond procurement. Official agreements describe Poland as a potential
regional production center, Polish assembly and later localization, shared
standards, and a $4 billion U.S. Foreign Military Financing agreement. A
ministerial response makes detailed production and technology-transfer terms
conditional on demand from the Polish Armed Forces, and a parliamentary
inquiry records that a public purchase order, output, and delivery were not
yet visible in the cited materials. A separate CBOS survey found Polish
support divided by financing route: 51% supported the EU SAFE route and 46%
supported using central-bank gold profits, with only 17% supporting both.
These records show how alliance capacity, industrial sovereignty, financing,
and public permission can move on different clocks. They do not establish a
commissioned plant, produced or delivered system, local-content share,
reduced dependence, or changed adversary behavior. See the [Poland-U.S.
defense reciprocity record](records/us-poland-us-defense-reciprocity-regional-coproduction-2026.json)
and [Polish defense-financing opinion record](records/us-poland-defense-financing-public-opinion-2026.json).

### 9. Firm growth and public capacity can coexist with transferred risk

Firms can grow, recover, receive financing, adopt AI, or gain infrastructure
capacity while workers, customers, suppliers, households, and communities carry
more uncertainty. A public loan can preserve a business without proving worker
security. A productivity tool can help a firm without giving workers more
control. A data center can generate revenue while ratepayers and local systems
carry unresolved costs.

The recurring question is not simply whether an organization succeeded. It is
who gained room, who became more dependent, and who absorbed the downside.

Environmental policy adds a belief-and-legitimacy layer to this bargain. An
NBER survey found that Americans were more worried about consumer energy bills
under market-based tools than under standards, unlike environmental economists;
an information experiment moved beliefs and policy support closer to the expert
pattern. This is not evidence about anyone's actual bill or the policy's
pollution result. It shows that how people imagine who pays can shape which
environmental tool is politically usable. See the [environmental-policy belief
record](records/us-nber-environmental-policy-cost-beliefs-policy-support-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-environmental-policy-cost-beliefs/findings/us-environmental-policy-cost-beliefs-001.md).

Cash-policy evidence adds a household-perception step. An NBER study of the
2021 Child Tax Credit reports that each $1,000 of lost benefit was associated
with a 1.7-point fall in consumer sentiment, with larger effects among
lower-income families with multiple children and persistence for nearly two
years. This is evidence that a policy can change how people judge the economy
through their own lost household room. It is not evidence of a changed vote,
trust in government, or spending path; those outcomes need separate data. See
the [CTC sentiment record](records/us-nber-child-tax-credit-sentiment-benefit-loss-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-policy-sentiment-cash-benefit/findings/us-policy-sentiment-cash-benefit-002.md).

Student-loan policy uncertainty adds a belief-before-receipt mechanism. An
NBER study linked beliefs about possible forgiveness or payment pauses to
credit, employment, and spending records. More optimistic borrowers paid about
$40 less per month on student loans and spent about $80 more on nondurables;
optimism about pauses was followed by a 7.5-percentage-point higher likelihood
of delinquency after payments resumed. The promised relief was never
implemented, so this is evidence that policy expectations can change present
behavior before a benefit exists—not evidence of received relief or changed
political identity. See the [student-loan uncertainty record](records/us-nber-student-loan-policy-uncertainty-beliefs-spending-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-student-loan-policy-uncertainty/findings/us-student-loan-policy-uncertainty-001.md).

Work-policy evidence adds a political endpoint. An NBER study using decades of
EITC expansions and welfare reform as employment instruments reports that
employment lowered turnout and civic engagement and increased conservative
policy preferences among lower-income mothers. The effects were not uniform:
they were stronger for some groups and concentrated among White women despite
larger employment gains among non-White women. This is not a claim that work
generally makes people conservative; time, care, income, workplace, and
coworker mechanisms remain separate questions. See the [employment-and-voting
record](records/us-nber-employment-mothers-voting-political-identity-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-employment-mothers-voting/findings/us-employment-mothers-voting-001.md).

Firm politics adds a second legitimacy route. An NBER study finds that
corporate PACs temporarily cut visible contributions to Republican House
candidates after fatal school shootings—70.3% in competitive districts and
60.9% in safe Democratic districts—then returned toward baseline within nine
months. No substitution toward Democratic candidates was detected, and the
response was absent in safe Republican districts. This suggests firms manage
the reputational visibility of political ties without necessarily abandoning
their longer-term political strategy. PAC giving is not lobbying or policy
influence. See the [corporate-PAC record](records/us-nber-corporate-pac-reputational-risk-temporary-divestment-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-corporate-pac-reputational-risk/findings/us-corporate-pac-reputational-risk-001.md).

Carbon-trade modeling adds a geopolitical policy boundary. A US carbon tariff
alone can raise modeled US emissions while lowering foreign emissions; a
tariff combined with a domestic carbon tax lowers both and produces about twice
the global emissions reduction of the tariff-only case. Both approaches raise
modeled US GDP and welfare, but more tariff-only countries do not automatically
produce more global reductions. This is a model, not evidence about household
bills, firm relocation, foreign acceptance, or real emissions. See the
[carbon-tariff record](records/us-nber-carbon-tariff-domestic-emissions-global-coordination-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-carbon-tariff-climate-geopolitics/findings/us-carbon-tariff-climate-geopolitics-001.md).

Geoeconomic coercion adds a target-selection layer. An NBER model shows that
great powers can pressure foreign governments, private firms, or both through
trade and finance. Private targets may internalize less of the national
consequence of complying, while governments can influence firms beyond direct
reach. This explains how a state contest can land on companies and workers,
but it does not show which target is used, who complies, or whether the
strategy succeeds. See the [geoeconomic-coercion record](records/us-nber-geoeconomic-coercion-government-firm-targets-2026.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-geoeconomic-coercion-targets/findings/us-geoeconomic-coercion-targets-001.md).

The retaliation record adds the next geopolitical stage. A Congressional
Research Service timeline records China, Canada, and the European Union
answering U.S. tariff pressure with tariffs, export restrictions, company
designations, pauses, exemptions, and negotiations. A foreign response is
therefore visible, but it is not the same as surrender, permanent escalation,
or U.S. strategic success. Firm costs, household prices, alliance durability,
and the policy result remain open. See the [retaliation record](records/us-crs-tariff-retaliation-foreign-response-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-geoeconomic-coercion-targets/findings/us-geoeconomic-coercion-targets-002.md).

Transportation adds a place-and-time price. BLS reports that consumer units
spent an average of $13,318 on transportation in 2024, or 17% of spending;
the Census Bureau reports a 27.2-minute average one-way work trip and 9.3% of
workers taking at least an hour. Movement can create access to jobs, care,
food, or better housing, but the trip also consumes money and time. These are
separate national averages, not a same-household burden or an access outcome.
See the [transportation record](records/us-transportation-cost-time-access-2024.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-transportation-household-access/findings/us-transportation-household-access-001.md).

The firm evidence gives this pattern real shape. A causal study of more than
167,000 disaster-loan applications found lower firm exit and bankruptcy and
higher employment and revenue after recovery lending, but the study does not
show worker pay, job quality, owner-household recovery, or customer prices.
In a separate field experiment, AI access reduced reported email time for
users by about two hours per week, but the evidence does not show that the
time became worker control rather than employer capacity. See the [disaster
liquidity record](records/us-small-business-disaster-liquidity-causal-2024.json)
and [AI work-adoption record](records/us-ai-work-adoption-control-2024-2026.json).

Gallup's repeated workforce studies add the meaning and management layer.
Frequent workplace-AI users reported concern about job elimination at more than
twice the rate of infrequent users, but the association was smaller among
workers who rated their managers highly for respect or strongly agreed that
their organization cared about wellbeing. The reported within-worker pattern
also links AI-related job concern with lower engagement and satisfaction,
higher burnout, and more active job search. This is a longitudinal association,
not proof that management caused the buffer or that anyone actually lost a
job, pay, schedule control, bargaining power, or household security. It does
show that workers interpret the same technical exposure through a social
relationship: respect and care can change the meaning of risk before a formal
employment outcome is visible. See the [Gallup AI-work record](records/us-gallup-ai-work-manager-support-displacement-2026.json).

New York City's Local Law 25 adds a public-measurement response. The law
requires a city study of algorithmic tools and automated employment decision
tools, including hiring impacts, effects on regular employee work functions,
and employee feedback. As of the September 2026 official recheck, the city was
still in planning stages and no completed study or public report was located.
This advances the chain from concern and rule-making toward institutional
visibility, but it does not identify a tool, exposed worker, notice, appeal,
correction, compensation, workload or pay change, or exit. A government can
mandate measurement before it can show worker protection. See the [NYC
algorithmic-work study record](records/us-nyc-local-law-25-algorithmic-work-study-2026.json).

An NBER administrative-data study adds a distributional warning about public
firm support. When a program's eligibility expanded toward larger firms,
government contracts shifted toward those firms while affected smaller firms
experienced lower revenue, more exits, lower wages, and less patenting—
especially among younger, productive, and financially constrained firms. The
paper's abstract does not provide effect sizes or customer, worker-household,
or local-service outcomes, and larger firms may sometimes provide scale or
resilience. Still, support can strengthen aggregate capacity while weakening
the smaller-firm ecosystem that supplies experimentation, local presence, or
alternative employers. See the [NBER firm-support record](records/us-small-business-support-size-rule-2026.json).

The size-divergence pattern appears in current firm surveys too. The New York
Fed's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey reports that small-firm employment
growth remained positive even as the smallest firms had weaker revenue paths,
lower pricing power, and a larger fall in expected revenue growth; reported
debt averaged about $70,000 per employee across the employer-firm sample. The
World Bank's 2024 U.S. establishment profile adds a different split: formal
training was reported by 29.1% of small establishments and 78.9% of large ones,
bank-financed investment by 19.2% and 33.9%, while electrical outages were
reported by 21.0% of small and 36.2% of large establishments. These are separate
survey indicators, not a firm panel. They show that scale can bring training
and finance access while also increasing infrastructure exposure, and that
employment growth does not settle revenue, worker, or household security. See
the [small-business capacity record](records/us-nyfed-small-business-capacity-2025.json)
and [World Bank firm-capacity record](records/us-world-bank-enterprise-survey-firm-capacity-size-2024.json).

The DHS contract review adds a state-capacity version of transferred risk.
DHS reported more than $10.5 billion in possible cost avoidance after
terminating 438 contracts, but GAO found that more than $1.7 billion had
already been obligated through existing government-wide contracts for the same
IT requirements. Cancelling one contract is therefore not the same as
eliminating the work or saving the headline amount. The review does not show
service continuity, contractor or worker effects, mission performance, or
public value, and a replacement contract could still improve price or quality.
See the [DHS contract-termination record](records/us-gao-dhs-contract-termination-cost-avoidance-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/ai-work-control/findings/ai-work-control-137.md).

### 10. Care and health are household infrastructure

Medical coverage, provider capacity, childcare, eldercare, transportation, and
family support keep the economy and daily life running. But a covered person
may still face a denied authorization, long wait, inaccessible provider,
medical debt, lost work time, or unpaid care by relatives.

Care is therefore both a health issue and a distribution issue. The hidden
question is who supplies the time and labor when formal care is delayed,
unaffordable, or unavailable—and whether the household can keep working,
resting, moving, and recovering.

The scale is visible in different source families. BLS estimates about 38.2
million people provided eldercare in 2023–2024; employed eldercare providers
spent an average 2.84 hours on care days. Census reports that households with
child-care problems lost an average 65.8 work days, rising to 114.6 days among
affected households below the poverty line. These are not one linked family
story, but together they show care as a large labor and time system rather than
a private side issue. See the [eldercare record](records/us-atus-eldercare-work-time-2023-2024.json)
and [child-care adaptation record](records/us-census-childcare-challenges-work-adaptation-2025.json).

GAO adds a national valuation layer. It estimates that most people spend
almost four hours a day on unpaid household work, worth around $70 per day by
selected replacement-wage methods. The estimated 2024 national value is at
least $5.6–$6.0 trillion, or about 19.2–20.2% of GDP. Women made up 54% of
people doing this work while representing 51% of the population in 2021–2024.
This makes hidden social capacity visible, but the replacement value is not
pay, wellbeing, care quality, or proof that the burden was freely chosen. See
the [GAO unpaid-household-work finding](projects/us-gao-unpaid-household-work/findings/us-gao-unpaid-household-work-001.md).

The MEPS event screen shows the institutional interface more directly. Among
people with a dated office visit, those reporting an insurance denial or
prior-authorization delay also reported care delay at about 15.6%, versus 5.8%
among those without that reported friction, and medical debt at about 24.9%
versus 13.3%. For emergency-room events, the corresponding care-delay figures
were about 19.0% and 7.7%. These are same-person event-family comparisons, not
causal estimates: the denial measure is annual, its timing relative to the
event is unknown, and severity, coverage, and prior debt may shape both the
friction and the outcome. Still, the pattern makes the broader point concrete:
coverage and a medical encounter can coexist with an administrative route that
pushes delay and financial risk back onto the household. The record does not
show appeal, correction, completed care, or recovery.

The VA electronic-health-record review adds a health-technology realization
clock. VA can restart and accelerate deployments while cost documentation,
operational testing, user satisfaction, ticket correction, and readiness remain
unfinished. The report does not establish patient harm or clinical benefit;
it shows why system deployment, successful use, and safer care must remain
separate stages. See the [VA EHR finding](findings/us-gao-va-electronic-health-record-modernization-implementation-2026-matched-evidence-001.md).

HHS-OIG's Medicare Advantage review adds a sharper appeal boundary for
skilled-nursing admission. Across 19 reviewed organizations, 12% of requests
were denied initially; only 18% of those denials were appealed, yet 95% of the
appealed denials were overturned. The review does not show why people did not
appeal, whether care was delayed or substituted, or whether an overturned
request became timely and adequate care. The bounded lesson is that a high
appeal-overturn rate can reveal a correctable gate without measuring everyone
who was unable, unwilling, or too exhausted to enter the appeal route. See the
[skilled-nursing authorization record](records/us-hhs-oig-medicare-snf-prior-authorization-2026.json).

A layered health-cost synthesis adds a broader bridge, but not one universal
story. In the 2025 SHED, 26% reported foregoing care because of cost. Among a
separate 2024–25 recontact group that continued to report care-forgoing, 41.8%
reported medical debt, 65.3% reduced savings, 36.4% increased borrowing, and
76.4% delayed a major purchase in 2025. The historical Oregon Medicaid lottery
provides a different causal layer: coverage increased by 24.1 percentage
points, reduced unpaid bills sent to collections by 6.4 points, reduced any
out-of-pocket spending by 20 points, and increased outpatient visits by 21
points; a short-run turnout effect did not persist through 2010. These sources
use different people, dates, designs, and outcomes. Together they show how
health cost can move through care choice, household room, credit, and even
political contact on different clocks—not that one household followed every
step. See the [health-cost household-choice synthesis](records/us-health-cost-household-choice-end-to-end-2024-2026.json).

The broader meaning is that care arrangements are not just private family
preferences; they are ways of allocating work, money, time, and future
opportunity. Census data show that households below the poverty line reported
an average 114.6 converted work days lost when they had a child-care problem,
compared with 65.8 days across affected households overall. Lower-income
households also reported less paid leave and more not looking for work, while
higher-income households reported more paid-leave use. Pew's working-parent
survey adds the meaning layer: 83% of parents in a two-full-time-parent
arrangement described a positive financial impact, but only 49% described a
positive impact on children and 22% a negative one. These are separate
descriptive sources, not one family trajectory, but together they show why a
family can look economically functional while experiencing care as lost time,
career constraint, strain, or an unresolved bargain about what kind of life is
possible.

The BLS leave module adds a workplace-interface version. In 2024-25, 94% of
wage and salary workers reported access to either paid or unpaid leave, yet 8%
still needed leave during an average month and did not take it. Among those
workers, too much work, fear of job consequences or a denied request, and the
cost of lost income were major reasons. Formal access can therefore exist
without usable time for illness, family care, or personal needs. The survey
does not show whether care happened, pay was lost, or the job and household
recovered. See the [BLS leave record](records/us-bls-atus-leave-access-unmet-need-2024-2025.json)
and [bounded finding](projects/us-aging-care-strain/findings/us-aging-care-strain-019.md).

Pew's newer caregiving survey adds the distributional and meaning layer. About
10% of U.S. adults identified as caregivers for an aging parent and 3% for an
aging spouse or partner; among adults with an aging parent, the share was 24%.
Lower-income adults with an aging parent or partner were more likely to
identify as caregivers than upper-income adults (39% versus 16%). Parent
caregivers reported more negative than positive effects on emotional
well-being, physical health, work, finances, and social life, even while the
relationship itself could be experienced positively. Women caring for a parent
also more often reported negative emotional and physical effects than men.
These are self-reported caregiver identities, not hours, care quality, or
causal burden estimates, but they show that family care can create connection
and obligation while shifting unequal costs into health, work, money, and
social life. See the [Pew family-caregiving record](records/us-pew-family-caregiving-aging-burden-2025-2026.json).

### 11. Progress and security can move on different clocks

People may report higher income, family progress, employment, enrollment,
business survival, or new infrastructure while still facing debt, housing
uncertainty, insurance risk, care strain, discrimination, or a less believable
future. A positive aggregate or institutional result can coexist with reduced
room for particular groups.

This is why the atlas tracks protected outcomes and sacrificed outcomes
together. Improvement in one measure does not erase the costs that made it
possible or the people who remain unable to use the improvement.

The current macro-financial comparison makes the separation concrete. BEA
reported 1.5% annualized real GDP growth in 2026 Q2 and a $400.9 billion
quarterly increase in corporate profits, while the Federal Reserve reported
continued credit-card and auto-loan delinquency pressure and relatively tight
credit for some households and small businesses. In the 2025 SHED, 63% of
adults said they could cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent, but
12% said they could not; among borrowers required to make student-loan
payments, 41% reported difficulty. These sources do not describe the same
people or a causal chain. They show why output, profits, financial-system
capacity, and household room must remain separate stages. See the [public
financial-capacity comparison](records/us-public-financial-capacity-household-room-crosssource-2025-2026.json).

A linked Census pulse screen makes the timing problem visible within the same
respondents. From April to June 2025, 41.8% remained in the household-expense-
difficulty group and 7.5% moved from difficulty to no difficulty; in the same
linked sample, 11.8% remained in the high-confidence-in-Congress category and
5.3% moved out of it. Those are not matched causes or recovery rates, but they
show that material conditions and institutional confidence are separate
measures that can persist, change, or reverse on different clocks. The linked
sample is a selected retained subset and uses April weights rather than a
documented attrition-adjusted longitudinal weight, so it does not estimate a
national transition or explain why any person changed. See the [linked Census
material/trust record](records/us-census-htops-material-trust-panel-april-june-2025.json).

The same linked respondents add a useful counterexample to any simple “hardship
lowers all trust” story. People who reported expense difficulty in April showed
larger June gaps in food insufficiency, energy nonpayment, and job loss than
those who did not. But high confidence in Congress was nearly the same in the
two groups, 17.2% versus 16.9%, while confidence gaps were larger for the
presidency, military, police, Supreme Court, public schools, and statistical
agencies. This is a descriptive cross-lagged comparison, not a causal estimate
or an attrition-adjusted national transition. It suggests that material strain
and institutional judgment can travel together without producing one uniform
political response. See the [HTOPS cross-lagged record](records/us-htops-expense-difficulty-cross-lagged-2025.json).

The inflation-beliefs rotation adds the interpretation layer between a price
problem and public judgment. A national survey found broad agreement that
inflation was harmful, but disagreement about whether government, firms, supply
costs, labor markets, or the Federal Reserve were responsible. Respondents also
favored remedies for different reasons and often expected inflation control to
avoid major trade-offs. This means that “prices hurt,” “who caused it,” “what
should be done,” and “what am I willing to sacrifice” are separate cultural and
political questions. The survey does not establish the respondent's actual bill,
durable belief change, spending, or vote. See the [inflation-beliefs record](records/us-nber-inflation-cause-beliefs-2024.json).

The new Census neighborhood-and-firm study adds a place-based version of
unequal practical agency. Childhood neighborhoods are associated with later
earnings through both person conditions and access to establishments, and the
between-neighborhood relationship is stronger than the within-neighborhood one.
That does not mean a move automatically creates opportunity. The reported
conditional pattern in which higher-Black-share neighborhoods have higher adult
earnings at similar average neighborhood earnings also warns against turning
racial composition into a simple deficit story. The missing middle is the lived
route through schools, networks, discrimination, reachable firms, job quality,
housing, belonging, and later civic capacity. See the [neighborhood-and-firm
finding](findings/us-census-neighborhoods-firms-intergenerational-mobility-2026.md).

The companion Census mobility study adds a cultural-status qualification. A
neighborhood's racial composition is associated with White out-mobility
differently depending on the resident's relative socioeconomic position. This
does not tell us whether a move was chosen, forced, affordable, or beneficial;
it shows why “white flight,” contact, competition, and belonging cannot be read
from composition alone. The missing middle is the same household's status,
housing and job constraints, contact quality, discrimination, destination, and
later security or civic life. See the [relative-status finding](findings/us-census-neighborhood-racial-status-white-outmobility-2026.md).

The homophily rotation adds a work version of the same cultural mechanism. A
job opportunity is not equally usable when the destination carries different
expected social costs. Black and Hispanic workers in the Census study responded
more to opportunities in places with larger own-race populations, but the data
do not reveal whether that reflects networks, safety, expected discrimination,
family support, information, or preference. The missing middle is the worker's
actual move or stay, treatment, job quality, household effect, and later sense
of belonging. See the [homophily finding](findings/us-census-homophily-race-job-mobility-2026.md).

The founder-retention study adds a firm-culture version of the same pattern.
Founder departure increased employee turnover, especially in older or larger
startups and among workers with longer shared tenure. That suggests a firm can
depend on relationships and meaning around a person, but turnover is not itself
proof of worker harm or cultural loss. The missing middle is replacement
leadership, pay, control, voice, job quality, firm survival, household effects,
and later careers. See the [founder-retention finding](findings/us-census-founder-departure-startup-employee-retention-2026.md).

The manufacturing-allocation study adds a firm-side warning about national
averages. Apparent productivity decline can come from different combinations
of margin and input-mix dispersion, and a small set of industries can account
for much of the aggregate change. The missing middle is which firms changed,
what happened to workers and customers, and whether any place or household felt
the effect. See the [manufacturing allocation finding](findings/us-census-manufacturing-misallocation-margin-input-mix-2026.md).

## The larger interpretation

The recurring structure across the atlas is **unequal practical agency**.

People and institutions may have nominal choices while having very different
amounts of time, money, information, safety, support, status, and ability to
leave. Those differences shape not just material outcomes, but cultural
meaning and political judgment.

The central question for every theme is:

> Who can turn a formal option into a usable one, who performs the work when it
> fails, who carries the remaining risk, and who gets to change the rules?

BEA's household-production account adds a macro version of the same question.
Unpaid household activity represented about 16% of total output in 2024 when
counted in an expanded framework, but the account cannot show who did the work,
whether it was chosen, or whether the people doing it had a safe substitute.
See the [BEA household-production finding](projects/us-bea-household-production-account/findings/us-bea-household-production-account-001.md).

Small-business credit adds a firm-side version. Federal Reserve survey data
show that the route from wanting credit to receiving it changes with lender
choice and reported firm risk. A business can have lenders available while the
usable amount, terms, speed, and approval odds remain constrained. See the
[small-business credit finding](projects/us-fed-small-business-credit-route/findings/us-fed-small-business-credit-route-001.md).

Taken together, the local-economy evidence suggests a broader cultural and
political subtheme: money can enter a place without local control. Credit can
stop at approval, recovery contracts can lack reliable local-vendor tracking,
investment can be counted before community improvement, and recreation can
generate regional value without equal resident access. See the [local economic
participation synthesis](US-LOCAL-ECONOMIC-PARTICIPATION-SYNTHESIS_V1.md).

The geopolitical lane adds an administrative version of the same pattern.
GAO found 303 DOD acquisition-related international arrangements associated
with $64 billion from FY2021–2025, while the portfolio declined and review,
staffing, cost-sharing communication, and challenge management remained uneven.
International commitment is therefore not yet usable joint capability. See the
[DOD international-arrangements finding](projects/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements/findings/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements-001.md).

GAO's 2026 readiness synthesis adds the sustainment side of that problem.
Military demand, modernization, and operations can continue while maintenance,
staffing, fatigue, housing, data, and availability remain weak. In one concrete
Army watercraft example, the fully mission-capable rate fell below 40% in 2024
against a 90% policy goal. This does not prove overall force failure; it shows
why inventory and activity are not enough to establish dependable capability at
the point of need. See the [GAO readiness finding](projects/us-military-readiness-sustainment/findings/us-gao-military-readiness-sustainment-tradeoffs-2026-001.md).

Historical evidence adds a household-to-mobilization mechanism. A 2026 NBER
study reports that wives and daughters of disabled Civil War veterans entered
the labor force at higher rates, and that labor participation, newspaper
information, and proximity to other protest towns were related to Temperance
Crusade activity. This is not a modern estimate, but it shows how household
responsibility can sometimes create money, contacts, and public roles that make
collective action more possible. See the [historical household-mobilization
finding](projects/us-historical-household-mobilization/findings/us-nber-civil-war-womens-work-temperance-mobilization-2026-001.md).

Outdoor recreation adds a cultural-and-place version of the same question. BEA
estimates that outdoor recreation produced $696.7 billion in value added in
2024, but an economic footprint does not show which residents can afford,
reach, or enjoy the activity, who captures the gains, or whether the place
benefits. See the [BEA outdoor-recreation finding](projects/us-bea-outdoor-recreation-economy/findings/us-bea-outdoor-recreation-economy-001.md).

This is the plain-language version of the program-level finding, [Unequal
optionality is the recurring structure behind pressure, adaptation, and
power](findings/us-broad-program-unequal-optionality-path-001.md).

## What the evidence does not yet prove

The atlas combines surveys, administrative records, research studies, company
and platform records, public proceedings, infrastructure reports, and
geopolitical sources. These sources usually describe different people,
households, firms, places, or countries.

They support recurring comparisons and layered interpretations. They do not
automatically establish that one person experienced the entire chain from
shock to outcome, or that a broad pattern is causal.

The main open links are:

- actual receipt and adequacy after a formal approval or remedy;
- who pays and who benefits after infrastructure operates;
- whether support lasts or merely postpones the problem;
- whether belonging produces delivered help and durable action;
- whether institutional visibility produces correction and trust; and
- whether state capability changes external behavior.
