# Rotation HA — mental-health deinstitutionalization, lived experience and community recovery

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question; subtheme deepening of the care-infrastructure portfolio  
**Research lane:** community mental health, deinstitutionalization, coercion, housing, peer support, recovery and lived-experience governance

## Why this rotation exists

Deinstitutionalization is often counted as closing hospital beds, reducing
lengths of stay or publishing a community-care policy. Those are upstream
changes. The intended outcome is that a named person can live in a community
with choice, safety, relationships, housing, health care, education, work and
support, without being abandoned or exposed to coercion. This packet deepens the
existing care-infrastructure theme by focusing on the transition corridor from
institution to community and on whether people with lived experience have real
decision power.

## Research question

When mental-health systems shift from long-stay institutions toward community-
based, rights-oriented care, under what conditions do named people experience
continuity, autonomy, recovery and participation rather than discharge without
support, fragmented services, homelessness, family overload, repeated crisis or
coercive care in a different setting?

## Conversion chain

```text
institutional stay / crisis / policy reform / hospital closure / community plan
  -> housing, income, health, peer, family, legal and social supports with lived-experience governance
  -> person leaves or avoids institution and receives continuous person-centred care
  -> person exercises choice, relationships, education/work and community participation safely
  -> recovery, dignity, agency and non-recurrence persist without coercion or abandonment
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. WHO's October 2026 practical guide states that deinstitutionalization requires
   system-wide reform across policy, law, funding, community services, housing,
   care practices and culture. It organizes action around reducing admissions and
   long stays, transforming institutional care, supported discharge, continuity
   and community participation. A guide closes the implementation architecture,
   not the lived outcome for a discharged person.
2. WHO reports that only 9% of responding countries had fully shifted to
   community-based mental-health care, while 53% remained at an early stage;
   nearly half of reported admissions were involuntary and many stays remained
   long. These figures establish a global transition gap, but do not show that a
   particular reform caused recovery or reduced coercion.
3. WHO emphasizes that housing, health care, social support, education,
   employment and family support are essential to successful community life.
   This prevents the common analytical error of treating discharge or hospital
   closure as deinstitutionalization itself.
4. WHO's 2026 lived-experience material reports that only a minority of
   responding countries had ongoing collaboration with people with lived
   experience and family/caregiver groups in planning, delivery or monitoring.
   Consultation is therefore a governance mechanism to test for depth: equal
   partnership, decision influence, remuneration, representation and feedback
   are not guaranteed by a listening session.
5. WHO's QualityRights work documents the harms and mistrust associated with
   seclusion and restraint and frames coercion-free care as a change in daily
   practice, communication, legal capacity and staff support. A non-coercion
   policy or training course does not establish that a person in crisis could
   access a safe alternative or receive remedy after harm.
6. WHO's 2026 Poland assessment identifies coordination, care navigation,
   clinical standards and data monitoring as gaps in scaling community mental
   health. This is a concrete implementation case showing that governance and
   service-network readiness matter; it is not evidence that the national
   transition has already delivered equitable recovery.

## Bounded proposition

**A hospital closure, shorter stay, discharge plan, community mental-health
centre, peer-support programme, crisis line, housing placement, anti-restraint
policy, lived-experience consultation or deinstitutionalization strategy is not
by itself recovery, autonomy, safety, continuity, dignity, community belonging,
education, work, family resilience or non-coercive care.** A person can leave an
institution or be counted in a community programme without having stable
housing, income, medication or psychosocial continuity, a trusted relationship,
legal capacity, crisis support, meaningful choice, protection from violence or
the power to shape the service intended for them.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Community care is not a universal package detached from place. Housing supply,
family structures, income, transport, language, stigma, faith, customary care,
gender, disability, migration status, rurality and local workforce shape what
“community” can provide. A discharge in a city with peer workers and supported
housing is not equivalent to discharge in a rural area with no transport,
specialist care or safe housing. Family support can be protective but can also
shift unpaid and gendered burden without consent or resources.

Lived experience is knowledge and governance power, not only testimony. The
relevant question is whether people can influence budgets, service design,
clinical practice, admission/discharge decisions, data use, evaluation and
complaints. Recovery should be measured through the person's chosen life,
relationships, safety and agency, not only bed occupancy or symptom scores.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Institutions may provide essential acute care for some people; the packet does
  not equate every hospital service with harm or every community placement with
  quality.
- Reducing beds without housing, staff, crisis alternatives and continuity can
  create abandonment, homelessness or repeated emergency contact.
- Community care can still be coercive, surveillant or unsafe; location alone is
  not rights-based practice.
- Peer participation can improve relevance and trust, but tokenistic inclusion,
  unpaid labour or over-reliance on a small group can reproduce inequity.
- Recovery is not linear. A temporary admission or intensive support may be
  compatible with autonomy when consent, safeguards, review and relationship are
  respected; the ledger must preserve exceptions and reversals.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: WHO evidence documents the global
transition gap, system-wide requirements, coercion concerns and lived-experience
governance mechanisms. Promote as **Tier 2 conversion question**:
deinstitutionalization-to-community-recovery-and-lived-agency, requiring named
evidence from discharge or crisis route through housing, care continuity,
choice, participation, safety, work/education and remedy.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | hospital closure, policy, community centre, peer programme, crisis alternative or anti-coercion reform | Global reform need and policy direction are well documented |
| Mechanism | funding, housing, workforce, navigation, legal capacity, family/peer support, data and crisis response | System conditions are documented; local readiness varies |
| Operation | named person leaves, avoids or returns to care and receives coordinated support | Assessments and examples exist; longitudinal person-level routes are limited |
| Material consequence | stable housing, health, safety, relationships, education/work, reduced coercion or crisis recurrence | Outcomes are rarely joined across services and time |
| Meaning | dignity, identity, belonging, hope, autonomy, cultural fit and trust | Lived-experience principles are explicit; durable meaning is under-measured |
| Power | person controls decisions, data, complaint, appeal, budget influence and service design | Consultation is visible; realized equal partnership and remedy remain open |

## Next test

Select three place-bound transitions: a long-stay hospital supported-discharge
pathway, a community crisis alternative and a peer-led service. Trace one person
or cohort from admission/discharge decision through housing, income, care
navigation, medication or psychosocial support, family/peer relationship,
education/work, crisis recurrence, coercion, complaint and remedy. Record who
made decisions, whether lived-experience participants were paid and influential,
and whether the person could choose, refuse or change support.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for the transition architecture, global reform gap,
rights-based and coercion-free principles, lived-experience participation and
service-network requirements. It is weaker for comparable same-person evidence
that deinstitutionalization delivered durable recovery, safety, housing,
participation and agency without abandonment across geographies. This remains a
Tier 2 research opportunity, not a promoted causal finding.
