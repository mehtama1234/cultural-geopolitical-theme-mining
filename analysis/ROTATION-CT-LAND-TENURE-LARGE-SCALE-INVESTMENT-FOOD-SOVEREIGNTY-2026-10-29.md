# Rotation CT — Land tenure, large-scale investment and food sovereignty

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** land tenure / customary rights / large-scale land investment / agriculture / food sovereignty / land degradation / climate and biodiversity pressure / gender / Indigenous and rural authority / rural transformation
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 tenure-to-livelihood-and-food-security question

## Research question

When states, investors or climate and conservation programs formalize, lease,
acquire or reallocate land, what converts legal or financial control into secure
rights, productive and sustainable use, local livelihoods, food security,
customary authority and legitimate land-based development?

## Conversion chain

```text
land, soil, water, forest, customary territory, farm, carbon or infrastructure need
  -> title, map, lease, purchase, concession, conservation or climate project
  -> consultation, consent, compensation, tenure recognition, access, finance, land-use rule and enforcement
  -> continued residence, farming, grazing, forest stewardship, local food supply, income, restoration and dispute resolution
  -> dignity, cultural continuity, food sovereignty, resilience, gender equity and legitimate development
  -> secure and sustainable land governance
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** land formalization, agricultural investment, carbon removal,
  conservation, renewable energy and restoration projects are promoted as
  productivity, climate, biodiversity or food-system solutions.
- **Mechanism:** documentation, customary recognition, mapping, valuation,
  consultation, consent, compensation, finance, infrastructure, water access,
  enforcement and gendered authority determine who can use or lose land.
- **Operation:** a state, investor or project maps, titles, leases, purchases,
  restricts or restores land; households and communities farm, graze, move,
  negotiate, contest or adapt; authorities process claims and disputes.
- **Material consequence:** tenure security can support investment, credit,
  conservation and food production, while insecure or transferred rights can
  produce displacement, lost commons, lower food access, debt, conflict and
  ecological harm.
- **Meaning:** land is home, livelihood, ancestor, identity, commons and
  political authority—not only an asset. A title may increase security for one
  person while excluding a customary user, tenant, pastoralist, woman or youth.
- **Power:** governments define legal recognition; investors control capital and
  information; customary authorities, women, smallholders and mobile pastoralists
  may hold legitimate but undocumented claims; courts and grievance systems
  determine whether claims become enforceable.

## Bounded finding

> **Land title, investment, concession, conservation or climate-project control
> is not secure livelihood, food sovereignty or legitimate land governance.** A
> cadastral record, lease, purchase, carbon project, protected area, restoration
> plan, compensation package or land-use policy does not establish that named
> people retained access, received fair recognition and consent, maintained
> livelihoods and food, gained equitable benefits, protected cultural authority,
> or obtained effective remedy.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Land governance is experienced through place and social identity. A boundary
that appears empty on a map may contain seasonal grazing, sacred sites, fuel,
water, seed exchange, burial grounds or women’s livelihood routes. Formal title
can protect smallholders from rival claims, but it can also convert collective
or customary authority into an individualized asset that is easier to sell or
lose. Large-scale climate and conservation projects may protect carbon while
restricting food, mobility and cultural practice. The relevant outcome is not
only hectares titled or capital invested; it is whether communities can remain,
produce, steward, participate and contest decisions on terms they recognize as
legitimate.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Formal documentation can materially reduce conflict and transaction costs;
  insecurity should not be inferred from every title or land registry.
- Investment can raise productivity, jobs or infrastructure when rights,
  participation and distribution are credible.
- Customary tenure is not uniform; authority can be internally unequal,
  particularly for women, youth, migrants and minority clans.
- Community consent can be negotiated under unequal information, urgency or
  dependency; a signed agreement does not prove free, informed and continuing
  consent.
- Compensation can be paid while access to commons, food, identity and future
  options are lost; cash is not equivalent to land or cultural continuity.
- Land-use and food outcomes vary with soil, water, markets, climate, crop,
  tenure duration and local institutions; global averages cannot close a local
  conversion.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: documentation, recognition, transaction,
consent, compensation, access, production, food, ecology, authority and remedy
are separate stages. Keep the tenure-to-livelihood-and-food-security claim in
Tier 2 until a named land intervention joins rights and transaction terms to
continued access, production, income, food, ecological condition, gender and
customary authority, dispute resolution and long-term benefit distribution.

## Next test

Select one land-certification program, one agricultural or renewable-energy
concession and one conservation or carbon project. Record claimant subgroup,
customary use, gender, lease/title terms, map, consultation, consent,
compensation, displacement, access to water/common resources, production,
income, food purchases, ecological change, grievance, remedy and benefit
distribution at baseline, 1, 3 and 5 years. Compare titled and undocumented
users, women and men, resident and seasonal users, and project and neighboring
communities.
