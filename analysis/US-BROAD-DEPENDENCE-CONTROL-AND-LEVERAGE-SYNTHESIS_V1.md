# Dependence, control, and leverage across scales

**Checked:** 2026-09-25  
**Status:** bounded cross-source synthesis; no pooled leverage estimate  
**Scope:** labor markets, platforms, infrastructure, and geopolitical supply

## The cross-cutting question

When a person, firm, community, or state depends on one route, supplier,
employer, platform, or infrastructure system, what turns dependence into
control—and what prevents that control from becoming actual leverage?

The atlas contains a recurring structure:

```text
dependence on a route, supplier, employer, platform, or system
  -> cost of refusal, switching, delay, or non-use
  -> visibility of status and available alternatives
  -> bargaining, adaptation, contestation, or acceptance
  -> institutional or market response
  -> changed autonomy, trust, political meaning, or external leverage
```

The sources below do not measure one common form of dependence. They show a
shared mechanism at different scales and must remain separate evidence layers.

## What the source layers show

| Scale | Source evidence | Directly visible | Still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worker and local politics | NBER employer electoral-monopsony study | Local employer concentration is associated with reported employer political communication and influence; its shift-share analysis reports higher Republican turnout in more concentrated labor markets | Same worker's message, belief, vote, coercion, welfare, or later ability to act |
| Consumer/platform | NBER randomized search experiment | Active choice alone produced a small market-share change; temporary exposure to Bing changed quality beliefs and some continued preference | Durable switching, privacy/security, accessibility, broad welfare, or political action |
| Connected data and services | FTC/GM OnStar record | Formal deletion, opt-out, location, and unenrollment routes exist, with model, identity, timing, and safety-service conditions | Actual request completion, verified deletion, residual collection, service trade-off, trust, or exit |
| Infrastructure and firms | PJM and Virginia large-load records | Data-center load can create operational stress and new ride-through, tariff, minimum-demand, cost-allocation, and tax-administration rules | Named facility payment, household bill, feeder reliability, emissions, local acceptance, or replaceability |
| International supply | SIPRI arms-transfer data | The United States accounted for 42% of global major-arms exports in 2021–25, 48% of European imports, and 58% of European NATO imports | Delivery, sustainment, readiness, recipient autonomy, consent, alliance obedience, deterrence, or strategic leverage |

## The synthesis

### Dependence changes the price of saying no

A worker may depend on a dominant local employer for wages and career
expectations. A consumer may depend on a default search engine or connected
vehicle service. A community may depend on a transmission or hospital system.
An ally may depend on a foreign supplier for major weapons.

In each case, the relevant alternative is not merely whether another option
exists. It is whether the actor can reach it without losing income, time,
information, safety, service quality, local belonging, or strategic readiness.

### Control is strongest when alternatives are costly and status is opaque

The platform and GM records show that a formal choice route can coexist with
friction: defaults, model-year eligibility, identity verification, processing
windows, safety exceptions, and loss of valued services. The labor and
infrastructure records show a similar problem at a larger scale: workers,
ratepayers, and communities may be affected by a system whose internal rules
are difficult to observe or contest.

This makes **status visibility** a cross-cutting currency. A person needs to
know whether a request was received and corrected. A ratepayer needs to know
which customer class pays. An ally needs to know whether a capability is
available, maintained, and replaceable. A public record of a rule or contract
is not the same as practical control.

### Dependence does not automatically produce leverage

The SIPRI record measures supplier concentration, not political obedience. The
PJM record shows that large-load dependence can produce governance and
operating responses, but not yet who paid or whose service changed. The search
experiment shows that exposure to an alternative can update beliefs and create
some persistence, which is a counterexample to treating users as permanently
locked in.

The broader distinction is:

```text
dependence = exposure to the cost of refusal
control = ability to shape another actor's available route
leverage = observed change in that actor's behavior, decision, or autonomy
```

The atlas should not promote the first into the third without an attributed
response, concession, substitution, refusal, or durable change.

### Political meaning can appear before observable coercion

The employer study is important even with its limits because workers can
interpret local job dependence politically before a researcher can observe a
direct threat or message-to-vote sequence. Likewise, households may interpret
large-load cost allocation, data collection, or service dependence as a
question of fairness and public control before a bill, deletion result, or
alliance decision is directly linked.

That is a hypothesis for the cultural and political lanes—not a claim that
dependence produces one party preference or one legitimacy response. The next
test must preserve attribution, information, identity, and action separately.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Alternative-search exposure changed some beliefs and continued preference,
  showing that dependence can be softened by information and trial access.
- Backup generation and grid-operator intervention may limit immediate public
  effects during a large-load event; operational stress is not proof of a
  household outage or unfair cost shift.
- Arms-transfer concentration can coexist with recipient diversification,
  domestic production, resistance, or political bargaining.
- Employer communication can provide information rather than coercion, and
  concentrated employment may correlate with place, industry, education, media,
  migration, or existing party composition.
- A formal privacy or switching route may be genuinely useful for some users
  even when it is not equally available to all users.

## What this adds to the 14-theme atlas

This synthesis connects:

- firm and labor-market power;
- consumer power, platforms, data, and exit;
- infrastructure, energy, technology, and dependency;
- unequal exposure and practical alternatives;
- trust, legitimacy, and political meaning; and
- geopolitical and state consequences.

The recurring subtheme is **unequal replaceability**: the actor with fewer
credible substitutes may carry more cost, accept more monitoring, have less
political room, or become strategically dependent even when the system offers
a formal right to choose.

## Next decisive tests

1. A worker record linking local employer dependence to a dated political
   communication, belief, action, and later employment choice.
2. A platform or connected-service record linking a real control request to
   response, residual exposure, continued use, switching, or exit.
3. A named infrastructure account or project linking operating output to
   payment, service quality, local burden, replaceability, and acceptance.
4. An accepted defense capability linked to maintenance, partner choice,
   attributed external response, or changed bargaining behavior.

Until those tests exist, the defensible cross-source claim is that dependence
is becoming a shared structure across social, consumer, institutional,
infrastructure, and geopolitical life—but its conversion into control or
leverage remains conditional and must be demonstrated separately in each lane.
