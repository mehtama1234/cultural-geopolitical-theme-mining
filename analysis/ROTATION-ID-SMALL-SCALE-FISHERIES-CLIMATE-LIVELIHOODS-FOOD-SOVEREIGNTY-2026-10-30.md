# Rotation ID — Small-scale fisheries, climate livelihoods and food sovereignty

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When climate-resilient fisheries, marine conservation, landing infrastructure and blue-economy finance are introduced, do small-scale fishing communities gain secure access, income, nutrition, safety and cultural continuity—or are benefits captured by larger actors while access, risk and uncertainty remain local?

## Why this packet matters

Small-scale fisheries are simultaneously livelihood, food system, ecological practice, gendered labor system and cultural identity. The relevant chain is:

```text
fishery / coast / climate pressure / governance rule
  -> access rights, safety and community voice
  -> boat, landing, cold chain, finance and information
  -> catch, processing, market and value capture
  -> income, food/nutrition and social protection
  -> ecosystem stewardship and climate adaptation
  -> cultural continuity, dignity and intergenerational viability
```

Infrastructure and conservation can help, but can also restrict access, raise compliance costs, displace customary practices or privilege formal firms. Women’s post-harvest work and subsistence fishing are often undercounted.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| ID1 | FAO reports small-scale fisheries support livelihoods for approximately 46 million people in Asia and the Pacific, with women representing 40% of those involved and the sector contributing nearly half the region’s catch; climate change and limited policy/financial support remain challenges. | Regional scale, gendered contribution and constraint framing. | Household income, food intake, access rights, safety or ecological sustainability. |
| ID2 | FAO’s 2025 global synthesis says nearly half of small-scale-fishing livelihoods depend at least partly on the sector and that it generates 44% of the economic value of all fisheries landed. | Global livelihood/economic importance. | Distribution of value, women’s receipt, nutrition, tenure, labor conditions or resilience. |
| ID3 | World Bank’s 2025 Guinea Kounki Fisheries and Aquaculture Project provides $100 million for climate resilience, value chains, disaster-risk infrastructure, climate-smart practices and community-based fisheries/mangrove management. | Named public investment and integrated resilience/value-chain mechanism. | Fisher household receipt, access rights, income, nutrition, ecosystem outcome or local authority. |
| ID4 | World Bank’s 2025 Tanzania fisheries financing combines sustainable marine management, economic opportunity and locally selected climate-resilience activities; fish supplies 30% of mainland animal protein and 90% in Zanzibar. | Food/nutrition significance and community-choice mechanism. | Catch, price, household nutrition, distribution, participation, conservation or income. |
| ID5 | FAO’s Namibia initiative uses gender-transformative and human-rights-based approaches, community associations and value addition to build climate-resilient, equitable food systems and livelihoods. | Named governance, gender and value-addition approach. | Women’s control of value, safety, earnings, catch, food security or ecosystem outcome. |
| ID6 | World Bank’s Yemen SFISH programme describes fisheries as a source of food, identity and dignity amid conflict and climate pressure, with community-based management plans and mariculture opportunities under development. | Conflict-sensitive cultural and livelihood framing. | Security at sea, access rights, income, displacement, recovery, ecological sustainability or durable governance. |
| ID7 | FAO’s solar cold-chain guide treats small-scale fishing as household-level activity using minimal capital and small boats and explores energy solutions for post-harvest loss and value. | Technology/value-chain opportunity. | Affordability, maintenance, ownership, gendered labor, market access, emissions or food-quality outcome. |
| ID8 | World Ocean Assessment identifies gender, identity, displacement, labor precarity, safety at sea, social protection and wellbeing as factors shaping how small-scale-fishing benefits are distributed, especially for Indigenous fisheries. | Rights, distribution and wellbeing lens. | A comparable community chain through policy, catch, value, nutrition, safety and cultural continuity. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Fish is food, income, social identity and sometimes customary law. Climate change, marine protected areas, industrial fleets, port development, aquaculture, export markets and maritime security can change access and bargaining power. Women may be central to processing and trade without holding formal fishing rights. Conflict and displacement can break landing routes, cold chains, documents and social protection while transboundary fish stocks require regional governance.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A project can improve a port, cold chain or marine-management plan while small-scale fishers lose access or fail to capture value. A food-protein statistic is not proof of household nutrition. Community-based management language is not proof of decision power. Public evidence closes scale, investment design, technology opportunity, food significance, gender approach and rights framing, but not a comparable fisher/community chain through secure access, catch, income, nutrition, safety and cultural continuity.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Fisheries investment, marine conservation, climate-resilience infrastructure, value-chain planning, catch or protein contribution is not by itself secure access, safe work, fair income, household nutrition, ecological stewardship or cultural continuity.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **small-scale-fisheries-investment-to-secure-access-food-livelihood-and-cultural-continuity**. The strongest next acquisition is a named community/fishery chain linking access rights, climate pressure, governance voice, equipment/cold chain, catch, processing, market price, women’s value capture, food use, safety, social protection, ecosystem condition and intergenerational continuity.
