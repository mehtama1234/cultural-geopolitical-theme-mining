# Rotation ES — Health financial protection, care continuity and household security

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** universal health coverage / financial protection / affordability / chronic care / gender / poverty / household resilience

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes rural health access, AI healthcare, health-data continuity,
local medicine production, AMR, G2P payments, food security and care
infrastructure. This rotation isolates the financial route through healthcare:
whether a person can seek, complete and continue needed care without foregoing
care, selling assets, borrowing, reducing food or education, losing work, or
transferring unpaid care to another household member.

Service coverage and insurance ownership can coexist with medicines outside the
benefit package, copayments, transport, waiting, informal payments, diagnostic
fees, denied claims, provider shortages, debt, gendered control of money and
care interruptions. The unit is a named person or household’s care episode and
financial sequence, not an insurance enrolment or national coverage index.

## Research question

When a government, insurer, employer, health provider, donor or digital-health
platform expands universal coverage, benefits, subsidies, primary care,
essential medicines, claims systems or financial-risk protection, under what
conditions does it produce timely and continuous care without catastrophic
spending, forgone treatment, debt, livelihood loss or unequal burdens for women,
children, older people, disabled people, rural households, migrants and people
with chronic conditions?

## Conversion chain

```text
illness / prevention / chronic condition / pregnancy / injury / care need
  -> entitlement, benefit package, provider, medicine, claims and payment policy
  -> clinic, pharmacy, laboratory, hospital, transport and household encounter
  -> care reached, paid, completed, followed up and financially survived
  -> health, function, work, schooling, caregiving, food, housing and resilience
  -> trust, agency, equity, accountability and remedy
```

The unit must be a named patient, caregiver, household, condition, episode,
provider, medicine, claim or payment with dates, location, eligibility, wait,
price, travel, denial, borrowing, forgone care, treatment and outcome fields.
Coverage, insurance, budget, claims processed or service contacts is an
intermediate signal—not proof of affordable, continuous or effective care.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Service coverage and financial protection are separate UHC pillars

WHO’s 2025 Global Monitoring Report estimates that 4.6 billion people lacked
full coverage for essential health services in 2023 and 2.1 billion experienced
financial hardship due to out-of-pocket health spending in 2022. WHO emphasizes
that progress on service coverage can coexist with financial hardship and that
both dimensions must advance together.

The conversion test must therefore follow the same person from need to service,
payment and later household consequence, not infer protection from coverage.

Source: [WHO UHC Global Monitoring Report 2025](https://www.who.int/teams/health-financing-and-economics/global-monitoring-report/2025).

### 2. Out-of-pocket spending can reduce access and deepen poverty

OECD’s 2025 health report states that weak financial protection can reduce access,
undermine health, deepen poverty and widen health and socioeconomic inequality.
Poorer households and people needing long-term treatment are especially exposed.
Pharmaceuticals account for a large share of out-of-pocket spending in OECD
countries, while catastrophic spending varies significantly across countries and
is most common among poorer households.

The material chain includes medicines, dental care, outpatient care, long-term
care, transport and informal costs. A low average burden can hide severe burden
for a subgroup.

Source: [OECD Health at a Glance 2025: financial hardship](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/financial-hardship-and-out-of-pocket-expenditure_4160e6d2.html).

### 3. Financial hardship can fall because people forgo care

WHO’s technical report warns that lower financial-hardship rates do not
necessarily mean better financial protection: people may avoid or delay care
because they cannot afford it. It also finds large within-country inequalities,
including higher hardship among poorer and rural populations.

This is a crucial negative-space test. “No catastrophic spending” can mean
successful protection, no need, free care—or care forgone. The packet must
record unmet need, delay, self-treatment, medicine splitting and untreated
conditions alongside spending.

Source: [WHO/World Bank UHC 2025 technical report](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/gho-documents/tracking-universal-health-coverage-global-report-2025.pdf?download=true&sfvrsn=f8cf10f6_11).

### 4. Entitlement is not a guarantee of protection

OECD notes that population entitlement to publicly financed healthcare is a
prerequisite but not a guarantee of financial protection. Copayment design,
exemptions, annual caps, primary-care coverage, provider distribution and
medicine policy determine whether a person can actually use the entitlement.

The care route must therefore include what is covered, where the provider is,
whether the claim is accepted, what is paid before and after care, and whether
the patient can appeal or change provider.

Source: [OECD financial protection and out-of-pocket evidence](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/financial-hardship-and-out-of-pocket-expenditure_4160e6d2.html).

### 5. Place, income and household composition change financial exposure

WHO’s Indonesia evidence reports that around 26.6% of the population still
experienced financial hardship from health spending, with particularly high risk
among the poorest families and rural families or households with children or
older members—even where insurance existed. These differences show why
population coverage and household experience must be separated.

Transport, waiting, lost wages, caregiving, language and informal payments can
turn nominally free care into unaffordable care. The route also has gendered
effects when women control less cash or absorb unpaid care after a family member
becomes ill.

Source: [WHO Indonesia UHC and financial-protection evidence](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news-room/item/12-12-2025-indonesia-moves-forward-on-universal-health-coverage-while-addressing-remaining-gaps).

### 6. Chronic and long-term conditions expose continuity failures

People needing long-term treatment are particularly vulnerable to recurring
medicine, monitoring, transport and consultation costs. A single subsidized visit
can be helpful while a chronic care route still fails through stock-outs, denied
claims, referral delays, price inflation or loss of work.

The outcome is not simply whether a claim was paid. It is whether a named person
maintained treatment, function, work, school, caregiving, food, housing and
health without unsustainable household tradeoffs.

## Bounded proposition 150

**A universal-health-coverage policy, insurance card, benefit package, free-care
promise, subsidy, primary-care expansion, essential-medicine programme, claims
platform, exemption, annual cap or health-financing reform is not by itself
affordable access, treatment completion, health, financial protection, care
continuity, household resilience, gender equality or trust.** Coverage indexes,
enrolment, budgets, claims processed, visits, reimbursements, facilities or
out-of-pocket averages do not establish that a named person could reach and
continue needed care, avoid forgone treatment and harmful debt, protect food,
housing, work and caregiving, or obtain correction and remedy when payment or
coverage failed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Care is a household financial decision.** Illness redistributes money,
   time, food, work and unpaid care across kin, often along gender and age lines.
2. **Insurance is interpreted through experience.** A card creates trust only
   when providers accept it, medicines are available and claims are corrected.
3. **Affordability is place-based.** Distance, transport, waiting, provider
   density and rural supply chains convert nominal coverage into different real
   prices.
4. **Chronic care makes protection recurring.** A one-time subsidy can coexist
   with years of medicine, monitoring and lost-income exposure.
5. **Financial protection is social protection.** Avoiding health debt protects
   food, housing, education, work and dignity; failing to do so can reproduce
   poverty and distrust.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Falling catastrophic spending can reflect successful protection or people
  forgoing care; both must be measured.
- Insurance enrolment can rise while providers, medicines, diagnostics or claims
  remain unavailable.
- A free consultation can still require unaffordable transport, tests, medicine,
  informal payment, lost wages or caregiver time.
- An annual cap can protect high-cost patients while routine medicines and
  outpatient fees still burden poor households.
- A national average can improve while rural, migrant, disabled, older or
  female-headed households experience worsening access.
- Digital claims can speed reimbursement while excluding people without phones,
  documents, literacy, connectivity or a correct health record.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, World Bank
and OECD evidence supports the coverage, financial-hardship, forgone-care,
place, chronic-care and household mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same patient and household
through need, payment, care completion, debt, work, caregiving and remedy.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the coverage, financial-hardship, forgone-care,
place, chronic-care and household mechanisms. It does not close named care
completion, health, debt, food/housing protection, work, caregiving, treatment
continuity, claim fairness or remedy outcomes. Those remain acquisition targets.
