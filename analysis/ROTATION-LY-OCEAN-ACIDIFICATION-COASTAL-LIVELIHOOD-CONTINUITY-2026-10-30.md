# Rotation LY — Ocean acidification, coastal livelihoods and cultural continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Lane:** ocean ecology / fisheries / food security / coastal geography / cultural livelihood / adaptation governance  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded chemistry-to-livelihood distinction plus Tier 2 monitoring-to-community-continuity question

## Research question

When ocean acidification, warming and other coastal stressors alter marine
species and habitats, what converts monitoring and adaptation into secure food,
income, safety, cultural practice and community continuity—and who bears the
cost when access, species range or ecosystem function changes?

## Conversion chain

```text
CO2 uptake, coastal chemistry and compound stressors
  -> pH/oxygen/temperature monitoring and species/ecosystem signal
  -> forecast, fishery rule, aquaculture adaptation, insurance or livelihood
     diversification
  -> catch, food, income, safety, reef protection and cultural practice
  -> durable household/community continuity, bargaining power and just access
```

## Bounded finding

> **Ocean monitoring or adaptation finance is not coastal livelihood continuity.**
> A pH trend, vulnerability map, regional network, fishery plan, insurance
> payout, aquaculture intervention or blue-economy project can close a science,
> planning or financial stage while a named fisher, household, women’s value-
> chain worker, Tribe, subsistence community or coastal place's food, income,
> safety, cultural practice, authority and durable adaptation remain unobserved.

## Geographic and cultural interpretation

Ocean acidification is global in cause but local in exposure and meaning. A
shellfish grower, Indigenous fisher, tourism worker, coastal household and
government regulator may experience the same chemistry through different
species, seasons, tenure, markets, food practices and fallback options. Coastal
upwelling, freshwater, nutrient runoff and circulation can make local conditions
depart from the global average, complicating both attribution and policy.

The cultural loss is not reducible to catch volume. Species range, landing sites,
seasonal knowledge, ceremony, diet, intergenerational teaching and women’s
post-harvest work can change even where aggregate fish production is stable.
Adaptation can protect one group while shifting access, costs or ecological
burden to another. Monitoring is therefore a governance resource only when
communities can interpret it, contest decisions and use it to secure safe and
affordable alternatives.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Some fisheries and aquaculture operations are adapting, and the Caribbean
  insurance payout shows that financial protection can deliver a concrete
  post-shock response; neither proves acidification-specific or durable welfare.
- Global pH change does not identify the local timing, species response or causal
  contribution of acidification relative to warming, hypoxia, runoff and fishing.
- A vulnerability assessment can improve visibility without changing tenure,
  access, compensation or decision power.
- Reef or fishery protection can improve ecological conditions while costs or
  restrictions are shifted to small-scale, subsistence or women’s workers.
- Fish catch or revenue can remain stable through species substitution while
  food culture, safety, access and intergenerational livelihood continuity
  deteriorate.

## Promotion decision

Promote the chemistry-to-coastal-vulnerability distinction to Tier 1. Keep the
monitoring-to-community-continuity, food-security, cultural-practice and just-
adaptation claims in Tier 2 until a named place and cohort are followed through
observation, decision, access, adaptation, cost, ecological response and later
livelihood/cultural outcome with a contrasting community or non-adapting case.

## Next acquisition

Build a paired coastal ledger for one shellfish or fisheries community and one
contrasting coastal place. Record chemistry/temperature/oxygen, species and
catch, warning/forecast, rule, tenure/access, adaptation cost, insurance or
finance, landing/market, household food and income, safety, women’s work,
Indigenous/community authority, cultural practice, grievance and later
ecological and livelihood outcome. Preserve both an adaptation success and a
community that remains exposed or loses access.
