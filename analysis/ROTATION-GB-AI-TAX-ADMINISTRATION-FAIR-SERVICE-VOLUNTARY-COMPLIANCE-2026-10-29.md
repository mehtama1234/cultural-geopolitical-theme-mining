# Rotation GB — AI tax administration, fair public service and voluntary compliance

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological, institutional, social, geographical and public-finance systems

## Why this rotation exists

Tax administrations are adopting AI for fraud detection, risk assessment,
case selection, virtual assistants, personalised guidance and staff support.
The administrative promise is improved accuracy, reduced burden and better
revenue mobilisation. The public outcome is more demanding: a taxpayer must be
able to understand an obligation, access a service, correct data, challenge a
decision, receive a fair refund or appeal and still regard the system as
impartial. Predictive targeting can improve collection while concentrating
burden on groups with weak records, language barriers, informal income or less
ability to contest.

## Research question

When a tax authority uses AI for service, risk scoring, fraud detection,
personalisation or enforcement, under what conditions does it produce fair,
understandable and accessible public service and voluntary compliance rather
than opaque targeting, unequal burden, privacy loss, administrative error or
revenue extraction without visible public value?

## Conversion chain

```text
tax rule / taxpayer data / return / query / risk signal / AI recommendation
  -> taxpayer receives understandable service or administrative action
  -> filing, payment, refund, audit, appeal, correction or resolution
  -> fair burden, service access, certainty, compliance or public value
  -> trust, legitimacy, voluntary compliance or contestation
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD reports that 29 of 38 OECD members used AI in tax administration in its
   2024 inventory, with use spanning detection, risk assessment, virtual
   assistants and support to officials. Adoption is an operating signal, not
   evidence of fair taxpayer outcomes.
2. OECD's 2025 analysis identifies tax administration as especially demanding
   because it holds sensitive data, must protect taxpayer rights to challenge
   decisions, and depends on trust in impartiality and fairness for voluntary
   compliance.
3. OECD reports that 74.4% of administrations using AI reported fraud or
   evasion detection use and 64.1% reported risk-assessment use. It also notes
   that no surveyed administration reported AI making final administrative
   decisions in the referenced 2024 inventory. This is an important boundary:
   assisted enforcement is not the same as autonomous adjudication.
4. OECD's 2026 tax-administration discussion warns that predictive systems can
   infer future behaviour rather than rely only on verified past behaviour,
   increasing the importance of fairness, proportionality, explainability and
   taxpayer rights.
5. The IMF's 2025 technical note describes generative AI use cases for
   compliance-risk analysis and treats them as capability and proof-of-concept
   support, not evidence that generated recommendations are correct or legally
   sufficient.
6. The World Bank's 2025 Armenia case records an AI pilot for compliance,
   risk management and fraud detection, emphasising leadership, capacity and
   solving concrete administrative problems. A pilot does not close the chain
   to taxpayer burden, appeals, refunds, trust or voluntary compliance.

## Bounded proposition

**An AI tax chatbot, risk score, fraud alert, audit-selection model,
personalised notice, automated workflow, compliance pilot or revenue increase
is not by itself fair taxation, understandable service, due process, accurate
assessment, equitable burden, visible public value or voluntary compliance.** A
model recommendation, contact, audit, payment, refund or closed case does not
establish that named taxpayers understood the rule, could correct their data,
receive proportionate treatment, challenge the decision, obtain a usable remedy
or trust the system's impartiality.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Tax administration is experienced through language, formality, income pattern,
place, digital access and the state's prior relationship with communities. A
model trained on formal transactions may misread informal businesses, seasonal
workers, migrants, cash economies or households sharing addresses. A digital
service may be efficient for a connected taxpayer and exclusionary for someone
with poor connectivity, disability, limited literacy or no safe private device.
Trust also depends on whether taxpayers can see a connection between collection
and public value; efficient extraction without fair service can weaken rather
than strengthen legitimacy. Local offices, interpreters, human assistance and
community intermediaries remain part of the conversion mechanism.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- AI may improve case selection or reduce staff workload; risk controls should
  not be read as proof that automation is inherently harmful.
- Higher compliance or revenue may reflect enforcement pressure, economic
  growth, taxpayer education, changed rules or one-time settlements, not
  voluntary compliance or visible public value.
- A human officer reviewing an AI recommendation may correct it, or may lack
  time, evidence or authority to challenge it; human involvement is not a
  sufficient safeguard.
- A chatbot can reduce waiting time while giving incorrect, inaccessible or
  legally nonbinding advice; service speed is not service accuracy.
- More data can improve detection but also expand privacy, purpose-limitation,
  cross-border and false-positive risks.
- Aggregate error rates can conceal concentrated burden on small businesses,
  migrants, informal workers, minority-language taxpayers or people with
  disabilities.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: AI tax administration is a high-consequence
conversion problem governed by data quality, explainability, proportionality,
human authority, appeal and public trust. Keep the named taxpayer-to-fair
service-and-voluntary-compliance chain as **Tier 2** until case-level outcomes,
burden distribution, remedy and later compliance are observable.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | adoption, use case, risk score, pilot, revenue or service claim | strong OECD and country evidence |
| Mechanism | data quality, privacy, model objective, explainability, proportionality and access | strong bounded evidence; implementation varies |
| Operation | named taxpayer receives service, notice, audit, refund, decision or appeal route | partial; administrative examples exist |
| Material consequence | burden, payment, refund, error correction, service access or public-value receipt | not closed for a general AI claim |
| Meaning | fairness, intelligibility, dignity, trust and fiscal legitimacy | not closed; needs taxpayer-informed follow-up |
| Power | contestation, remedy, behavioural change, voluntary compliance and institutional learning | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one AI-assisted tax process and trace named, anonymized cases from data
input to notice, staff decision, appeal, correction, refund or payment. Compare
outcomes by language, geography, business form, income volatility, disability
and digital access. Measure false positives, time and cost to comply, human
override quality, appeal success, resolution time, privacy complaints, visible
public-service receipt and later filing/payment behaviour. Separate enforced
collection from voluntary compliance.

## Source boundary

The public record closes adoption, use-case, risk, rights and governance
mechanisms. It does not justify a universal claim that AI tax administration
produces fair burdens, accurate service, visible public value, trust or
voluntary compliance for named taxpayers. Those later outcomes remain
acquisition targets.
