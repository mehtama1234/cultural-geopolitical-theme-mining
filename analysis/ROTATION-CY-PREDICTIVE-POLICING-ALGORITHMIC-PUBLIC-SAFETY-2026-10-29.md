# Rotation CY — Predictive policing, algorithmic public safety and geographic legitimacy

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** predictive policing / hotspot mapping / police data / algorithmic profiling / facial recognition / ANPR / feedback loops / racialized geography / community safety / public trust / remedy
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 predictive-public-safety-to-trusted-and-equitable-community-safety question

## Research question

When police use historical data, predictive models, facial recognition or
automated alerts to allocate attention and force, how does that capability
change who is watched, stopped, searched, detained or helped across places—and
what evidence would show improved safety without reproducing historical
over-policing or reducing public legitimacy?

## Conversion chain

```text
reported incidents, calls, arrests, stops, images, sensors, social data and place history
  -> data cleaning, model training, hotspot/person/network score, face/plate match,
     alert, patrol allocation or investigative lead
  -> officer interpretation, deployment, encounter, stop, search, seizure, arrest,
     force, referral, service response, complaint and record creation
  -> exposure, error, disparate burden, fear, deterrence, reduced or increased harm,
     community voice, correction, discipline and remedy
  -> safe streets, equal treatment, privacy, civic participation, trust and legitimate
     community safety
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** a police agency announces a predictive tool, real-time camera
  network, facial-recognition capability, hotspot map, violence-reduction
  strategy or accuracy claim.
- **Mechanism:** historical records and sensor data transform place, person,
  association or image into a risk score, match, patrol allocation or lead.
- **Operation:** an officer or analyst receives the output and decides whether
  to watch, stop, search, question, arrest, use force, refer or ignore; the
  resulting event becomes new data.
- **Material consequence:** residents experience safety, disruption, delay,
  surveillance, fear, injury, service referral, arrest, record creation or
  reduced victimization, unevenly distributed by neighbourhood and subgroup.
- **Meaning:** the same deployment may be understood as protection by one
  group and racialized occupation, automated suspicion or loss of civic space by
  another.
- **Power:** police, vendors, courts, oversight bodies, local officials and
  residents have unequal ability to inspect the model, challenge records,
  change deployment or refuse the data relationship.

## Bounded finding

> **A predictive-policing model, facial-recognition match, hotspot map or
> public-safety deployment is not by itself safer, fairer or more legitimate
> policing.** A model score, accuracy rate, patrol allocation, camera network,
> arrest reduction, crime decline, audit, policy or community-safety claim does
> not establish that named residents experienced less harm, equal treatment,
> privacy, meaningful explanation, correction, non-discriminatory encounters,
> effective remedy or durable trust.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The place in a police dataset is not a neutral container. It may encode where
people report crime, where police patrol, where residents distrust or avoid
police, where housing and transport concentrate, and where surveillance is most
available. A model can therefore convert earlier institutional attention into a
forecast of future attention. Young people, Black and Indigenous residents,
migrants, disabled people, unhoused residents and people with mental-health
needs may experience the same score or camera as a different level of risk.
Language, disability, local history and cultural authority affect whether a
resident can understand, report, contest or cooperate with a system. Legitimacy
requires a community to see safety gains without becoming the raw material for
permanent suspicion, and to have a credible route to correction and remedy.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Data-informed deployment can help allocate scarce resources when the target,
  data provenance, use limits, human judgment and outcome measures are valid.
- Correlation between a model score and later police activity is not evidence
  of prevented crime; it may measure police exposure and reporting patterns.
- A demographic disparity does not by itself prove intentional discrimination,
  but it is a material signal requiring disaggregated validation and causal
  investigation.
- Facial or plate matches can assist investigation while still producing false
  matches, chilling effects, privacy harms or unequal surveillance.
- A citywide crime decline cannot be attributed to a model without a named
  deployment, comparison, timing, implementation record and alternative causes.
- Community consultation is not community consent or safety; participation must
  connect to data rules, deployment decisions, correction and remedy.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: data signal, model mechanism, officer
operation, geographic exposure, resident consequence, community meaning and
institutional legitimacy are separate stages. Keep the predictive-public-
safety-to-trusted-and-equitable-community-safety claim in Tier 2 until a named
agency joins deployment, data, subgroup exposure, encounters, safety outcome,
complaints, correction, remedy, alternatives and sustained resident trust.

## Next test

Select one named agency and tool. Record the data sources, historical policing
patterns, model purpose, vendor, geography, protected-group performance,
validation, audit, officer workflow, alert, encounter, stop/search/arrest/force,
victim or service outcome, complaint, record correction, discipline, community
engagement and longer-term safety and trust. Compare model-targeted areas with
matched areas and compare residents by race, ethnicity, age, gender, disability,
income, housing status and language, while separating police exposure from
actual victimization and harm reduction.
