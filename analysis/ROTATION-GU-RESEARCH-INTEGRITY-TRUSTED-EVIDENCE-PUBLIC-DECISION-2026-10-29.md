# Rotation GU — research integrity, trusted evidence and public decision

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** research ethics, scientific integrity, incentives, assessment, AI in research and public trust

## Why this rotation exists

Open publication and data access can widen scrutiny, but openness does not
guarantee that evidence was honestly generated, appropriately interpreted,
fairly credited or responsibly communicated. Research systems are shaped by
funding, rankings, publication incentives, institutional power, commercial
interests and political pressure. Generative AI adds hidden prompts,
fabrication, authorship and accountability questions. The relevant conversion
is therefore from research practice to trusted evidence and a later public or
professional decision—not from publication to truth.

## Research question

When research is designed, funded, conducted, assessed, communicated and used,
under what conditions do integrity controls produce evidence that named people,
communities, professionals and public institutions can understand, contest and
use responsibly rather than polished output with hidden error, bias, pressure,
misconduct or unaccountable AI assistance?

## Conversion chain

```text
question / funding / protocol / data / analysis / authorship / peer review / communication
  -> ethical conduct, transparency, reproducibility, correction and accountable attribution
  -> evidence is judged appropriately by users and decision-makers
  -> policy, care, education, environment or livelihood decisions improve or avoid harm
  -> trust, legitimacy and knowledge agency persist across communities and places
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The European Commission treats research ethics and integrity as integral to
   Horizon Europe and promotes ethics-by-design, fundamental rights and the
   European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity. This closes an institutional
   expectation and appraisal mechanism, not the quality of every funded result.
2. The European Research Area's updated guidance on generative AI in research
   keeps accountability, transparency and responsibility central, and adds
   issues such as third-party interactions and hidden prompts. AI use therefore
   creates a research-integrity layer beyond ordinary tool adoption. Guidance
   does not prove that researchers disclosed or controlled every use in practice.
3. OECD's Global Science Forum identifies research integrity and research
   security as a continuing international policy project and notes the need for
   guidance, advisory structures and assessment tools. Cross-border research
   requires more than a local code because funding, data, institutions and
   pressures can span jurisdictions.
4. OECD's work on research assessment reports that quantity-oriented metrics can
   undermine ethics, diversity and innovation, while opaque rankings and
   commercial analytics can weaken institutional autonomy and trust. Assessment
   is therefore an operating mechanism: it changes what researchers optimise,
   whose knowledge is visible and how community-based or policy-relevant work is
   valued.
5. OECD PISA 2025 reports that fewer than half of students in OECD countries
   both check information-source credibility and place greater trust in
   scientific evidence than common sense. This is not evidence of research
   misconduct; it is a downstream public-capacity signal showing why integrity
   must connect to communication, education and users' ability to evaluate
   evidence.
6. The Swedish Research Council's Good Research Practice 2024 frames integrity
   as requiring ethical knowledge, implementation ability and ongoing discussion
   across the research process. Its living-document approach illustrates a
   positive institutional pathway, while also showing that integrity is a
   continuous culture and governance task rather than a one-time compliance
   certificate.

## Bounded proposition

**A research grant, ethics approval, preregistration, peer-reviewed article,
open dataset, citation count, integrity code, AI-use disclosure, institutional
investigation, ranking or public science communication is not by itself valid
evidence, reproducibility, fair attribution, trustworthy advice, safe policy,
better care, public understanding or legitimate decision-making.** A study can
be published, compliant or highly cited without establishing that its methods
were robust, its incentives were aligned, its affected communities were
represented, its errors were corrected, its limitations were understood or its
later use produced a beneficial outcome.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Integrity is experienced through unequal research systems. Language, funding,
infrastructure, disciplinary norms, legal protection, career stage, gender,
institutional prestige and proximity to decision-makers affect who can speak,
who is credited, whose data are collected and whose interpretation counts.
Global rankings and English-dominant publishing can make some work more visible
while under-valuing community-based, Indigenous, local-language or practice
knowledge. A universal code may establish common principles but not equal power
to challenge a senior researcher, funder, institution or government.

The public-decision link is also place-specific. A finding may be methodologically
sound yet poorly translated into a local service, culturally inappropriate or
misapplied outside its population. Trust should therefore be tested at the
encounter between evidence, interpreter, decision-maker and affected people,
with correction and remedy available when the chain breaks.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Peer review, ethics review, open methods and independent oversight can improve
  quality; the proposition rejects automatic equivalence, not these practices.
- A retraction or correction can strengthen integrity, but may arrive after
  clinical, policy, reputational or financial harm; visibility of the remedy
  matters.
- High citation or ranking performance may reflect real importance, language,
  network access or incentive gaming; it is not a standalone quality measure.
- AI can support translation, coding and literature work, but disclosure alone
  does not establish accurate outputs, responsible authorship or protection of
  confidential data.
- Public scepticism is not proof that science is wrong, and public trust is not
  proof that a claim is true. The atlas keeps epistemic quality, communication,
  legitimacy and downstream outcome as separate clocks.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official frameworks identify
integrity, ethical conduct, responsible AI use, assessment incentives and
public evidence literacy as system conditions. Promote as **Tier 2 conversion
question**: research-integrity-to-trusted-evidence-and-public-decision,
requiring named evidence from research practice through interpretation, use,
correction and material decision effect.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | code, grant condition, ethics review, AI guidance, assessment reform, publication or retraction | Official frameworks and reform activity are well documented |
| Mechanism | protocol, data, authorship, conflicts, review, incentives, disclosure, correction and oversight | Controls and failure modes are documented; implementation is uneven |
| Operation | named study/team/institution conducts and reports work with accountable participants and users | Institutional examples exist; cross-place same-study operating evidence is incomplete |
| Material consequence | decision, treatment, policy, education or environmental action improves or avoids harm | Public sources rarely join integrity practice to a later named outcome |
| Meaning | trust, credibility, dignity, recognition, epistemic inclusion and legitimacy | Surveys and principles exist; trust after a specific evidence encounter is under-traced |
| Power | ability to challenge, correct, withdraw, receive attribution and shape agenda or use | Formal procedures exist; realized power across hierarchy and geography remains open |

## Next test

Select three cases: an AI-assisted research project, a public-interest study
used in policy or care, and a misconduct or correction case. Trace the same
claim from question and funding through data, analysis, authorship, review,
communication, user interpretation, decision, correction and remedy. Record
language, community participation, conflicts, incentives, AI disclosure,
reviewer independence, time to correction and whether affected people could
contest or benefit from the result.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for integrity principles, institutional duties,
AI-specific research guidance, incentive risks, assessment reform and public
science-literacy signals. It is weaker for comparable same-unit evidence that
integrity controls produced durable, equitable public decisions or prevented
harm across geographies. This remains a Tier 2 research opportunity, not a
promoted causal finding.
