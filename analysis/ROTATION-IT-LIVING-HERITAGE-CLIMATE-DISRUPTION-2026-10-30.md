# Rotation IT — Living heritage, climate disruption and community resilience

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When climate hazards, displacement and environmental change threaten living heritage, do community-led safeguarding, education and disaster-risk integration protect transmission, livelihoods and local authority—or mainly document practices after their social and ecological conditions have already been damaged?

## Why this packet matters

Climate risk affects more than monuments. It can remove the habitats, materials, seasons, routes, livelihoods, elders, gathering places and social relationships that allow living heritage to be practiced and transmitted. At the same time, living heritage can be an active source of adaptation knowledge, preparedness and recovery.

```text
climate hazard / environmental change / displacement
  -> place, resource, livelihood and practitioner disruption
  -> community knowledge, safeguarding, education and DRR integration
  -> continued practice, adaptation, preparedness and recovery
  -> transmission, wellbeing, livelihood and local authority
  -> durable cultural-ecological resilience
```

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Living heritage is place-dependent but not place-static. Migration, relocation and diaspora can carry practices while also severing access to materials, sacred landscapes, seasonal cycles, language and collective authority. Pacific island, mountain, coastal and Indigenous communities face different hazard geographies, but the common question is whether adaptation protects the conditions of cultural reproduction rather than merely recording an endangered practice.

Climate policy can instrumentalize culture: a tradition may be celebrated as a resilience asset while its bearers lack land, income, services or decision power. Conversely, emergency standardization can erase local protocols. Youth involvement, community-controlled inventories and links between schools, cultural institutions and disaster agencies are promising mechanisms, but they remain intermediate stages until practice, wellbeing and authority are observed over time.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A heritage inventory, case study, emergency plan, cultural festival, education module, community meeting, DRR toolkit or adaptation narrative is not by itself continued practice, safe livelihood, cultural authority, youth transmission, ecological recovery or durable resilience. The evidence closes regional case framing, risk taxonomy, named Pacific and Nepal mechanisms, urban adaptation, emergency architecture and community-leadership principles, but no public source here closes a comparable community chain through hazard, resource, practice, livelihood, youth transmission and repeated recovery.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** A climate-risk map, heritage inventory, documentation project, safeguarding plan, DRR training, youth workshop, adaptation project or community consultation is not by itself living-practice continuity, safe livelihood, cultural authority, intergenerational transmission or durable resilience.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **climate-hazard-to-community-controlled-living-heritage-practice-and-resilient-livelihood**. The strongest next acquisition is a named community/practice/place chain linking hazard, habitat/material, practitioner, livelihood, warning, decision authority, safeguarding, youth learning, adaptation action, displacement, recovery, wellbeing and repeated practice.
