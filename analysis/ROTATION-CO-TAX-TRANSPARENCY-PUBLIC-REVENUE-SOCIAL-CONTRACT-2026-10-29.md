# Rotation CO — Tax transparency, public revenue and social-contract legitimacy

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** tax transparency / illicit financial flows / domestic resource mobilisation / public spending / fiscal federalism / tax morale / public trust / local services / geopolitical cooperation
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 tax-capacity-to-visible-public-value question

## Research question

When governments and international institutions improve tax transparency,
exchange financial information or mobilize domestic revenue, what converts
formal fiscal capacity into fair collection, visible public services, local
voice and durable social-contract legitimacy across regions and communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
tax base, cross-border income, asset ownership, public need or fiscal gap
  -> tax rule, registration, exchange of information, audit, collection, transfer or budget allocation
  -> compliance cost, enforcement, redistribution, procurement, leakage, local capacity and public visibility
  -> household payment, service access, infrastructure, health, education, safety and local participation
  -> perceived fairness, tax morale, trust, compliance, consent and institutional legitimacy
  -> resilient fiscal and social contract
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** tax transparency, beneficial ownership, automatic information
  exchange, anti-IFF action, fiscal reforms and revenue mobilization are often
  presented as development or governance improvements.
- **Mechanism:** registration, legal identity, reporting, audit, enforcement,
  cross-border cooperation, informal-sector treatment, procurement, transfers,
  corruption and budget execution determine who pays and where resources go.
- **Operation:** a taxpayer or firm is registered, assessed, audited or served;
  information crosses borders; a ministry or local government receives funds;
  procurement and service systems convert, lose or delay the money.
- **Material consequence:** revenue can finance education, health, transport,
  water and safety, but can also produce compliance burden, privacy exposure,
  arbitrary enforcement, regressive collection or no visible service.
- **Meaning:** people judge the fiscal contract through fairness, dignity,
  reciprocity and whether public institutions are present in their place—not
  through the existence of an exchange standard or a national revenue target.
- **Power:** international jurisdictions, finance ministries, tax authorities,
  firms, high-net-worth individuals, local governments, procurement actors and
  communities have unequal ability to shape rules, evade burden or demand proof
  of use.

## Bounded finding

> **Tax transparency, revenue mobilization or fiscal authority is not visible
> public value or social-contract legitimacy.** An information-exchange network,
> beneficial-ownership register, tax reform, audit, recovered revenue figure,
> budget allocation or fiscal-capacity index does not establish that named
> people were treated fairly, that leakage was prevented, that public funds
> reached their place, that services improved, or that taxpayers experienced a
> legitimate and reciprocal state.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The fiscal contract is experienced locally. A national revenue gain may coexist
with a rural clinic without staff, an informal worker facing repeated compliance
burden, a city receiving less than its population share, or a region whose tax
base is extracted while procurement and infrastructure flow elsewhere. The same
tax rule can be interpreted as reciprocity where services are visible and as
extraction where collection is coercive or opaque. Cross-border transparency can
reduce elite evasion while also increasing documentation and privacy burdens for
small firms, migrants and households whose financial lives cross jurisdictions.
Subnational budget execution, service completion and citizen voice are therefore
the cultural-geographical bridge.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Additional revenue can be real without producing additional public services;
  earmarking, debt service, procurement delay and leakage change the route.
- Higher tax morale can reflect identity, fear, social norms or a temporary
  political moment rather than observed service reciprocity.
- A transparency standard may improve detection without recovering money or
  changing the conduct of a powerful actor.
- Digital tax administration can reduce discretion and improve convenience but
  can also exclude informal, rural, low-literacy or poorly connected taxpayers.
- Public spending can increase while quality, accessibility or distribution
  worsens; budget size is not household-level receipt.
- Cross-country trust comparisons are not interchangeable with local legitimacy;
  language, history, state formation, conflict and service expectations matter.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: tax transparency, revenue, budget and trust
are separate stages. Keep the tax-capacity-to-visible-public-value claim in
Tier 2 until a named reform joins taxpayer exposure and enforcement to recovered
revenue, budget execution, subnational allocation, service completion,
distributional burden, complaint/remedy and changes in trust or compliance.

## Next test

Select one exchange-of-information or beneficial-ownership reform, one national
or subnational tax reform and one named public-service budget route. Record the
taxpayer or firm subgroup, geography, registration and enforcement burden,
revenue identified and actually collected, allocation, procurement, delivery,
service use, complaint, remedy and trust/compliance at 6/12/24 months. Compare
formal and informal firms, urban and rural places, and regions that pay into the
system but receive different levels of visible service.
