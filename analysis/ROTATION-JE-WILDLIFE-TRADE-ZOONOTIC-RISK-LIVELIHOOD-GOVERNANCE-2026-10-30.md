# Rotation JE — Wildlife trade, zoonotic risk and livelihood governance

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When governments, customs agencies, conservation groups and communities address legal and illegal wildlife trade, do enforcement, One Health surveillance, market regulation and livelihood programs reduce trafficking and disease risk while protecting rural agency—or mainly produce seizures, arrests, risk warnings and restrictions that displace markets and burdens?

## Why this packet matters

Wildlife trade is simultaneously an ecological, economic, cultural, public-health and criminal-justice system. People may hunt, trade, consume, keep or care for animals for food, medicine, income, identity or tradition; criminal networks may exploit the same routes and regulatory gaps. Public-health controls can reduce spillover risk, but poorly designed restrictions can remove income, increase concealment or stigmatize communities without changing high-level demand.

```text
species / market / route / livelihood / demand
  -> harvest, breeding, transport, sale, consumption and enforcement
  -> pathogen exposure, species pressure, income, food and cultural practice
  -> customs, surveillance, community voice, legal trade or alternative livelihood
  -> seizure, prosecution, health protection, conservation and trust
  -> durable wildlife governance with fair local benefit and accountable power
```

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Wildlife trade cannot be reduced to a simple legal/illegal binary. The same species can be protected, farmed, hunted, traded legally, traded informally or trafficked depending on jurisdiction and documentation. Conservation rules may be necessary, but legitimacy depends on whether communities helped define the rule, can comply without losing subsistence, and receive a fair share of lawful benefits.

The geography of enforcement is asymmetric. Source communities may experience raids, confiscation, travel restrictions and livelihood loss, while brokers, financiers and end-market consumers remain harder to reach. Border controls can shift routes rather than reduce demand. Customs and health agencies also need to share information without turning every trader, market worker or migrant into a presumptive criminal or disease carrier.

One Health is valuable precisely because it links human, animal and ecosystem outcomes, but it can become a slogan unless a named market or route shows who received protection, who bore cost, what changed in practice, and whether the intervention was culturally and economically workable. Geopolitical cooperation must therefore be tested through shared investigations, data governance, benefit sharing and behavior at source and end markets.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A seizure, arrest, prosecution, wildlife-listing decision, border inspection, market closure, risk assessment, surveillance platform, alternative-livelihood project or One Health workshop is not by itself lower trafficking, lower spillover risk, species recovery, food security, income protection, cultural legitimacy or durable deterrence. Seizures can increase because enforcement improved or because trafficking expanded; a market closure can shift sales online; and a new livelihood program can fail if it is inaccessible, culturally inappropriate or controlled externally.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Wildlife-trade enforcement, public-health controls, One Health surveillance, CITES listing, community consultation or alternative-livelihood programming is not by itself reduced trafficking, reduced zoonotic risk, conservation, fair livelihood security, cultural legitimacy or durable deterrence.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **wildlife-trade-governance-to-lower-trafficking-zoonotic-risk-and-fair-community-livelihood**. The strongest next acquisition is a named species/market/route/community chain linking demand, harvest, legal status, actor, pathogen exposure, customs, surveillance, enforcement, livelihood, food/cultural use, community voice, benefit, remedy, market substitution and later species/health outcomes.
