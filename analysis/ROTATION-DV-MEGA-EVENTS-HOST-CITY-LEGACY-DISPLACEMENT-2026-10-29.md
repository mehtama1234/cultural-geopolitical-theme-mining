# Rotation DV — Mega-events, host-city legacy and displacement

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** geopolitical / cultural / geographical / infrastructure / rights

## Why this rotation

The atlas has separate packets on sport infrastructure, tourism and resident
livelihoods, public space and cultural diplomacy. This rotation isolates the
mega-event as a concentrated geopolitical and urban-development intervention:
Olympics, World Cups, expos, cultural capitals and other events that bring
temporary global attention, accelerated construction, exceptional governance,
security and place branding.

The same event can create transport, housing, contracts, cultural pride and
international visibility while also creating debt, displacement, labour risk,
surveillance, restricted public space and post-event maintenance costs. The
critical distinction is between the event’s spectacle and its host-city legacy.

## Research question

When a city or state hosts a mega-event, under what conditions do accelerated
infrastructure, procurement, cultural programming and global visibility become
durable local assets, inclusive employment, affordable housing, civic belonging
and legitimate geopolitical reputation—and who is displaced, indebted,
excluded, surveilled or left with stranded assets after the event?

## Conversion chain

```text
bid / geopolitical ambition / urban-development need
  -> host contract, finance, land, construction, procurement and security
  -> event delivery, visitor movement, cultural programme and public attention
  -> venue/housing/transport use + jobs + local contracts + participation
  -> resident wellbeing, access, displacement, debt, environmental and cultural effects
  -> post-event governance, trust, reputation, reuse, maintenance or exit
```

The unit must be a named host project, neighbourhood, venue, worker, resident
group, contract or post-event facility. Visitor numbers, broadcast reach and
medal counts are signals, not proof of local development or legitimacy.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Legacy is now an explicit host requirement

The IOC requires potential Olympic hosts to put sustainability and legacy at
the heart of their proposals, align projects with long-term development plans,
and address social, economic, human-development and environmental factors,
public support and funding. Its venue evidence reports that 86% of permanent
venues across all Games and 94% of 21st-century venues remain in use.

These are important institutional commitments and a useful counterexample to
the claim that all venues become abandoned. They remain largely aggregate and
institutional; they do not establish equitable access, affordability,
maintenance finance or neighbourhood-level benefit.

Sources: [IOC host requirements](https://www.olympics.com/ioc/becoming-an-olympic-games-host/requirements-to-host-the-olympic-games)
and [IOC post-Games venue evidence](https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/olympic-legacy-in-images-understanding-the-lifecycle-of-olympic-venues).

### 2. UN-Habitat identifies displacement as a recurring mega-event risk

UN-Habitat’s displacement evidence identifies mega-events, including Olympic
Games, as potential drivers of development-induced displacement. It describes
evictions, informal-settlement clearance, street-vendor and homeless-person
“clean sweeps,” construction-related removal and housing-cost pressure linked to
land speculation.

This establishes a rights and geography mechanism. It does not mean every host
event displaces residents, but every event ledger should track those who leave,
cannot return, lose livelihood or experience changed access even if no formal
eviction is recorded.

Source: [UN-Habitat World Cities Report 2026, displacement chapter](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2026/05/wcr_2026_chapter_4.pdf)
and [UN-Habitat forced-evictions evidence](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/download-manager-files/Forced%20Evictions%20%2C%20Global%20Crisis%2C%20Global%20Solutions.pdf).

### 3. Events can create procurement and skills pathways

OECD’s Paris 2024 legacy work describes possible local-development benefits
through infrastructure, jobs, local contracts, social and solidarity economy
procurement, training, apprenticeships and pathways for underrepresented groups.
It emphasizes strategic procurement and social/environmental clauses as tools to
extend benefits beyond the event.

This is a plausible conversion mechanism, not a result. The test is whether
named local firms and workers received contracts, skills, payment and continued
work, rather than whether a procurement policy or jobs headline existed.

Source: [OECD, Paris 2024 jobs, procurement and SMEs](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/09/the-legacy-of-the-paris-2024-olympic-and-paralympic-games_fa7c7102/full-report/committed-games-creating-jobs-leveraging-the-social-and-solidarity-economy-and-generating-opportunities-for-smes_ac067f5d.html).

### 4. Post-event housing can be a real asset or a distributional promise

IOC host documentation requires Olympic villages to explain how they fit into a
long-term housing strategy and identify future ownership and housing types.
The Olympic Village can become social, student, market or other housing, but
the conversion depends on tenure, price, allocation, services, accessibility
and whether existing residents can remain or return.

Source: [IOC future-host housing legacy requirements](https://library.olympics.com/default/digitalCollection/DigitalCollectionAttachmentDownloadHandler.ashx?documentId=619121&parentDocumentId=619119&skipCopyright=true&skipWatermark=true).

### 5. Human rights and labour risk are part of event delivery, not externalities

The UN mega-sporting-events report documents the possibility of worker-rights
violations, migrant-worker risk, displacement of street workers and restrictions
on expression and protest around event preparation. It also identifies positive
opportunities for gender equity, minority-owned procurement, migrant rights,
universal facilities and LGBTQIA+ inclusion in host planning.

The evidence therefore supports a dual audit: events can widen access and
rights when protections are built into procurement and governance, but the
international spotlight does not guarantee rights protection.

Source: [UN, Using Mega Sporting Events to create a lasting legacy](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4025078/files/1386798EN.pdf).

## Bounded proposition 127

**An Olympic Games, World Cup, expo, cultural capital, major festival, host-city
bid, venue programme or event-led infrastructure plan is not by itself durable
local development, inclusive cultural belonging, affordable housing, decent
work, public legitimacy or geopolitical reputation.** A bid, broadcast reach,
visitor count, venue, contract, jobs estimate, security operation, medal result
or stated legacy plan does not establish that named residents retained housing
and livelihoods, workers received safe pay and remedy, local firms gained
lasting capability, public space remained accessible, debt and maintenance were
manageable, displaced people obtained restoration, or the host’s reputation
survived later rights and governance scrutiny.

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical interpretation

1. **The event compresses time but distributes costs over decades.** Political
   leaders receive visibility immediately; residents may bear debt, rent,
   maintenance or displacement long after the closing ceremony.
2. **Legacy is a governance relationship.** It depends on ownership, budgets,
   rules, access and accountability after the event organization dissolves.
3. **Global visibility magnifies both pride and contestation.** Cultural
   representation, protest, minority rights and labour conditions become part of
   the geopolitical meaning of the host.
4. **A venue’s continued use is not equivalent to public benefit.** It may serve
   private or elite users, be inaccessible by price, or require public subsidy.
5. **The host city is not one public.** Residents, migrants, vendors, workers,
   visitors, athletes, businesses and displaced households experience different
   events and different legacies.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Many venues remain in use; “white elephant” is not a universal outcome.
- A new transport line can improve connectivity while construction displaces
  residents or raises land costs near stations.
- Event procurement can create durable SMEs and skills, but temporary contracts
  can also leave debt, unpaid suppliers or no post-event work.
- A village can add housing while the allocation, price or tenure excludes the
  households displaced by construction.
- Security measures can protect attendees while restricting protest, informal
  trade or ordinary civic access.
- A host can gain soft-power visibility and still lose legitimacy if rights
  abuses, corruption or accountability failures become salient.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. IOC, UN-Habitat,
OECD and UN evidence support the legacy, procurement, displacement and rights
mechanisms, including positive counterexamples. Keep the integrated outcome as
**Tier 2** because public records rarely connect the same host neighbourhood to
pre-event housing and livelihood, event exposure, post-event access, maintenance
finance, public trust and geopolitical reputation.

## Next test

Select one recent Olympic/Paralympic host, one World Cup host corridor and one
smaller cultural or business mega-event. Track:

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | bid, event objective, legacy promise, public support and finance plan |
| Mechanism | land, procurement, security, housing, transport, cultural or labour pathway |
| Operation | named venue/neighbourhood/contract/worker/resident and dates |
| Material consequence | housing, income, debt, mobility, displacement, access, environment or maintenance |
| Meaning | pride, belonging, fairness, protest, representation, trust and attribution |
| Power | resident voice, remedy, contract accountability, post-event ownership and geopolitical response |

Follow the same named places for at least one pre-event and two post-event
periods. Include venues that became useful, venues that failed, residents who
stayed, those who moved, workers who gained and lost work, and groups whose
public-space access changed.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the legacy-planning, procurement, housing,
displacement and rights-risk mechanisms. It does not close named resident
wellbeing, worker remedy, post-event affordability, maintenance, cultural
belonging or durable geopolitical reputation. Those remain acquisition targets.
