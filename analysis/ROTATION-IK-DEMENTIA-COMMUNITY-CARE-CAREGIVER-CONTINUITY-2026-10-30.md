# Rotation IK — Dementia-inclusive community care, caregiver support and continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When dementia strategies, diagnosis, telemedicine, community care, respite and caregiver training are introduced, do people living with dementia and their families receive continuous, culturally safe and dignified support—or do systems stop at awareness, diagnosis, facility capacity or caregiver burden without practical relief and participation?

## Why this packet matters

Dementia care is a long-duration social and health system, not a single clinical encounter:

```text
memory/function concern
  -> recognition, diagnosis and communication
  -> person-centred plan and family/community support
  -> daily care, medication, safety and social participation
  -> respite, income and caregiver mental-health support
  -> continuity through progression, crisis and transitions
  -> autonomy, dignity, belonging and end-of-life choice
```

The chain is culturally sensitive: language, family roles, stigma, faith, gender, rural access, disability, housing and community identity shape whether support is acceptable and usable. Technology can reduce isolation or widen exclusion if it is not embedded in age-friendly, human support.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| IK1 | WHO’s 2025 community-care mapping framework calls for diverse accessible inclusive services for older people and people with disabilities, with professional roles and prevention of institutionalization. | System architecture and anti-institutionalization frame. | Person-level service receipt, cultural fit, continuity, caregiver relief or functioning. |
| IK2 | WHO recommends psychosocial interventions and psychoeducation/counselling for dementia carers and says respite care should be considered; it also recommends assessing/treating caregiver depression and anxiety. | Caregiver-support requirements. | Availability, uptake, affordability, respite hours, caregiver wellbeing or care recipient outcome. |
| IK3 | WHO’s dementia-carer data report says 70% of informal care is provided by women and the global action plan targeted 75% of countries providing carer support/training by 2025; only one in three GDO countries has carer-support guidelines. | Gendered burden and policy-coverage boundary. | Quality, actual training, financial relief, worker substitution or family functioning. |
| IK4 | WHO reports only 37 of 78 Observatory countries could report dementia diagnostic rates in 2024, with median diagnosis rates far lower in low- and middle-income countries than high-income countries. | Measurement and diagnosis gap. | Person-level diagnosis, disclosure, treatment, support, equity or quality of life. |
| IK5 | WHO Europe’s 2025 telemedicine review finds digital tools can reduce depression, anxiety, isolation and caregiver stress when embedded in age-friendly environments and strong community support. | Technology-plus-community mechanism. | Device access, digital literacy, clinical continuity, safety, cultural fit or causal patient outcome. |
| IK6 | World Bank reports over 1,600 people, many elderly, living more independently through new in-home care/support services in North Macedonia, with pathways for Roma communities. | Named community-care and inclusion outcome signal. | Dementia-specific diagnosis, caregiver hours, quality, affordability, continuity or autonomy measurement. |
| IK7 | WHO Europe launched an online informal-caregiver course in 2025, attended by more than 450 people, designed for adaptation to workshops, support groups and community initiatives. | Training infrastructure and adaptation route. | Completion, skill use, respite, caregiver health, care quality or reach beyond participants. |
| IK8 | WHO’s dementia framework states people living with dementia and carers should live with dignity, respect, autonomy and equality and remain engaged in family and community life. | Rights, participation and dignity standard. | A named community’s actual inclusion, safety, agency or end-of-life continuity. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Dementia care is often organized through families, especially women, while formal services vary by rurality, wealth, language and migration. Diagnosis can carry stigma or be unavailable; telemedicine can connect remote families but requires privacy, connectivity and trusted human support. Community care can protect belonging, but insufficient respite can convert family care into exhaustion, lost income and unsafe care.

## Counterexample and source boundary

A national strategy, diagnostic rate, online course, telemedicine pilot or in-home-care count is not proof of daily continuity. The 37/78 reporting boundary shows how measurement can be weaker than need; the caregiver recommendations have low or conditional evidence for some interventions; and a service can increase independence for older adults without closing dementia-specific caregiver relief. Public evidence closes architecture, caregiver requirements, gender burden, diagnostic gaps, technology mechanism, named community care and rights standards, but not a comparable person–caregiver chain through continuity, respite, functioning and dignity.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** A dementia plan, diagnosis, telemedicine tool, caregiver course, community-care service, respite recommendation or independence statistic is not by itself culturally safe continuous care, caregiver relief, autonomy, social participation or durable dignity.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **dementia-community-care-to-continuous-person-and-caregiver-wellbeing**. The strongest next acquisition is a named person/family/community chain linking concern, diagnosis, language, care plan, daily support, safety, medication, social participation, respite, caregiver mental health, cost, transition and person-defined autonomy.
