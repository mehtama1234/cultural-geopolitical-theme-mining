# Rotation FX — access to justice, legal aid and people-centred remedy

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question
**Research lane:** social, geographical, legal, cultural and governance systems

## Why this rotation exists

Justice is often measured by courts, case counts, laws, legal-aid budgets or
digital filing. People experience justice earlier and more broadly: recognizing
a problem, finding trustworthy advice, negotiating or mediating, reaching an
appropriate forum, being heard in a language they understand, obtaining a fair
decision and receiving a remedy they can use. Cost, distance, disability,
gender, migration, social identity and fear can prevent the formal system from
being usable even when rights exist on paper.

## Research question

When a state, court, legal-aid provider, community intermediary, NGO or digital
service expands justice access, under what conditions does it produce a named
person’s affordable, fair, understandable and timely resolution, protection and
repair rather than procedural contact without meaningful remedy?

## Conversion chain

```text
legal need / information / referral / legal aid / mediation / court / remedy
  -> person or household enters a justice pathway
  -> advice, representation, hearing, decision, enforcement or settlement
  -> protection, recovery, status, housing, income, safety or continuity outcome
  -> agency, trust, legitimacy, prevention or refusal
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD's 2025 people-centred justice work estimates that two-thirds of the
   world's population lack meaningful access to justice and that around 600
   million people in OECD countries cannot resolve everyday justice problems.
   The estimate is a global signal, not a case-level outcome measure.
2. OECD recommends starting from people's legal needs, capabilities and
   outcomes, using accessible entry points, plain language, guided or self-help
   tools, prevention and timely resolution. Court capacity alone is not the
   same as a usable justice pathway.
3. OECD's 2025 toolkit organizes people-centred justice around purpose and
   culture, service design, governance infrastructure, empowerment and
   evidence-based planning, and emphasizes affordable, effective and inclusive
   services that improve through user feedback.
4. UNDP's 2025 Uzbekistan legal-needs assessment found social-security, land/
   housing and financial problems dominant; many people first seek local
   mahalla help, while only 8.6% could afford a lawyer. This demonstrates the
   importance of community intermediaries and affordability, but is one national
   case rather than a global estimate.
5. UNDP's 2026 India justice assessment identifies gaps between legal-aid
   frameworks and on-the-ground delivery across six states, with differences by
   location, gender, caste, tribe, religion, disability and migration. Mixed
   methods expose pathways that court statistics alone miss.
6. A 2025 World Bank Kenya assessment reports uneven legal-aid coverage,
   low awareness and heavy reliance on civil-society and donor-supported
   providers. Availability of an institution therefore does not prove that a
   person knows about it, qualifies, reaches it or receives a resolved case.

## Bounded proposition

**A court, legal-aid statute, lawyer network, online filing system, mediation
program, community paralegal, justice hotline, rights campaign, case-clearance
target or favorable judgment is not by itself meaningful access to justice,
fair process, affordable protection, enforceable remedy, safety, housing,
livelihood continuity or institutional trust.** A legal right, referral,
registration, hearing, settlement or order does not establish that named people
understood the route, could participate without retaliation, received a usable
outcome or avoided recurrence.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

- **Justice begins before court:** local leaders, paralegals, women’s groups,
  disability organizations, unions and trusted intermediaries may be the first
  route into formal or informal resolution.
- **Place determines usability:** distance, transport, language, internet,
  documentation, opening hours and safety shape whether a service can be used.
- **Informal does not mean unjust:** customary or community resolution can be
  accessible, but may also reproduce gender, caste, age or minority power gaps;
  safeguards and user choice must be observed.
- **Legal needs are material:** land, housing, social protection, debt, family,
  work and violence disputes connect justice to daily security and continuity.
- **Representation is unequal:** a formally open process may still exclude
  people with disability, low literacy, migrant status, minority language or
  fear of authorities.
- **Remedy is relational:** trust depends on explanation, respect, speed,
  enforcement, non-recurrence and whether the person can safely return to life
  after the case.

## Counterexamples and cautions

1. More legal-aid applications can indicate improved awareness or worsening
   legal need; volume alone is ambiguous.
2. A case closure or settlement may reflect abandonment, pressure, compromise or
   non-enforcement rather than fair resolution.
3. Digital filing reduces travel for some people while excluding others through
   connectivity, identity, language or accessibility barriers.
4. Community mediation can be faster and culturally legitimate but unsafe for
   survivors of violence or minorities without independent safeguards.
5. A favorable judgment is not a material remedy if payment, housing, status or
   protection is not delivered.
6. National justice indicators hide differences between rural and urban places,
   courts, legal-aid providers and population groups.
7. Legal literacy campaigns can increase knowledge without changing affordability,
   institutional responsiveness or power to enforce rights.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote the Tier 1 distinction that access to justice is a people-centred
pathway from need to usable remedy, not institutional availability or case
throughput. Keep the following as Tier 2:
**legal-need-to-affordable-fair-remedy-and-household-security conversion**. The
next evidence must name the person/group, legal need, route, language and
barriers, service, decision, enforcement, material result, safety and recurrence.

## Next test

Build a six-month legal-needs ledger across one rural/urban corridor and two
subgroups. Follow social protection, land/housing, debt, family and violence
matters from first recognition through advice, referral, representation,
hearing/mediation, decision, enforcement and later stability. Record cost,
travel, language, disability, safety, community intermediary, abandonment and
user-reported fairness.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What is currently supported | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | legal-needs surveys, access gaps, legal-aid coverage and justice standards | local recognition, under-reporting and cultural meaning |
| Mechanism | information, referral, representation, mediation, court, enforcement and remedy | power, retaliation, affordability and intermediary trust |
| Operation | person, household, need, provider, forum, case and decision can be named | repeated pathway and enforcement response |
| Material consequence | status, housing, income, safety, protection, cost and continuity are relevant | named usable remedy, payment, stability and non-recurrence |
| Meaning | dignity, fairness, agency, belonging and institutional trust are central | participant attribution and contested legitimacy |
| Power | legal capability, representation, collective action, exit and enforcement alter leverage | durable equality, prevention, accountability and remedy |

## Source boundary

The packet closes discovery and framing for access to justice and legal aid.
Public sources support legal-needs prevalence, people-centred design,
community pathways, affordability and subgroup/geographic gaps. They do not by
themselves establish a named person’s fair hearing, enforceable remedy,
household recovery, safety or durable trust.
