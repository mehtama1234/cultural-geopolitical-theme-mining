# Rotation GJ — market power, practical choice and consumer agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** economic, social, geographical, digital and household systems

## Why this rotation exists

Competition is often represented by concentration ratios, merger decisions,
new entrants, lower prices, platform access or a consumer-protection rule. Those
signals matter, but a market can remain formally contestable while people face
lock-in, opaque ranking, switching costs, geographic gaps, unequal information,
data extraction, weak substitutes or no realistic remedy. Small firms may be
listed on a marketplace without having bargaining power or viable margins.

## Research question

When competition policy, market entry, interoperability, procurement design,
consumer protection or platform regulation changes a market, under what
conditions does it produce understandable, affordable, safe and switchable
choice for named households and small firms rather than a formal option set
without practical agency?

## Conversion chain

```text
market structure / rule / platform / merger review / entry / remedy
  -> people and firms can discover, compare, afford and switch among options
  -> suppliers compete on price, quality, privacy, access and innovation
  -> household welfare, small-firm viability and resilience improve
  -> users can contest terms, exit dependence and shape future market power
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD reports that competition has weakened on average in OECD countries,
   with higher concentration and markups; it also cautions that concentration
   is an indicator rather than a complete measure of competition and recommends
   narrower market definitions and geographic measurement.
2. OECD's 2025 business-dynamism analysis finds low and declining contestability
   among market leaders and warns that entrenched market power can undermine
   innovation and consumer choice. This supports a dynamic, not merely static,
   view of competition.
3. OECD's 2025 ten-year enforcement review shows that most notified mergers
   were unconditionally cleared, while authorities still face changing digital
   business models and complex theories of harm. Clearance is therefore not
   proof that every affected user will have practical alternatives.
4. UNCTAD identifies network effects, scale and data dependence in digital
   markets, and notes that dominant platforms can affect both consumers and
   small businesses. A platform listing or nominal access route does not show
   fair ranking, discoverability, bargaining power or viable margins.
5. OECD's AI-market work identifies switching costs, user lock-in, default
   positioning, bundling and exclusive distribution as mechanisms that can
   reduce choice even while products and services continue to be offered.
6. OECD's digital-market and consumer-policy work indicates that competition
   and consumer-protection tools address different failure modes: one may
   change market structure, while the other may provide more immediate relief
   for misleading or unfair practices. Neither alone establishes durable user
   agency, affordability or remedy for named groups.

## Bounded proposition

**A competition law, merger review, market-share measure, price reduction,
platform marketplace, procurement auction, interoperability rule, switching
tool or consumer-choice disclosure is not by itself practical choice,
affordability, quality, innovation, small-firm viability, household agency or
fair market power.** A lower price, additional supplier, market entry,
disclosure, complaint, settlement or divestiture does not establish that named
households or firms could understand options, switch without lock-in, obtain a
usable substitute, avoid discrimination or influence the terms of exchange.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Choice is lived through income, language, disability, trust, migration status,
care responsibilities, local infrastructure and social norms. A rural household
may face one physical provider even when a national market has many firms. A
small supplier may depend on a dominant marketplace whose ranking, fees and
data rules are opaque. A migrant, older person or disabled user may value
continuity and human support more than nominally cheaper digital options.
Market power therefore has a place-based and cultural dimension: substitution
must be usable, trusted, safe and compatible with local routines, not merely
counted in a national market definition.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Concentration can sometimes reflect efficiency or quality; it is not proof of
  abuse, and a low concentration ratio does not guarantee fair treatment.
- Entry can expand variety while creating precarious suppliers, surveillance or
  hidden fees; more apps or listings are not necessarily more agency.
- A remedy can improve prices or access for some users while leaving rural,
  low-income, disabled, language-minority or small-firm users behind.
- Switching can be legally available but economically impossible because of
  data loss, retraining, compatibility, contract, trust or migration costs.
- Competition and consumer-protection interventions can complement one another;
  the proposition is a measurement boundary, not an argument against either.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: market power is a conversion problem from formal
contestability to practical, affordable and contestable choice. Keep the named
market-power-to-practical-choice chain as **Tier 2** until user-level,
small-firm, geographic and longitudinal evidence closes the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | concentration, markups, mergers, entry, platform rules, complaints or prices | strong OECD, UNCTAD and policy evidence |
| Mechanism | network effects, switching costs, defaults, data, fees, information and geographic access | strong bounded mechanism evidence |
| Operation | named user or firm can compare, afford, switch, substitute and contest terms | partial; requires micro-level and place-specific observation |
| Material consequence | price, quality, privacy, resilience, income or household welfare changes | not closed for a general market-power claim |
| Meaning | fairness, dignity, trust, dependence and cultural fit | not closed; requires user-informed follow-up |
| Power | exit, bargaining leverage, supplier viability and durable market contestability | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one essential market in three contrasting places and follow named
households and small firms for a year. Record available suppliers, total cost,
quality, wait, language and accessibility, contract/data lock-in, failed
switches, complaints, refunds, supplier margins, ranking exposure and actual
exit. Compare national concentration with local substitutability and ask users
whether they understood options and could contest or leave without losing a
critical service.

## Source boundary

The public record closes the existence of competition risks and plausible
mechanisms involving concentration, declining contestability, digital scale,
data, network effects, switching costs and differentiated policy tools. It does
not justify a universal claim that a policy, entry event or remedy produced
practical choice, affordability, welfare, small-firm viability or durable user
power for named people in named places. Those later outcomes remain acquisition
targets.
