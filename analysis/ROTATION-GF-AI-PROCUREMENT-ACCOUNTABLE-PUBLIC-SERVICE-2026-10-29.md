# Rotation GF — AI procurement, accountable public service and resident remedy

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological, institutional, procurement, social and geographical systems

## Why this rotation exists

Public-sector AI is often evaluated at the moment of purchase: a contract is
awarded, a model is available, a dashboard is launched or an agency reports a
use case. Residents experience the system later, through a benefit decision,
inspection, health service, permit, emergency response, caseworker interaction
or public-information channel. Procurement determines data rights, testing,
vendor dependence, accessibility, security, auditability, contestation and exit;
these conditions shape whether an acquired capability becomes accountable
service or an opaque dependency.

## Research question

When a public body procures an AI product, service, model, cloud capability or
integrator, under what conditions does the acquisition produce a reliable,
accessible and contestable public service for named residents rather than
vendor lock-in, weak oversight, degraded performance, exclusion, privacy harm
or a contract-compliant system without public value?

## Conversion chain

```text
need / specification / tender / vendor / contract / model / data rights
  -> test, deploy, monitor, explain, maintain and govern the capability
  -> resident receives an accurate, accessible, timely and contestable service
  -> remedy, trust, equity, institutional learning or public value
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. GAO's 2026 review of 13 federal AI acquisitions across DOD, DHS, GSA and VA
   reports that federal agencies more than doubled AI use from 2023 to 2024 and
   used varied acquisition approaches through fiscal year 2025. Challenges
   include requirements, contract terms, data and intellectual-property
   protection, early testing, continuous evaluation, expertise, pricing and
   cost.
2. GAO reports that four agencies were not prepared to systematically collect
   lessons learned because their policies did not require it. A procurement
   portfolio therefore lacks organizational learning even when individual
   contracts exist.
3. OECD's 2025 public-procurement report finds AI and machine learning already
   used or under development in many procurement systems, while emphasizing
   connected processes, user-centred design, data and digital capability.
4. OECD's 2026 Digital Government Outlook identifies public procurement as one
   of seven enablers of government AI and warns that weak procurement support
   creates vendor lock-in, unclear accountability and limited transparency into
   system behaviour and performance. Only 21 of 36 OECD countries reported
   central support for procuring AI goods and services.
5. OECD notes that procurement is approximately 13% of GDP in OECD countries
   and that AI can improve efficiency and operational decisions while creating
   requirements for transparency, accountability and third-party assessment.
   Procurement scale makes contract design a public-governance issue, not only
   an IT issue.
6. GAO's 2025 review identifies 94 government-wide AI requirements and records
   requirements for use-case inventories, strategies, oversight and AI-contract
   guidance. Requirements and advisory structures are governance inputs; they
   do not prove that residents received better or more equitable services.

## Bounded proposition

**An AI tender, contract, framework agreement, model card, impact assessment,
vendor assurance, use-case inventory, pilot, dashboard or compliance review is
not by itself reliable, accessible, fair or accountable public service.** A
procurement award, deployment, uptime measure or contract deliverable does not
establish that named residents received accurate and understandable service,
could challenge an error, avoided privacy or exclusion harms, obtained remedy,
or that the institution retained the authority and capability to change or exit
the system.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Procurement decisions are distant from the people who experience them. A
national framework can hide local language, disability, connectivity, cultural
practice and service-delivery differences. Vendors may optimize for a buyer's
technical specification while residents need explanation, human support,
translation, offline access or a safe appeal route. Public agencies may also
lose practical sovereignty when data, models, skills and maintenance sit with a
vendor. Local service workers and affected communities should have a role in
requirements, testing, monitoring and correction; otherwise “user-centred”
procurement can remain an institutional claim rather than lived public value.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A vendor can provide useful infrastructure and public services can improve;
  the packet does not treat procurement as inherently harmful.
- Contract clauses may require testing while agencies lack expertise, time,
  data access or authority to verify compliance; paper safeguards are not
  operating safeguards.
- Open-source or self-hosted systems can improve exit options but shift cost,
  security, skills and maintenance burdens to the public agency.
- A successful pilot may fail at scale because data drift, usage patterns,
  staffing, procurement terms or local conditions change.
- High uptime or low unit cost can coexist with wrong decisions, inaccessible
  interfaces, discriminatory effects or no meaningful remedy.
- Public consultation and vendor transparency can inform design without giving
  affected residents power over deployment, correction or exit.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: AI procurement is a conversion problem linking
contract architecture to service reliability, resident rights, agency capability
and accountability. Keep the named acquisition-to-public-value-and-remedy chain
as **Tier 2** until deployment, resident outcomes, procurement learning and exit
evidence are observable.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | tender, contract, model, use case, policy or acquisition spend | strong GAO and OECD evidence |
| Mechanism | requirements, data/IP rights, testing, oversight, cost, lock-in, accessibility and exit | strong bounded procurement evidence |
| Operation | named agency deploys, monitors, explains, maintains and changes system | partial; acquisition cases and guidance exist |
| Material consequence | resident service, decision, cost, delay, error, privacy or remedy | not closed for a general procurement claim |
| Meaning | fairness, dignity, trust, legitimacy and perceived publicness | not closed; needs affected-user follow-up |
| Power | agency capability, contestation, vendor accountability, learning and exit | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one public AI acquisition and build a contract-to-resident ledger: need,
specification, vendor, data rights, model version, test results, accessibility,
language, incident, override, complaint, appeal, service outcome, cost,
maintenance, audit finding and exit option. Compare procurement promises with
real outcomes for different places and user groups, and record whether lessons
changed the contract, model or service.

## Source boundary

The public record closes AI-acquisition growth, procurement mechanisms,
contractual challenges, oversight requirements and vendor-dependence risks. It
does not justify a universal claim that responsible procurement produces
reliable, equitable, accessible public service or resident remedy. Those later
outcomes remain acquisition targets.
