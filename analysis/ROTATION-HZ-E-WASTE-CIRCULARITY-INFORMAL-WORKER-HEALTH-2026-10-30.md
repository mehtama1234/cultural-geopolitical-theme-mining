# Rotation HZ — E-waste, circularity and informal-worker health

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When governments and firms promote e-waste collection, recycling, extended producer responsibility and circular electronics, do they create safe traceable material recovery and decent livelihoods—or shift toxic exposure, livelihood loss and environmental costs to informal workers and nearby communities?

## Why this packet matters

E-waste is simultaneously a technology, health, labor, climate, trade and geopolitical system. The formal chain is often counted through collection, recycling and recovered material. The lived chain is:

```text
device design / sale / obsolescence
  -> discard, repair, resale or collection
  -> sorting and dismantling
  -> material recovery / export / disposal
  -> worker exposure, income and safety
  -> child, household, soil, water and food effects
  -> traceability, remedy and circular value
```

The key audit is not whether e-waste enters a “formal” channel. It is whether the channel is safe, economically viable, inclusive of existing informal workers, and accountable across borders and supply chains.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| HZ1 | WHO reports 62 million tonnes of e-waste generated in 2022, with only 22.3% documented as properly collected and recycled. | Global scale and formal-documentation gap. | Actual informal flow, material recovery, exposure or health outcome. |
| HZ2 | WHO identifies lead, cadmium and other toxic materials released through unsound recycling and says children and pregnant women are especially vulnerable. | Hazard mechanism and vulnerable groups. | Named exposure dose, worker protection, household pathway or clinical outcome. |
| HZ3 | WHO notes that informal e-waste recycling is an important income source, while children may be exposed at homes or parents’ workplaces and have less agency over exposure. | Livelihood and household-exposure trade-off. | Whether formalization protects income, changes child exposure or provides remedy. |
| HZ4 | UNEP reports that high-income countries sent about 3.3 billion kg of e-waste and used electronics to middle- and low-income countries through uncontrolled transboundary movements in 2022. | Geopolitical and trade-flow burden. | Destination-level enforcement, worker conditions, material recovery or responsibility allocation. |
| HZ5 | UNEP’s Mexico circularity project (September 2025–March 2026) addresses environmental, economic and social challenges of e-waste, informal recycling and limited infrastructure in a major electronics manufacturing, trade and consumption hub. | Named national circularity transition and infrastructure challenge. | Worker inclusion, collection performance, safety, income, traceability or local health. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Repair, resale, reuse and dismantling have local economic meanings and skills. Informality may reflect exclusion from licenses, finance, land, procurement and formal employment—not merely non-compliance. Gendered sorting and household work can make women and children invisible in official records. Cross-border flows distribute value and hazard unevenly, while critical-mineral demand and producer-responsibility rules can intensify competition for recovered materials.

## Counterexample and source boundary

Formal collection or circularity branding is not automatically safer. The India E-SAFE case identifies hazardous informal conditions even as the sector becomes more traceable and strategically important. The global 22.3% documentation figure is a documentation measure, not proof that the remaining material was all recycled, all informal, or all hazardous. Public evidence closes scale, hazard, employment, policy and named intervention stages, but not a comparable worker/community chain through exposure, income, material recovery, remedy and health.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** E-waste volume, collection, recycling, extended producer responsibility, circularity policy, recovered material or formalization is not by itself safe work, decent income, child protection, environmental recovery, traceability or accountable cross-border value.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **e-waste-circularity-to-safe-inclusive-material-recovery**. The strongest next acquisition is a named site or worker group linking device flow, ownership and contract, sorting/dismantling, exposure controls, wages, gender/age, material recovery, community pathway, health monitoring, formalization, complaint and remedy.
