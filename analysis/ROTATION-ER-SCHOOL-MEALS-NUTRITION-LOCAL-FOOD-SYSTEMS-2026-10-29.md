# Rotation ER — School meals, child nutrition and local food-system resilience

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** school nutrition / education / food security / public procurement / gender / local agriculture / culture / social protection

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes food-security benefit access, food environments, informal
markets, school attendance, child welfare, Indigenous food systems, G2P payments
and public procurement. This rotation isolates school meals as a daily
institutional conversion system: whether a meal reaches the child, is nutritious
and culturally acceptable, supports learning and attendance, protects household
budgets, and creates fair demand for local producers and women’s work.

A school-meal policy can exist while meals are missed, nutritionally weak,
unsafe, unaffordable to the programme, culturally rejected, inaccessible to
disabled children, or interrupted by school closure and food-price shocks. Local
procurement can create farmer demand while small producers cannot bid, meet
volume, access bank accounts, or receive payment. The unit is the named child,
school, meal, household, producer and procurement route—not the programme
budget or number of beneficiaries.

## Research question

When a government, school system, WFP, donor, municipality or public buyer
introduces universal or targeted school meals, nutrition standards, homegrown
procurement, school kitchens, cash or food transfers, food education or local
farmer contracts, under what conditions does it produce safe nutritious and
culturally legitimate meals, attendance, learning, health and household
security while generating fair local livelihoods without excluding smallholders,
women, refugees, remote schools or children with different needs?

## Conversion chain

```text
child hunger / food insecurity / school access / local production / public budget
  -> meal, nutrition, procurement, kitchen, transport and school-food policy
  -> school kitchen, vendor, farm, market, classroom and household encounter
  -> meal delivered, accepted, safe, nutritious and repeated
  -> diet, attendance, concentration, learning, health and household protection
  -> farmer income, women’s work, local resilience, dignity and trust
```

The unit must be a named child, school, meal day, household, producer,
cooperative, kitchen, contract or food item with dates, menu, portion,
attendance, nutrition, safety, price, procurement, payment and outcome fields.
Coverage, meals budgeted, children enrolled, contracts awarded or food purchased
is an intermediate signal—not proof of consumption, nutrition, learning, local
income or resilience.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. School meals are a major safety-net and food-system institution

World Bank evidence describes school feeding as reaching approximately 466
million children and attracting about $84 billion in annual investment. It can
support education and social protection while creating predictable food demand
for local economies. The same evidence shows effects vary by context and model,
so the unit must follow the child and producer rather than assume a universal
multiplier.

Source: [World Bank evidence on homegrown school feeding](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/a362c04547b7665a4a730f51499a4f5f-0050052026/original/From-Projects-to-Learning-Systems-DIF-Annual-Report-2026.pdf).

### 2. Meals can improve dietary diversity and attendance, but effects need named follow-up

The World Bank’s Jordan evidence reports a randomized evaluation across 473
schools. Healthy meals increased dietary diversity, reduced spending on sugary
or fatty snacks, increased physical activity and produced about one additional
school-attendance day per year. The programme also created local kitchen and
producer employment pathways and is being expanded into refugee settings.

This is a valuable positive case, but it does not establish that every meal was
consumed, that learning gains persisted, or that all groups benefited equally.
The evaluation must remain bounded to its population, period and outcomes.

Source: [World Bank Jordan school-feeding evidence](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/feeding-the-future-workforce--how-evidence-is-shaping-jordan-s-s).

### 3. Homegrown procurement can connect children’s nutrition to producer livelihoods

World Bank evidence from Burundi, The Gambia, Madagascar, Malawi and Jordan
reports improvements in food security or dietary diversity in several settings,
and describes farmer-cooperative revenue and women’s local-kitchen employment
effects. In Burundi, the pilot increased the number of days students received
food and reduced cost relative to an earlier procurement model.

The local-economy conversion is not automatic. The same evidence notes that
only a minority of Malawi farmers had submitted school bids because of stock,
bank-account, tender-information and administrative barriers.

Source: [World Bank homegrown school feeding evidence](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/a362c04547b7665a4a730f51499a4f5f-0050052026/original/From-Projects-to-Learning-Systems-DIF-Annual-Report-2026.pdf).

### 4. Reach and national policy do not prove daily meal quality

UNICEF’s 2025 foundational-learning tracker reports that 45% of countries had
nationwide school-nutrition services, up from about one-third in 2023 and 2024.
This is a useful policy signal, but it does not establish meal frequency,
nutrient adequacy, safety, cultural acceptance, school-level access or actual
consumption.

UNICEF school-nutrition assessments also show that schools can have meal or
snack policies without consistently providing food, and that nutrition
environments require work across families, vendors, teachers and health staff.

Sources: [UNICEF foundational-learning tracker 2025](https://www.unicef.org/media/175231/file/Foundational-learning-action-tracker-2025-Global-report.pdf)
and [UNICEF Indonesia school nutrition environment toolkit](https://www.unicef.org/indonesia/media/24206/file/nutrition-environment-assessment-toolkit-for-schools-Indonesia.pdf).

### 5. Procurement design affects who can participate

WFP’s 2025 evidence summary covers local and regional procurement evaluations in
Cambodia, Kenya, Laos, Nepal and Rwanda. It underscores that procurement is a
mechanism requiring local market, producer, contract, payment and implementation
analysis rather than a label.

World Bank evidence from Guatemala similarly identifies production capacity,
administrative procedures, incentives and information as barriers for family
farmers, especially women. Local sourcing can therefore strengthen food
systems—or concentrate public demand in larger suppliers.

Sources: [WFP local and regional procurement evaluation summary](https://www.wfp.org/publications/summary-evaluation-evidence-usda-mcgovern-dole-school-meals-programs-local-and)
and [World Bank/Guatemala family-farming procurement evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099647307212221205/pdf/IDU0dbdaa9b10af16042ab08407017cb31f384ba.pdf).

### 6. Meals are cultural, gendered and crisis-sensitive

Menus, ingredients, religious restrictions, taste, disability, meal timing,
stigma, gender and local food knowledge affect whether children eat and whether
families trust the programme. Food prices, drought, conflict, school closure,
refugee status, transport and kitchen capacity can interrupt delivery or shift
the burden to households.

School meals can be a social protection floor and a platform for local dignity,
but they can also be experienced as charity, surveillance or cultural erasure
if children and communities have no voice in design.

## Bounded proposition 149

**A school-meal programme, nutrition standard, food basket, homegrown
procurement scheme, school kitchen, public food contract, cash-plus meal,
nutrition lesson or local-farmer partnership is not by itself child nutrition,
attendance, learning, health, household food security, cultural dignity, gender
equality, farmer income, women’s employment or local resilience.** Budgets,
children enrolled, meals planned, meals served, contracts awarded, food
purchased, nutrition standards, school coverage or producer participation do not
establish that named children consumed a safe and acceptable nutritious meal on
the needed days, that households were protected, that learning or health
improved, or that small producers and workers received fair and durable value.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **The meal is a daily social contract.** Reliability, dignity, taste and
   fairness shape whether children and families trust the institution.
2. **Food is culturally legible.** Local ingredients can improve acceptance and
   livelihoods, but “local” is not automatically nutritious, safe, affordable or
   inclusive of minority diets.
3. **The school is a market-maker.** Public procurement redistributes demand,
   bargaining power, risk and information across farmers, kitchens, vendors and
   intermediaries.
4. **Women’s work is often hidden.** Cooking, care, farming, procurement and
   household food decisions can increase agency or intensify unpaid and
   underpaid labour.
5. **Crisis reveals the route.** Drought, conflict, inflation, displacement and
   closure test whether meals remain available or the burden shifts back home.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A meal policy can exist while schools lack kitchens, water, storage, staff or
  reliable delivery, producing irregular or unsafe meals.
- A nutritious menu can be rejected because it conflicts with local taste,
  religion, disability needs, stigma or meal timing.
- Local procurement can increase farmer revenue while excluding women, small
  farms or remote producers who cannot meet tender, volume, bank or transport
  requirements.
- Attendance gains can reflect a meal incentive without durable learning or
  improved diet outside school.
- A universal programme can miss children who are absent, displaced, disabled,
  in non-formal schools or unable to eat the offered food.
- Centralized procurement can stabilize quality and cost in one setting while
  weakening local markets and cultural control in another.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WFP, World Bank
and UNICEF evidence supports the nutrition, attendance, procurement, gender,
culture, crisis and local-economy mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same child, household,
school, producer and contract through meal consumption, health, learning,
income, resilience and remedy.

## Next test

Follow named children across at least two terms, one school kitchen or vendor,
one household and one local producer/cooperative. Compare a local-procurement
school with a centralized-procurement school and include a disruption such as
price shock, drought, school closure or supplier failure. Record meal receipt,
consumption, nutrient and cultural fit, attendance, learning, household food

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the nutrition, attendance, procurement, gender,
cultural, crisis and local-economy mechanisms. It does not close named meal
consumption, safety, diet, learning, health, household protection, producer
income, women’s work, continuity, exclusion or remedy outcomes. Those remain
acquisition targets.
