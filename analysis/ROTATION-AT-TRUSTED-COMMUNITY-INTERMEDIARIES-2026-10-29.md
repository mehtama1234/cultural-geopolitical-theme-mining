# Rotation AT — Trusted community intermediaries

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** social infrastructure / health / culture / language / public-service access  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 service-completion question

## Research question

When governments and health systems use community health workers, promotores,
faith leaders, clinicians, local organizations or other trusted intermediaries,
what converts formal information and service capacity into completed care or
public-service access—and what happens when the intermediary is underpaid,
overloaded, excluded from decision-making or treated as a temporary channel?

## Conversion chain

```text
formal service, risk or benefit
  -> trusted local intermediary
  -> culturally and linguistically legible interpretation
  -> navigation, accompaniment or referral
  -> completed appointment, benefit, prevention or protective action
  -> continuity, health, agency and reciprocal trust
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| CDC 2025 national survey of CHW understanding | CHWs are defined as trusted community members or people with unusually close understanding; they connect communities to health and social services and have recognized intervention roles. | role, trust mechanism and service-connection concept | named-person service completion, continuity and outcome |
| CDC risk-communication guidance | The messenger affects trust and uptake; agencies are advised to work with trusted partners and equip them to communicate in their own words. | communication design and trust mechanism | whether information becomes feasible action and sustained protection |
| GAO-25-107002 public-health workforce | Jurisdictions rely on public-health staff including CHWs; workforce gaps, recruitment and retention challenges constrain local capacity. | workforce role and capacity problem | stable coverage, caseload, referral completion and community outcome |
| GAO health-care value forum | Participants linked trust and connection with more equitable access and identified navigators, social workers and CHWs as potential bridges across services. | expert mechanism and navigator concept | comparative effect on cost, access, care completion and equity |
| HRSA 2025 workforce report | National workforce data can identify CHW-like occupations and demographic composition, but classification and employment setting limit direct interpretation. | workforce measurement boundary | local supply, compensation, language match and service continuity |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** programs increasingly name trusted messengers, CHWs and navigators
  as part of public-health and service-delivery architecture.
- **Mechanism:** trust, language, social identity, local knowledge, credibility,
  accompaniment and reduced navigation burden can bridge formal systems to
  people who would otherwise disengage or be missed.
- **Operation:** an intermediary contacts, interprets, refers, accompanies or
  follows up with a person and coordinates with the receiving institution.
- **Material consequence:** the person completes a visit, receives a benefit,
  adopts a protective action, manages a condition or remains connected to care.
- **Meaning:** an intermediary can make an institution feel locally present and
  respectful. But using community trust without sharing authority, pay,
  protection or feedback can turn cultural labor into extraction.
- **Power:** institutions control funding, referral rules, data and eligibility;
  intermediaries carry relational labor and reputational risk. Public sources
  rarely show the full burden or the person-level endpoint.

## Bounded finding

> **Trusted intermediaries are conversion infrastructure, not merely outreach.**
> A CHW program, community partnership, navigator role or communication plan
> establishes a bridge, but does not prove that a named person completed care,
> obtained a benefit, remained connected, or experienced greater agency.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Trust is situated. A messenger’s credibility depends on language, history,
identity, place, institutional memory and whether the person can help with the
actual next step. In migrant, Tribal, rural, low-income and disability contexts,
the intermediary may be the only actor who can translate not just words but
eligibility, transport, paperwork, stigma, family obligations and institutional
behavior.

The atlas should therefore track two relationships: the intermediary-to-resident
relationship and the intermediary-to-institution relationship. A program can
strengthen the first while weakening the second through unstable funding,
excessive caseloads, data extraction or refusal to act on community feedback.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A trusted messenger can improve reach without changing eligibility, supply,
  affordability or institutional responsiveness.
- Institutional trust is not uniform within a community; no intermediary speaks
  for an entire population.
- Workforce counts do not measure trust, language fit, caseload, compensation or
  completed referrals.
- Intermediary involvement can be useful without producing a measurable health
  outcome in the available observation window.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: trusted intermediaries are an observable
conversion mechanism, but program presence is not service completion. Keep the
person-level outcome and reciprocal-governance claim in Tier 2.

## Next test

Follow one CHW or navigator team across a defined cohort and service route.
Record contact, language/cultural match, referral, accompaniment, appointment or
benefit completion, failed handoff, time/caseload, compensation, institutional
response and participant-reported agency. Include a case where the intermediary
relationship worked and one where the institutional bottleneck remained.
