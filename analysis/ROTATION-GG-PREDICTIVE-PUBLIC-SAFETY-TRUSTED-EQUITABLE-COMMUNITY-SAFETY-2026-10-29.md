# Rotation GG — predictive public safety, trust and equitable community safety

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological, social, geographical, civic and human-rights systems

## Why this rotation exists

Predictive policing, crime-information centres, facial recognition, license
plate readers, drones, cameras, gunshot detection and analytic dashboards are
often presented as ways to anticipate harm and deploy scarce public-safety
resources. The lived outcome is not a prediction or patrol count. It includes
whether violence or harm falls, whether people feel safe, whether enforcement
burden is distributed fairly, whether residents can contest errors and whether
police-community relationships become more or less legitimate. Data generated by
past enforcement can reproduce the geography of past surveillance and create a
feedback loop.

## Research question

When public-safety agencies use predictive, biometric, sensor or analytic tools,
under what conditions do they produce safer, more equitable and trusted
communities rather than over-policing, privacy loss, discriminatory profiling,
false suspicion, displacement of harm or security capacity without legitimacy?

## Conversion chain

```text
incident / data / prediction / sensor / dashboard / patrol / investigation
  -> agency acts in a named place with oversight and community voice
  -> violence, victimization, response, procedural treatment or service outcome
  -> perceived and observed safety, equal protection, trust or remedy
  -> cooperation, accountability, legitimacy or refusal
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. A 2025 UN human-rights report warns that predictive policing and biometric
   data collection can embed race, gender and socioeconomic bias, reinforce
   over-policing and complicate fair-trial and due-process rights. It recommends
   human-rights due diligence and says systems that cannot comply with rights
   standards should not be used unless compliance can be demonstrated.
2. GAO reports that DHS law-enforcement agencies use more than 20 detection,
   observation and monitoring technologies, including facial recognition,
   license-plate readers and AI-enabled analytics. GAO found gaps in privacy
   protections and bias assessment across technologies, not only systems
   explicitly labelled AI.
3. GAO's 2025 smart-cities assessment finds that cities use cameras, Bluetooth
   sensors and gunshot detectors for public services and law enforcement, but
   costs can be high, benefits unclear and collected data can raise privacy and
   civil-liberties concerns.
4. A 2025 NIJ/OJP study of Baltimore intelligence centres found mixed effects;
   centres were generally associated with increases in recorded crime in the
   studied districts, potentially reflecting improved detection and recording
   rather than more underlying crime. Recorded incidents cannot be treated as a
   simple safety outcome.
5. An OJP evaluation of a Michigan State Police internal dashboard found no
   effect on traffic-stop racial disparities. A dashboard that exposes disparity
   metrics is not the same as a mechanism that changes behaviour.
6. OJP research on procedural justice and civilian oversight provides a way to
   evaluate dignity, participation, neutrality and trustworthy motives in actual
   police-citizen interactions. This is a service-quality and legitimacy lens,
   not proof that predictive technology improves safety.

## Bounded proposition

**A predictive-policing model, crime dashboard, camera, biometric system,
license-plate reader, gunshot detector, patrol deployment, intelligence centre,
bias audit or transparency report is not by itself lower victimization, equal
protection, safe mobility, procedural justice, community trust or durable
legitimacy.** A risk score, alert, stop, arrest, recorded incident or published
policy does not establish that named residents were safer, treated fairly,
could challenge an error, avoided surveillance or gained meaningful influence
over public-safety decisions.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Safety is experienced differently across neighbourhoods, identities and times.
Residents may value rapid response while fearing surveillance, immigration
consequences, racial profiling, gendered violence or criminalization of public
space. A place with more recorded incidents may have better reporting or more
police attention, not more underlying harm. Community voice must include people
who are over-policed, under-protected, disabled, migrant, unhoused, young,
minority-language or otherwise unlikely to appear in administrative data.
Trust requires explanation, respectful interaction, correction and accountability
after harm—not only technical accuracy or crime statistics.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Sensors and analytics can improve response, evidence and resource allocation;
  the proposition is not that all public-safety technology fails.
- More recorded crime can indicate improved detection, reporting or enforcement
  rather than an increase in victimization.
- Predictive accuracy does not establish causal prevention, fairness or equal
  protection; deployment itself can change the data-generating process.
- A bias audit can identify disparity while agencies lack authority, incentives
  or community governance to change practice.
- More patrol can reduce harm in one location while displacing it elsewhere or
  increasing fear and contact burden for another group.
- Community consultation can improve legitimacy without giving residents power
  over data retention, deployment, correction, complaint or exit.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: predictive public safety is a high-consequence
conversion problem requiring place-specific outcomes, rights safeguards,
procedural justice and community oversight. Keep the named prediction-to-safe,
equitable-and-trusted-community chain as **Tier 2** until longitudinal and
disaggregated evidence closes the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | model, sensor, alert, dashboard, patrol, recorded incident or policy | strong UN, GAO and OJP evidence |
| Mechanism | data provenance, feedback loop, bias, privacy, geography, oversight and community voice | strong bounded risk evidence |
| Operation | named agency deploys tool and acts in a place with review and correction | partial; multiple technology cases exist |
| Material consequence | victimization, response, stops, arrests, mobility, privacy or service result | not closed for a general predictive-safety claim |
| Meaning | dignity, fairness, fear, trust, legitimacy and perceived protection | not closed; requires community-informed measures |
| Power | resident governance, accountability, cooperation, correction and exit | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one place and one tool. Trace data provenance, alert, officer action,
resident contact, complaint, correction and later safety outcome. Compare
victimization and response—not only recorded incidents—by neighbourhood, race,
gender, age, disability, migration status and time. Pair administrative data
with confidential resident surveys, civilian oversight and procedural-justice
observation; record displacement, false positives and the ability to opt out or
seek remedy.

## Source boundary

The public record closes predictive-safety risk, surveillance, bias, privacy,
recording, oversight and procedural-justice mechanisms. It does not justify a
universal claim that predictive public-safety tools reduce harm, improve equal
protection or create durable community trust and legitimacy. Those later
outcomes remain acquisition targets.
