# Cultural meaning, trust, and political action: source-led subtheme map v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-25  
**Scope:** 438 current registry records carry the
`trust_identity_meaning` label and 285 carry
`political_judgment_action`. This map identifies recurring, source-specific
structures inside those records. It is not evidence that “culture” is one
attitude, that every social difference is political conflict, or that judgment
automatically becomes action.

## What the source material supports

The strongest recurring pattern is not a single rise or decline in trust. It
is the separation of:

> institutional evaluation, procedural fairness, family and social belonging,
> cultural distinction, and the practical capacity to participate.

Each has a different unit and route to consequence.

## 1. Institutional confidence is uneven, not a single national verdict

The [Gallup confidence record](records/us-gallup-institutional-confidence-party-race-2025.json)
found only three of 18 institutions at 50% or higher confidence in June 2025:
small business (70%), the military (62%), and science (61%); Congress was at
10%. Across the nine institutions tracked consistently since 1979, the
average confidence rating was 28%, with 37% for Republicans, 26% for
Democrats, and 25% for independents. Confidence in police was 52% among White
adults and 24% among Black adults.

These are descriptive survey ratings, not performance audits or direct
measures of withdrawal, service use, legitimacy, or political behavior.

The [2026 Gallup institutional-confidence record](records/us-gallup-institutional-confidence-2026.json)
provides a current but differently constructed surface: the arithmetic average
across its 14 tracked institutions was 27%, one point above its 2023 record
low, and had remained below 30% for five consecutive years. Its institution
contrast is more informative than that aggregate: 67% expressed substantial
confidence in small business versus 20% in large technology companies. The
reported average partisan gap was 13 points, with a 70-point Republican-over-
Democratic contrast for the presidency. The 14-institution 2026 average must
not be pooled with the 18-institution or nine-consistent-institution 2025
figures above; each is a survey summary, not a respondent-level legitimacy or
behavior measure.

**Supported subtheme:** people differentiate institutions, and social position
and political affiliation condition that differentiation.  
**Not established:** why any respondent holds a view, whether an institution
performed differently, whether high use coexists with low confidence, or
whether low confidence changes later action.

## 2. Procedural fairness can alter institutional action without proving legitimacy

The [property-tax fairness record](records/us-nber-perceived-tax-fairness-appeals-2024.json)
uses a local U.S. field experiment that disclosed average peer tax rates.
Higher perceived peer contribution increased reported fairness and reduced the
likelihood of a tax appeal. The authors estimate $0.43 in additional
willingness to contribute for each additional dollar paid by the average
household.

This is a rare direct bridge from comparative information to an administrative
choice. It remains setting-specific: lower appeal use does not establish
payment, compliance, public-service trust, durable legitimacy, or household
welfare.

**Supported subtheme:** fairness is not only an abstract value; information
about comparative treatment can shape a contestation decision.  
**Not established:** whether less contestation represented acceptance,
resignation, or an informed judgment about a wider tax system.

## 3. Belonging and social capacity extend beyond the household roster

The [Black family-networks record](records/us-pew-black-family-networks-support-2025-2026.json)
shows reported closeness across legal, biological, and chosen ties among 4,271
Black adults surveyed in 2025. Conditional on having each relationship, 73%
reported closeness to a nonrelative considered family, 48% to a grandparent
(versus 33% among non-Black adults), and 42% to a cousin (versus 20%).

The source measures reported closeness and family definition, not delivered
care, financial transfers, burden, co-residence, or a causal identity effect.

**Supported subtheme:** social infrastructure includes family ties that
standard household or legal-relationship measures can miss.  
**Not established:** who provides support under strain, whether those ties
reduce hardship, or whether they distribute care obligations unequally.

## 4. Cultural distinction is multidimensional, with convergence and divergence at once

The [cultural-distance record](records/us-nber-cultural-distance-media-consumption-time-attitudes-1965-2020.json)
uses several U.S. datasets to ask how accurately group membership can be
inferred from media, consumer behavior, time use, attitudes, and names. It
finds widening ideological differences in social attitudes, some widening
income differences, convergence between White and non-White groups on some
attitudes alongside divergence in consumer behavior, and many dimensions that
remain broadly stable. Gender time-use differences narrowed through 1995 and
then stabilized.

Classification-based cultural distance is not hostility, isolation, causal
polarization, or a measure of whether groups cooperate.

**Supported subtheme:** cultural change cannot be summarized by a one-way
“polarization” story; its direction depends on the group and the surface being
measured.  
**Not established:** the source of the changes, reduced contact, political
action, or a welfare effect.

## 5. Political judgment and public participation do not form one ladder

The [Pew democracy record](records/us-pew-democracy-dissatisfaction-reform-2026.json)
reports that 69% of U.S. adults were dissatisfied with how democracy worked in
March 2026, while 68% said the country used to be a good democratic example
but had not been in recent years. Dissatisfaction differed sharply by party:
86% among Democrats/Democratic leaners and 51% among Republicans/Republican
leaners. Those judgments do not show a common reform agenda, turnout, protest,
or rejection of democracy.

The [public-life engagement record](records/us-pew-public-life-engagement-groups-2025.json)
adds a capacity distinction. In its four descriptive engagement groups, 58% of
Mobilizers but 9% of Outsiders were highly interested in politics; federal
government trust remained low across all four groups (17%–22%), while national
news trust varied more. Cluster membership is not a fixed identity or causal
exposure.

The [cultural-signaling record](records/us-nber-multidimensional-signaling-cultural-politics-2026.json)
is a mechanism model—not current U.S. evidence—showing why cultural signals
could become prominent when voters cannot observe all policy preferences.

**Supported subtheme:** dissatisfaction, attention, trust, civic capacity, and
cultural signaling are separate political surfaces.  
**Not established:** that cultural rhetoric displaced economic concerns, that
participation causes trust, or that dissatisfaction produces a particular vote
or collective action.

## 5a. Demand for reform can coexist with low confidence that reform is possible

The [reform demand and feasibility record](records/us-pew-reform-demand-feasibility-2026.json)
separates a reform judgment from perceived capacity to achieve it. In the Pew
survey summarized by the 2026 analysis, 77% of adults said the political
system needed major changes or complete reform, while 27% were confident that
major political change could happen; around half were categorized as needing
reform while lacking that confidence. Separate 2023–24 surveys found broad
support for several institutional changes, including congressional term limits
(87%), age limits for federal elected officials (79%), campaign-spending limits
(72%), and a national-popular-vote presidency (63%).

**Supported subtheme:** dissatisfaction, preferred institutional direction,
and belief that coordinated political change is achievable are distinct
surfaces. A reform preference can coexist with low expected efficacy without
demonstrating apathy or rejection of democratic institutions.  
**Not established:** a common reform package, policy knowledge, intensity,
coordination, turnout, protest, constitutional change, institutional
performance, or a causal effect of political conflict or distrust. The items
come from separate descriptive surveys with different wording and dates.

## 5b. Party coalitions contain distinct cultural and geopolitical value packages

The [Pew political-typology record](records/us-pew-political-typology-values-coalitions-2026.json)
classifies 10,357 adults into nine value-based groups using 30 questions about
government, economics, immigration, social values, national identity, and
foreign policy. The two highly ideological right-anchor groups account for 21%
of adults and the two highly ideological left-anchor groups for 17%; the
Unconventional Right accounts for 23%, the Order and Opportunity coalition for
18%, and the Tuned-Out Middle for 9%.

The within-coalition contrasts matter. Support for making abortion illegal in
most or all cases ranges from 83% among Faith and Flag Conservatives to 43%
among the Unconventional Right. Foreign-policy preferences also cross-cut:
71% of adults say it is at least somewhat important for the U.S. to be the
world's only military superpower, while 62% say the U.S. should consider
allies' interests even when compromise is required.

**Supported subtheme:** party labels compress multiple cultural, national-
identity, and geopolitical value packages; coalition boundaries are not
identical to party boundaries.  
**Not established:** that typology membership is stable, causes voting or
policy behavior, predicts coalition durability, or translates stated power and
alliance preferences into foreign-policy action. The groups are cluster-derived
descriptive categories from one survey period.

## 6. Material pressure can produce withdrawal, targeted action, or no broad political shift

The [CIRCLE youth-financial-strain record](records/us-circle-youth-financial-strain-political-engagement-2026.json)
finds 60% of surveyed 18–29-year-olds sometimes/often struggled with basic
needs. Struggling youth were more likely to say their vote did not matter, but
also more likely to say they would participate if voice mattered, communities
changed, and candidates followed through. The pre-election cross-section does
not observe an eventual vote or a causal hardship effect.

The [CCES medical-affordability record](records/us-cces-medical-affordability-political-participation-2018-2020.json)
links same-respondent medical-expense hardship to different political acts:
turnout, contact, donation, and protest do not move together, and attribution
to government versus bad luck changes targeted contact/protest comparisons.
Its exploratory weighted screens are not causal, design-based estimates.

The [CCES job-loss follow-up record](records/us-cces-panel-job-loss-political-followup-2012-2014.json)
adds a retained three-wave panel comparison. Among 2012 respondents reporting
a job loss in the prior two years, 23.93% reported any local political meeting,
sign, campaign work, or donation in 2014, versus 31.57% among other panelists.
The job-loss group also had higher 2012 and 2014 uninsurance shares, but its
2014 congressional disapproval was nearly the same (80.85% versus 81.88%) and
favorable approval was slightly higher (13.86% versus 10.06%). Reported job
loss is retrospective and the comparisons are descriptive.

**Supported subtheme:** a work disruption can be followed by a lower measured
local-action surface without yielding a uniform institutional-approval result.
Material disruption, participation capacity, institutional evaluation, and
political meaning must be kept as distinct routes.  
**Not established:** a dated separation, job-loss causal effect, employment or
income recovery, benefit remedy, a trust change, vote choice, or exit. Prior
politics, health, income, duration of unemployment, local context, and panel
retention can jointly shape the observed difference.

The [guaranteed-income experiment record](records/us-nber-guaranteed-income-political-attitudes-behavior-2024.json)
provides a causal boundary: a three-year $1,000/month transfer to low-income
participants did not detectably change broad political participation,
engagement, government trust, polarization, or support for democracy, despite
some affect and work-meaning changes. A material intervention is not therefore
a general political-mobilization treatment.

**Supported subtheme:** material conditions can shape the resources and
interpretive setting for action, but attribution, credible responsiveness,
identity, and institution-specific experience determine whether pressure is
converted into withdrawal, targeted voice, or no observed broad shift.  
**Not established:** a dated hardship → remedy → recovery → durable action
sequence for the same person, or a universal political effect of income change.

## 7. Economic place conditions can constrain formal civic capacity without proving community collapse

The [economic-place civic-capacity record](records/us-census-economic-place-civic-capacity-2002-2015.json)
uses about 990,000 CPS Volunteering Supplement observations. Higher local
economic disadvantage and income inequality were associated with lower formal
volunteering; the Great Recession dampened volunteering overall, and the prior
rural volunteering advantage disappeared afterward. The data end in 2015 and
the design is associational.

**Supported subtheme:** local economic conditions can shape the time, resource,
and organizational conditions for one form of civic action. Formal volunteering
is therefore neither a pure cultural preference nor a complete measure of
community life.
**Not established:** individual causality, informal mutual aid, belonging,
voting/protest, current trends, or a conclusion that community capacity
disappeared.

## 7a. Local employer power can enter political participation through dependence, without proving one worker was coerced

The [electoral-monopsony record](records/us-nber-employer-market-power-political-influence-2026.json)
combines worker-survey evidence, individual voting data, and a shift-share
design based on national industry-concentration shocks and predetermined local
employment shares. Workers at major local employers more often reported
employer political communication and influence on voting or career
expectations. In the voting analysis, greater electoral monopsony power was
associated with increased Republican turnout; the paper's model distinguishes
passive worker internalization of job dependence from active employer
influence.

**Supported subtheme:** market structure can be part of the participation
environment: local job dependence may shape expectations about electoral
outcomes and coincide with a measurable turnout effect. This creates a bridge
between work control, place, and political action that cannot be reduced to
individual ideology alone.  
**Not established:** the same worker's employer message, belief change, vote,
coercion, welfare, or later voice; a universal local-employer effect; or a
modeled counterfactual election result. The public abstract does not disclose
the full survey/voting samples, and the identification depends on the
shift-share assumptions and concentration's separation from other local change.

## 8. Belonging can be an operational resource, not simply a reported attitude

The [political-homes record](records/us-circle-political-homes-youth-civic-capacity-2026.json)
reports that young people with access to a political home had about
20-percentage-point higher reported civic action and self-efficacy. The source
defines these settings as potentially providing safe third spaces, civic
information, food, financial compensation, and transportation, and reports an
association with buffering the mental-health cost of involvement.

**Supported subtheme:** social belonging and practical support can form a
participation infrastructure—the ability to act may depend on meals, travel,
safety, information, and peers as well as political interest.
**Not established:** causal effects of the setting, final turnout, policy
influence, durability, or whether participants were already more resourced or
motivated.

## 9. Nonvoting contains incompatible access and meaning routes

The [CPS voting-friction record](records/us-cps-voting-participation-friction-2024.json)
reports 65.3% voting among the citizen voting-age population in 2024, but its
nonvoter reasons cannot be reduced to apathy: 19.7% named lack of interest,
17.8% schedule conflict, 14.7% candidate/issue dissatisfaction, 12.4% illness
or disability, and smaller shares named registration, transport, or polling
place problems. The reasons are self-reported categories, not a causal model.

**Supported subtheme:** “did not vote” is an outcome with materially different
routes: a schedule, access, health, administrative, and political-judgment
route require different remedies and cannot be summed into disengagement.
**Not established:** a prior trigger, which barrier was binding, whether a
change would have produced participation, or other forms of civic action.

## 10. Visibility is a distinct political outcome from opinion, representation, and implementation

The [protest-effects record](records/us-nber-protest-effects-public-opinion-policy-2017-2022.json)
links 14 U.S. protest waves to digital, survey, electoral, legislator, and
policy measures. It finds substantial internet attention but minimal effects
on most other measured outcomes; the post-George-Floyd protests are a
reported exception with increased Democratic support and legislative activity
on racial issues. Backlash was generally associated with violence, not protest
as such.

**Supported subtheme:** attention is a real but intermediate political outcome.
It cannot be counted as durable opinion change, institutional response, policy
implementation, or personal recovery.
**Not established:** effects for individual participants and communities,
organizing capacity, a universal protest formula, or whether unmeasured
agenda/identity effects persisted.

## 10a. Organized political resources and mass sentiment can have different observed routes into policy

The [organized-interests responsiveness record](records/us-nber-organized-interests-gun-policy-responsiveness-2000-2024.json)
links 25 years of state campaign-finance data, a Gun Law Index, and estimates
of constituent sentiment across all 50 states. The paper reports that pro-gun
contributions were associated with deregulatory change under Republican
trifectas, while gun-safety contributions were associated with regulatory
tightening under Democratic trifectas. Its instrumental-variable estimates did
not find constituent sentiment causally predicted policy change under either
party trifecta. The abstract's high-support comparison is distinct: all 12
states with at least 90% support for universal background checks adopted them,
versus 8 of 36 states with 80%–90% support.

**Supported subtheme:** opinion, organized contribution, party control,
legislative response, and policy adoption are separate political stages. The
state-level results make a responsiveness pathway observable without reducing
representation to one sentiment measure.

**Not established:** that organized interests always override voters, donor
motivation, individual constituent contact or vote choice, policy
implementation, firearm safety or violence outcomes, public legitimacy, or
trust. Index construction, sentiment estimation, instrument validity, and
party-control selection remain material to the working-paper result.

## 10b. Party organization can be a separate representation mechanism from constituent preference

The [party-pressure representation record](records/us-nber-party-pressure-representation-house-voters-2026.json)
uses a spatial model that estimates U.S. House member ideology from roll-call
votes and constituent ideology from survey responses in a shared space. The
paper's preferred model reports that party pressure creates a wedge between
members' recorded votes and constituent preferences; without party pressure,
the model makes member and constituent ideologies appear less closely aligned.
It interprets party pressure as a strategic balance between legislative wins
and members' electoral concerns.

**Supported subtheme:** voter preference, member roll-call behavior, party
coordination, agenda bargaining, and representation are distinct institutional
surfaces. A recorded-vote divergence need not settle all district service or
constituent influence.

**Not established:** a causal effect of party pressure on any individual
representative, constituent trust, turnout, material policy outcome, or
legitimacy. The result depends on the working paper's ideology, issue, survey,
and party-pressure modeling assumptions and does not observe non-voted forms of
representation.

## 10c. Reputational pressure can change visible corporate political giving without demonstrating a durable political realignment

The [corporate-PAC reputational-risk record](records/us-nber-corporate-pac-reputational-risk-temporary-divestment-2026.json)
uses a 2000–2024 corporate-PAC/House-candidate panel and fatal school shootings
as gun-violence-salience shocks. After fatal school shootings, corporate PAC
contributions to Republicans fell 70.3% in competitive districts and 60.9% in
safe Democratic districts in the reported results; the abstract reports no
corresponding effect in safe Republican districts and no detected substitution
to Democrats. The divestment was stronger for consumer-exposed firms and
returned to baseline within nine months.

**Supported subtheme:** reputation risk can alter one visible political-spending
channel selectively and temporarily. Candidate race, district safety, consumer
exposure, PAC activity, lobbying, trade-association activity, and policy
influence are distinct organizational routes.
**Not established:** a policy effect, a broader reduction in corporate
influence, a consumer-behavior response, public legitimacy change, or durable
corporate political strategy. PAC contributions are not a complete record of
firm political action, and the public abstract does not disclose the full panel
denominator or all specifications.

## 10d. Formally race-neutral local maps can still produce unequal representation through geography and institutional design

The [local-redistricting record](records/us-nber-local-redistricting-race-representation-2026.json)
reconstructs 3,712 U.S. city-council and county-commission maps covering
18,521 districts, joins voter-file and Census data, and compares enacted maps
with simulated race-blind maps meeting common districting constraints. Enacted
maps overrepresented White voters and underrepresented Black and Hispanic
voters, with the gaps predicting officeholder racial composition. The
race-blind simulations reproduced much of the disparity, while enacted maps
improved substantially more on that baseline where Voting Rights Act oversight
or districting litigation applied.

**Supported subtheme:** representation is shaped by racial geography, map
rules, enacted boundaries, legal oversight, and officeholder selection. A
formal neutrality label does not determine representational equality, and a
geographic baseline does not make every enacted disparity accidental.
**Not established:** any single map's intent, a universal causal effect of
oversight, constituent service, policy outcomes, welfare, trust, turnout, or
national alignment. The simulated baseline depends on chosen districting
constraints, and jurisdictions with oversight or litigation may differ in
visibility and capacity.

## 10e. A close electoral win can become an organizational investment route rather than a one-day expression of preference

The [election winner-premium record](records/us-nber-election-winner-premium-political-investment-2026.json)
uses close U.S. presidential-election outcomes during the 1940–68 realignment
to trace later local political change. Counties where Democrats narrowly won
subsequently showed pronounced increases in Democratic local officeholding and
voter support. The paper identifies advertising, direct voter outreach, and
civil-rights organizing as channels, and reports the largest effects in
historical urban, Black, and union areas. Its local-news evidence also treats
the result as a binary winner/loser event, with celebration or mourning rather
than a marginal-vote narrative.

**Supported subtheme:** a narrow electoral result can change the later
organizational environment—investment, outreach, local officeholding, and
support—rather than simply registering preexisting preference. Vote result,
party investment, organizational capacity, and later political alignment are
separate stages.

**Not established:** a current U.S. effect, an effect of every close election,
household material benefit, national policy, trust, democratic legitimacy, or
a single causal contribution from organizing rather than national strategy,
migration, repression, economic change, media, or civil-rights conflict. This
is a historical 1940–68 study, not evidence about contemporary parties or
coalitions.

## 11. Recovery judgment can remain unsettled long after the emergency headline ends

The [Harvey recovery-legitimacy record](records/us-harvey-recovery-aid-legitimacy-2018.json)
is a weighted survey of 24 heavily affected Texas Gulf Coast counties about a
year after the storm. Fifty-eight percent reported household damage or
job/hours/income loss; among affected residents, 30% still reported disruption,
42% said they were not getting needed help, and 23% said Harvey had worsened
their financial situation. Only 9% of all area residents were aware of a
federal long-term-funding public-input route, while relief confidence was
mixed. These are regional self-reports, not linked aid records.

**Supported subtheme:** aid receipt, unmet need, procedural visibility, and
legitimacy can remain separate after a declared recovery; administrative
approval or aggregate spending cannot stand in for a socially settled outcome.
**Not established:** causal aid effects, observed application disposition,
later voting or organizing, trust change, or a general disaster-legitimacy
result.

## 11a. Financial dissatisfaction and generalized trust are historically related, but not one causal path

The [historical GSS financial-trust record](records/us-gss-financial-trust-historical-1972-2024.json)
uses weighted anchor-year cumulative survey screens. In 2024, 14.95% of people
not satisfied at all with their financial situation said most people can be
trusted, versus 32.90% of people pretty well satisfied; the corresponding
fairness shares were 23.66% and 58.87%. The direction is visible in earlier
anchors too: in 1972, trust was 39.45% versus 49.60%, and fairness 53.10%
versus 63.62%, for the same two financial-satisfaction categories. The 2024
survey design and weights require separate harmonization before formal trend
inference.

**Supported subtheme:** financial satisfaction, generalized interpersonal
trust, and fairness judgment are historically associated but are distinct
social surfaces. The persistence of a gap does not establish that household
conditions directly generate a social-trust outcome.  
**Not established:** a causal financial-dissatisfaction effect, an unbroken
annual trend, institutional trust, political behavior, action, or exit. Cohort,
education, age, race, health, party identity, survey mode, nonresponse, and
question administration may explain part or all of the association.

## 12. Financial worry, trust, and vote remain identity-laden rather than a single economic gradient

The [ANES panel record](records/us-anes-panel-worry-trust-vote-2024.json)
uses weighted descriptive cross-tabs among respondents retained in the
2016–2020–2024 panel. In 2024, 70.3% of the extremely financially worried
reported trusting the federal government only some of the time or never, versus
51.9% of the not-at-all-worried. Yet the record stresses a non-monotonic
pattern, and very worried respondents reported Trump (68.9%) more than Harris
(29.9%), while the not-at-all-worried showed the opposite (32.0% and 66.5%).
Panel temporal ordering does not remove party identity, prior belief,
information, or retrospective judgment as explanations.

**Supported subtheme:** worry can be connected to institutional judgment and
choice without forming a simple hardship-to-distrust or hardship-to-vote law.  
**Not established:** that a bill, price, or hardship caused a vote, a trust
change, or a particular political interpretation.

## 12a. Inflation harm, causal attribution, and remedy preference are separate political judgments

The [inflation-cause-beliefs record](records/us-nber-inflation-cause-beliefs-2024.json)
uses a U.S. online survey with conjoint and information experiments. Respondents
named government actions, production costs, supply disruption, oil prices,
firms, labor-market changes, and Federal Reserve action as possible causes;
Republicans more often attributed inflation to government policy, while
Democrats more often anticipated inequality effects. The most-noted reported
consequence was greater complexity in household decision-making. Many
respondents believed inflation could be managed without substantial activity or
employment trade-offs, and the study reports that relevant beliefs were hard to
move experimentally while monetary tightening remained unpopular.

**Supported subtheme:** shared recognition of price harm can coexist with
different causal stories, trade-off beliefs, and acceptable remedies. The
translation from household pressure to institutional judgment is therefore
interpretive and politically structured, not a single economic reflex.  
**Not established:** a respondent's actual price basket, rent, debt, income, or
financial loss; the economic cause of inflation; durable belief change;
spending; policy implementation; turnout; or vote choice. This is not a
household panel or a price-to-ballot causal path.

## 12b. Perceived institutional alignment can shape attention and belief updating before it changes public behavior

The [Federal Reserve partisan-trust record](records/us-nber-fed-partisan-trust-2024.json)
uses a 2024 U.S. online survey experiment with 5,205 politically representative
respondents. Sixty-three percent were classified as viewing the Fed as a
political out-group; 66% of Democrat-leaning respondents saw it as favoring
Republicans, while 60% of Republican-leaning respondents saw it as favoring
Democrats. In-group respondents reported higher pre-treatment management trust
(4.2 versus 3.1 on a seven-point scale), were more willing to seek Federal
Reserve communication, and placed more weight on it when updating inflation
expectations. Perceived alignment itself is not randomized.

**Supported subtheme:** institutional trust is partly an information-route
condition. A person can encounter the same public institution yet differ in
whether they seek its communication and how much weight they place on it,
without that difference establishing institutional bias or a real-world policy
effect.

**Not established:** actual Federal Reserve political alignment, lasting media
use, spending, portfolio behavior, realized inflation, voting, or a population
causal effect beyond the experimental setting.

## 12c. Environmental goals can survive while expected household cost reshapes instrument choice

The [environmental cost-beliefs record](records/us-nber-environmental-policy-cost-beliefs-policy-support-2026.json)
compares a probability-based U.S. respondent sample with environmental
economists while holding stated environmental impacts constant across policy
instruments. Respondents preferred weaker targets for consumer taxes and
stronger targets for standards. Unlike the expert comparison group, they
expected market-based instruments to raise consumer energy bills more than
standards. The source treats this as a policy-instrument preference tension,
not an observed bill or a measure of environmental commitment.

In a separate educational-video experiment, explanations of pass-through and
cost-effectiveness moved beliefs toward expert views and affected policy
support. The paper's politically constrained model then shows how beliefs can
favor a less cost-effective standard over a market instrument. That final
instrument-selection result is modeled, not a forecast or observed policy
outcome.

**Supported subtheme:** support for an environmental end and support for a
particular means can diverge through expected household incidence. Information
can change the belief and stated-support stages, while actual prices, fairness,
trust, enacted policy, and political behavior remain separate questions.

**Not established:** actual energy-bill pass-through, distributional fairness,
pollution or health effects, durable belief change, voting, instrument
adoption, or whether respondents' preferences reflect only cost rather than
control, transparency, enforcement, or trust.

## 12d. Health-cost concern can reach trust and policy demand, but not through one universal political mechanism

The [ANES health-cost concern, trust, and policy-demand record](records/us-anes-health-cost-concern-trust-policy-2024.json)
uses official 2024 panel survey tables. Pre-election, 63.1% of people
extremely concerned about paying health-care expenses said they trusted the
federal government only some or none of the time, compared with 53.3% of those
not at all concerned. In the post-election wave, 61.3% of the extremely
concerned and 57.5% of the not-at-all-concerned favored increasing government
help paying for health insurance. Within strong Republicans, support for more
help rose from 30.7% among the not-at-all concerned to 50.0% among the
extremely concerned; among strong Democrats, the reported direction was
opposite (84.6% to 71.2%). The retained controlled cross-tabs are descriptive,
and some extreme-concern cells are small.

**Supported subtheme:** a forward-looking health-payment concern can coexist
with lower federal trust and later health-insurance policy demand, but party
identity changes both the level and direction of the policy translation. A
material concern is therefore neither a uniform legitimacy effect nor a single
political demand.  
**Not established:** a dated bill, treatment, coverage denial, or care delay;
that concern caused trust or policy preference; remedy receipt; vote choice;
political contact; switching; exit; or health and financial recovery.

## 12e. Same-person material and institutional transitions still do not identify a material-to-trust mechanism

The [HTOPS material/trust panel record](records/us-census-htops-material-trust-panel-april-june-2025.json)
links 6,564 public-use respondents from April to June 2025, retaining April
weights for descriptive transition cells. Material measures show substantial
persistence: 41.8% of valid linked respondents reported expense difficulty in
both waves and 8.4% changed from no to yes; 69.9% reported price increases in
both waves. Institutional measures also have their own transitions: 74.5%
continued agreeing that policymakers need federal statistics, while only 11.8%
reported high confidence in Congress in both waves. These are separate marginal
transitions, not a cross-variable material-condition-by-trust transition table.

**Supported subtheme:** household expense, food, energy, price, and work
conditions—and views about federal statistics or Congress—can be observed for
the same retained people at two dates without collapsing them into one
legitimacy measure. Persistence and change are measurable on different clocks.
**Not established:** that a material change caused an institutional judgment,
or the reverse; population longitudinal estimates after attrition, a bill or
price event, party identity, information exposure, political contact, vote,
policy response, or later recovery. The linked subset uses cross-sectional
baseline weights and is not a documented attrition-adjusted longitudinal
estimator.

## 13. A disaster can create a representative-level policy-and-election sequence without proving constituent repair

The [hurricane accountability record](records/us-nber-hurricane-climate-policy-electoral-accountability-2026.json)
studies federal disaster declarations and congressional districts from
1989–2014. Representatives from hurricane-hit districts were more likely to
support environmental-regulation bills in the following year, with a persistent
agenda shift and reported checks against logrolling and lobbying explanations.
The study also finds an electoral penalty in later elections. It does not link
individual damage, aid, climate attribution, voter meaning, or implementation
to that district-level sequence.

**Supported subtheme:** a local shock can travel through representation,
legislative agenda, and electoral consequence without establishing a household
recovery or a resident-level political mechanism.  
**Not established:** why voters responded, whether the policy was implemented,
or whether it changed emissions, insurance, resilience, or security.

## 14. Platform-mediated news creates separate attention, trust, and action routes

The [news-platform civic-life record](records/us-pew-news-platform-civic-engagement-2025.json)
reports descriptive engagement types: 9% of adults were Mobilizers, among whom
87% reported voting in 2024 and 65% contacting an official, compared with 41%
and 3% among Outsiders. Separately, 21% of adults regularly got news from
social-media influencers (37% under age 30); among those users, 65% said this
helped them understand current events and 70% said it differed from news
elsewhere. The studies do not join the same respondents or establish a platform
effect.

**Supported subtheme:** information source, perceived understanding,
institutional trust, and political action are connected but separate surfaces;
digital news attention is not demonstrated civic influence.  
**Not established:** content accuracy, persuasion, polarization, platform
causation, or whether influencer news strengthens or weakens informed control.

## 14a. A synthetic outgroup conversation can change immediate contact willingness without showing a durable civic relationship

The [synthetic-contact record](records/us-ai-synthetic-contact-cross-partisan-2026.json)
contains five preregistered online U.S. partisan experiments. In the
incentive-compatible aversion study (608 participants), respondents accepted
5.06 minutes of an aversive alternative to avoid a three-minute AI-outgroup
conversation, compared with 9.65 minutes to avoid a live-human outgroup
conversation. In a separate costly-choice experiment (1,069 participants),
67% assigned to an outgroup bot chose a real outgroup conversation afterward,
versus 61% after an apolitical-chat control (odds ratio 1.33, 95% CI
1.04–1.71). These are experimental contact-route outcomes, not a measure of
public deliberation or real-world political action.

The endpoint prevents a simple depolarization reading. In the one-week
randomized follow-up of 1,104 Democratic participants, the preregistered
outgroup-warmth effect was d = 0.05 and not statistically significant (p =
.16), after an immediate d = 0.46 effect. The source also reports a
within-person 4.3-point warmth change after a ten-minute conversation, but
that result is not a controlled between-group estimate.

**Supported subtheme:** a lower-social-threat synthetic encounter can act as a
short-run bridge from outgroup avoidance to one immediate, costly choice for
human contact. Immediate warmth, willingness to talk, sustained relationship,
and collective political consequence remain separate stages.

**Not established:** faithful representation of political opponents, durable
belief or warmth change, reciprocal conversation quality, cooperation, voting,
institutional trust, population-scale depolarization, or safe public
deployment. The evidence is an online preprint experiment, not a civic-program
or electorate-level evaluation.

## 15. The perceived cost of speaking can coexist with support for principled action

The [courage and social-risk record](records/us-gallup-courage-social-risk-2026.json)
finds 67% of adults said courage in U.S. society was weaker than 20 years ago,
79% called courage from elected leaders very important, and 44% were concerned
about the personal costs of taking a principled stand. These are perceptions in
a single 2026 survey, not observations of speech, protest, workplace voice,
retaliation, or historical behavioral change.

**Supported subtheme:** the cultural valuation of voice and the perceived
personal risk of exercising it can coexist; stated approval cannot stand in for
an available action route.  
**Not established:** declining actual courage, a retaliation rate, or the
effect of perceived risk on civic or workplace behavior.

## 16. Shared national reference points coexist with sharply different accounts of security and opportunity

The [America 250 identity record](records/us-apnorc-america-250-identity-rights-dream-2026.json)
shows a distinction that is easily lost in a single "national unity" measure.
In its probability-panel survey, 87% said freedom of speech and 86% the right
to vote were important to U.S. identity, while 46% said speech faced a major
threat. Only 33% said the belief that hard work leads to getting ahead still
holds, compared with 50% who said it once held but no longer does; those are
cross-sectional meaning judgments, not a legal-rights or mobility audit.

The [America 250 social-ritual record](records/us-gallup-america250-social-ritual-meaning-2026.json)
adds a different layer: 66% planned to recognize the anniversary in some way,
and among planners 66% intended to spend time with family or friends, 32% to
take part in a community event, and 17% to attend an official program. Among
the separately defined civic/educational subgroup, history learning and
listening to personal stories were common intended activities. These are
nonexclusive plans, not observed participation, agreement, learning, or later
civic action.

**Supported subtheme:** national identity is carried through at least three
separate routes—shared rights language, contested assessments of whether those
rights and opportunity are secure, and family/community ritual that need not
run through official institutions. A common symbol or stated value therefore
does not demonstrate a common diagnosis, trust judgment, or political program.

**Not established:** actual anniversary participation, cross-party cohesion,
legal protection, economic mobility, a causal role for ritual in trust, or a
durable change in political behavior.

## 16a. Perceived family progress and belief in the national promise can diverge

The [Latino mobility and American-dream record](records/us-pew-latino-american-dream-mobility-2026.json)
uses a 4,923-person national survey of Latino adults. Fifty-seven percent said
their own standard of living was better than their parents' at the same age and
53% expected their children to do better, while only 20% said they had achieved
the American dream and 27% said it does not exist. Immigrants versus U.S.-born
Latinos reported different perceived mobility and attainability surfaces: 70%
versus 49% said they were doing better than parents, while 35% versus 48% said
the dream was harder to achieve than it had been for prior generations. In
open-ended coding, roughly 60% mentioned financial security, economic
opportunity, or homeownership as part of the dream, alongside overlapping
relational and freedom meanings.

**Supported subtheme:** a group's reported intergenerational progress,
expectation for children, and appraisal of whether a national promise exists
are distinct cultural judgments. Material, family, status, and meaning routes
can coexist without producing one optimism or legitimacy score.  
**Not established:** observed income, wealth, homeownership, migration or
nativity effects, causal mobility, legal status, voting, political action,
consumer behavior, or future outcomes. The reported categories are
self-assessments, and the open-ended themes overlap rather than form a single
hierarchy of values.

## Cross-subtheme finding and next test

The source-led chain is:

```text
institutional experience, local economy, comparative treatment, social tie,
public signal, financial worry, or local shock
  -> fairness, belonging, trust, identity, practical support, or interpretation
  -> available time, knowledge, safety, transport, and action route
  -> complaint, appeal, vote, organizing, non-use, attention, or no action
  -> institutional response, lived consequence, and later trust
```

The corpus often observes a single part of that chain well. It should not
claim the rest by implication. The next decisive evidence needs a longitudinal
or event-linked design that preserves the triggering experience, relevant group
and place, interpretation, action menu, action taken or declined, institutional
response, and later trust—while retaining the possibility that no action is
the appropriate or feasible outcome.
