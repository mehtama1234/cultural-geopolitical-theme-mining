# Rotation DR — E-waste, repair, reuse and informal livelihoods

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological / environmental / household / labour / geopolitical

## Why this rotation

The atlas has examined batteries and critical-mineral circularity, but not the
full social life of electronic devices. E-waste is simultaneously a household
affordability issue, a digital-access issue, a repair and skills economy, a
worker-health issue, a child-protection issue, a transboundary trade issue and a
source of secondary materials. A policy can reduce waste while making devices
more expensive to repair, formalising away informal livelihoods, or exporting
hazardous burdens to communities with weak protection.

This rotation asks whether repairability, reuse, take-back, recycling and right-
to-repair policy actually extend usable device life and preserve affordable
access while improving worker and community health.

## Research question

When a manufacturer, regulator, municipality or circular-economy programme
introduces repairability rules, spare-parts access, take-back, refurbishment,
formal recycling, right-to-repair or device-reuse schemes, under what conditions
does it create longer usable lifetimes, affordable access, decent local work and
safe material recovery—and who bears data, cost, toxic exposure, child-labour,
trade and livelihood risks?

## Conversion chain

```text
device failure / replacement demand / material scarcity
  -> design, warranty, repair, reuse, take-back and recycling rules
  -> consumer decision + repairer/workers + collection/export pathway
  -> usable life extension or material recovery + income + device access
  -> exposure, health, affordability, digital inclusion and local value
  -> accountability, safe work, traceability, remedy and durable circularity
```

The unit must remain a named device/product stream, household, repairer,
worker, facility or locality. Tonnes collected and a legal right to repair are
intermediate evidence, not proof of safe recovery or affordable access.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. E-waste is growing faster than formal recovery

WHO reports that 62 million tonnes of e-waste were produced globally in 2022,
with only 22.3% documented as formally collected and recycled. E-waste contains
valuable materials such as gold and copper but also toxic substances such as
lead and cadmium. The gap between device disposal and documented formal recovery
creates an opportunity for circularity and a risk of informal exposure.

Source: [WHO electronic waste factsheet](https://www.who.int/vietnam/news/fact-sheets/detail/electronic-waste-%28e-waste%29)
and [ITU Global E-waste Monitor 2024](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Environment/Documents/Publications/2025/d-gen-e_waste.01-2024-pdf-e.pdf).

### 2. Informal recycling is an income system and a health hazard

WHO describes informal recycling practices including open burning, acid
processing and manual dismantling. Children and adolescents may work at sites,
accompany parents or live near processing, creating exposure through work,
household and community pathways. Women of child-bearing age and pregnant
people may also face exposure risks. WHO calls for worker protection, exposure
monitoring, safer processing, health services and action across the product
life cycle.

The cultural and geographical issue is not solved by labelling informal work as
“illegal” or “backward.” Many workers depend on material recovery for income;
exclusion without transition can remove livelihoods while leaving the waste
stream and unsafe processing intact.

Sources: [WHO e-waste and child health](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/children-and-digital-dumpsites-e-waste-and-health)
and [WHO prevention of e-waste impacts](https://www.who.int/health-topics/environmental-health/preventing-impacts-of-electronic-waste-on-child-health).

### 6. Agbogbloshie shows why formalisation must preserve livelihoods while reducing exposure

WHO documents a pilot facility established by Pure Earth and Green Advocacy
Ghana at Accra's Agbogbloshie site. The facility enabled workers to strip and
recycle copper and aluminium without burning plastic sheaths; the project had
to adapt after initial machines were unsuitable for small wires and cables, and
training was provided with Ghana Health Service support. This is a named
informal-work-to-safer-processing case and an important counterexample to
one-time technology transfer. The public record does not establish exposure
reduction, worker income, child-labour exit, equipment uptime, recovered
material qualification or long-term livelihood transition.

Source: [WHO, Agbogbloshie risk-reduction intervention](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/341718/9789240023901-eng.pdf).

### 7. Coventry connects device reuse to digital inclusion

Coventry's #CovConnects system reports more than 6,000 devices distributed,
350 devices repaired, more than 2,800 kg of e-waste responsibly handled,
220,000+ kg CO2-equivalent emissions saved and 15,000+ residents impacted.
Devices, MiFi units and SIM cards were distributed through 200+ partners and
133 digital-inclusion hubs, including support for older adults, young people
and survivors of domestic abuse in temporary accommodation. This closes a
device-reuse-to-access pathway with local institutional reach, but the public
report does not provide device-level usable lifetime, recipient affordability,
repair recurrence, data-erasure outcomes, accessibility or employment and
education continuity.

Source: [Coventry City Council, #CovConnects phase-one report](https://www.coventry.gov.uk/cov-connects/help-people-impact-coventry/2).

### 8. Kingston separates collection from reuse and repair

The Royal Borough of Kingston's E-Waste Race engaged ten primary schools,
trained 547 students and reached more than 5,000 students, parents and
community members. In four weeks it collected 3,917 kg of material, with 732 kg
redirected for repair and reuse and 3,185 kg sent for recycling. The case makes
the material pathway more legible than a single collection tonnage and connects
circularity to STEM learning and local cultural practice. It still does not
establish final recycler destination, usable-life extension, household digital
access, worker safety or whether behaviour persisted after the event.

Source: [Local Government Association, Kingston E-Waste Race](https://lb2.local.gov.uk/case-studies/royal-borough-kingston-upon-thames-e-waste-race).

### 3. Right-to-repair policy closes a consumer-rights mechanism, not the whole loop

The EU’s Directive 2024/1799 promotes repair by requiring repair of specified
technically repairable goods, improving information and spare-parts access,
creating a repair platform, and extending the legal guarantee when consumers
choose repair. The Council and Commission frame repair as a way to reduce
premature disposal, resource demand and emissions while creating quality jobs.

The mechanism is significant but bounded. A formal right does not prove that a
repair is affordable, nearby, timely, data-safe, technically possible or chosen
over replacement. It also does not establish that small independent repairers,
informal workers or lower-income consumers share the benefits.

Sources: [European Commission repair directive](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/directive-repair-goods_en?prefLang=nl)
and [EUR-Lex Directive 2024/1799](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1799/oj/eng).

### 4. Repairability has a household affordability and agency pathway

The EU legal text identifies economic convenience, durability, availability,
proximity and time as factors in the repair-versus-replacement decision. This
is a useful household conversion: a device can be technically repairable but
functionally disposable if diagnosis, travel, data transfer, parts or labour
cost more than the household can absorb.

For digital devices, repair also affects continuity of work, education,
healthcare, identity and social connection. A repair policy that protects the
product but not data, accessibility or time may fail the user’s actual need.

Source: [Directive 2024/1799, rationale and repair rules](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1799/oj/eng).

### 5. Circularity can redistribute geopolitical and local burdens

E-waste contains secondary materials relevant to resource security, but flows
cross borders through resale, donation, recycling and disposal. A formal
collection rate can rise while hazardous processing, data-bearing-device risk,
or low-value residuals are shifted to another jurisdiction. Conversely, local
repair and refurbishment can preserve skills, extend affordable access and
reduce demand for new materials if worker protections and traceability are
real.

The evidence requirement is therefore chain-level: product origin, condition,
repair/reuse decision, destination, worker process, recovered material, buyer
use, residual waste and community exposure—not a single “recycled” label.

## Bounded proposition 123

**A right-to-repair law, repairability score, take-back scheme, refurbishment
programme, e-waste collection target, formal recycling facility or circular-
economy claim is not by itself longer usable device life, affordable digital
access, safe work, child protection, local livelihood security or verified
secondary-material recovery.** A legal obligation, tonnes collected, devices
repaired, repair platform, manufacturer disclosure or recycling certificate does
not establish that named households could afford and complete repair, preserved
data and accessibility, workers received safe conditions and remedy, children
were protected, informal livelihoods transitioned fairly, hazardous residues
were contained, or recovered material entered a qualified product stream.

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical interpretation

1. **Repair is a form of practical digital sovereignty.** The ability to keep a
   device working affects education, work, health, identity and participation.
2. **The formal/informal boundary is a distributional boundary.** Formalisation
   may improve safety and traceability, but can also remove income from workers
   without affordable alternative employment.
3. **A device has multiple afterlives.** Reuse, repair, parts harvesting,
   recycling and disposal produce different social and environmental outcomes.
4. **Circularity can hide export geography.** The place reporting collection may
   not be the place processing hazardous fractions or bearing residual exposure.
5. **Affordability and safety can conflict without public support.** Safer
   labour, certified parts and data protection may raise repair prices; policy
   must track who pays and who is excluded.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A repairable product can still be replaced because repair takes too long or
  costs more than a used replacement.
- Formal recycling can improve environmental controls while reducing informal
  workers’ income without transition, recognition or participation.
- A collection target can rise while reuse falls or hazardous fractions are
  exported; collection is not recovery.
- Refurbished devices can expand digital access, but poor battery life,
  inaccessible software or locked accounts can reduce usable benefit.
- Data wiping can protect privacy while destroying evidence, resale value or
  repairer ability to diagnose a device; the trade-off needs governance.
- Informal repair and recovery can provide genuine local expertise and income;
  the goal is safe, fairly compensated circular work, not simply removal of
  informal actors from the record.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO and ITU
support the scale and exposure mechanisms; EU law supports the repair-policy
architecture; the household and informal-livelihood conversion remains **Tier
2** because public sources rarely follow the same device and people through
repair, use, worker conditions, material recovery and final destination.

## Next test

Build three named product-stream ledgers: low-cost smartphones, household
appliances covered by repairability rules, and mixed e-waste from one informal
recycling locality. Track:

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | failure, replacement, policy, collection or export event |
| Mechanism | design, parts, price, data lock, repair access, worker or trade pathway |
| Operation | named device/household/repairer/facility, condition, process and geography |
| Material consequence | usable-life extension, cost, access, pay, exposure, injury, recovered material or residual waste |
| Meaning | dignity, agency, fairness, local expertise, stigma and trust |
| Power | consumer choice, worker voice, traceability, complaint, enforcement and buyer accountability |

Follow each stream to final known destination and include devices replaced
despite repair, workers excluded by formalisation, households unable to pay,
and facilities that safely recovered material as counterexamples.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the global scale, health-risk, consumer-rights and
policy mechanisms, and now adds named safer informal processing in Ghana,
device-reuse/digital-inclusion operation in Coventry, and a Kingston
repair/reuse-versus-recycling material split. It does not close named repair
affordability, data safety, worker transition, exposure reduction, child
protection, usable-life extension, final material use or local legitimacy.
Those remain acquisition targets.
