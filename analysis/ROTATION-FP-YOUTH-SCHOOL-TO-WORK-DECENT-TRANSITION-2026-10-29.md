# Rotation FP — youth school-to-work transition and decent local futures

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question
**Research lane:** social, cultural, geographical, labour-market and technological change

## Why this rotation exists

Youth employment is often represented through unemployment, enrolment, skills
or programme counts. Those measures can miss the transition itself: whether a
young person can move from school, training, care or inactivity into a job with
safe conditions, fair pay, learning, voice, protection and a credible future in
their place. The transition is geographically and culturally uneven, and young
women, low-income-country youth and people outside formal education are easy to
under-count.

## Research question

When a state, employer, school, platform, donor or industry body funds skills,
career guidance, apprenticeship, entrepreneurship or placement, under what
conditions does it produce a named young person’s secure and decent work,
income agency, social protection, continued learning, gender-equitable mobility
and durable attachment to—or dignified choice about—their local community?

## Conversion chain

```text
school / training / guidance / apprenticeship / placement / enterprise support
  -> learner, employer and transition encounter
  -> credential, task, contract, wage, protection and voice
  -> job quality, income, learning, wellbeing, mobility or exclusion outcome
  -> status, belonging, agency, household security and local legitimacy
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. ILO Global Employment Trends for Youth 2024 reports a 13% global youth
   unemployment rate in 2023 and 20% of young people NEET; two in three young
   NEETs were women. Headline recovery therefore does not close the transition
   or gender question.
2. ILO finds the recovery is geographically uneven. Low-income countries have
   lower school/training participation and higher NEET exposure, while the Arab
   States and North Africa retain especially severe youth labour-market
   challenges. Regional averages should not be treated as a local route.
3. ILO distinguishes employment quantity from quality: temporary and informal
   work can be the only available option in developing regions, with effects on
   income, hours, safety and legal protection. A placement or employment count
   is therefore not a decent-work outcome.
4. ILO reports that youth-targeted active labour-market programmes can improve
   outcomes, with skills, entrepreneurship, employment services, wage subsidies
   and combined interventions varying by country income group and target group.
   Programme impact is not transferable without observing the same participant,
   employer and later employment conditions.
5. OECD Education at a Glance 2025 shows that school-to-work patterns differ
   across countries, including combinations of study and work and the duration
   of NEET exposure. Extended absence can reduce experience and essential-skill
   development, but the measure does not by itself identify cause or remedy.
6. OECD Skills Outlook 2025 emphasizes that career navigation and social capital
   are unevenly distributed: disadvantaged learners may rely more heavily on
   schools and public or non-profit guidance to access employer information,
   mentoring and opportunities. Local networks are part of the transition
   infrastructure.
7. A World Bank-supported Rwanda example reports industry-aligned training and
   subsequent employment/self-employment, but it is a program result in one
   setting, not proof that all short-course skilling produces retention, decent
   pay, protection or durable local opportunity.

## Bounded proposition

**A skills strategy, vocational course, apprenticeship, career platform,
certificate, entrepreneurship grant, job fair, placement rate or employer-led
training program is not by itself decent youth employment, fair income, social
protection, gender equity, wellbeing, continued learning, local belonging or
durable economic mobility.** Enrolment, completion, certification, placement
or self-employment counts do not establish that named young people retained
work, received fair pay, had safe conditions and voice, gained protection,
reduced care or debt burdens, or could choose whether to stay, migrate or
return to their community.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

- **Transition is place-specific:** a credential only has value within an
  employer, transport, housing, care and information ecosystem that can absorb
  the worker.
- **Gendered invisibility:** NEET status can include unpaid care, discouraged
  search, safety constraints and social norms; treating it as individual
  inactivity can misdiagnose the policy problem.
- **Formal versus informal futures:** a job can reduce unemployment while
  increasing insecurity if it is temporary, unpaid, unsafe or without remedy.
- **Youth voice and status:** programmes designed around employer demand may
  produce placement without agency, cultural fit or the ability to contest
  exploitation.
- **Migration and belonging:** mobility can be an achievement, a necessity or a
  form of loss. The outcome must distinguish chosen mobility from forced exit
  and measure origin and destination effects.
- **Social capital:** guidance, mentors, family networks, language, digital
  access and transport can determine who can convert training into interviews
  and who is invisible to the employer.

## Counterexamples and cautions

1. A high placement rate may count short contracts, unpaid work, subsidized
   employment or jobs unrelated to the training.
2. Self-employment may indicate agency, but may also reflect missing wage jobs,
   debt, unpaid family work or exposure without social protection.
3. Lower youth unemployment can coexist with discouraged workers, informality,
   migration or withdrawal from education and work.
4. Training aligned to current employer demand can become obsolete or narrow
   young people’s options if technology or regional demand changes.
5. Apprenticeships can transfer skills and status, but can also hide unpaid,
   unsafe or discriminatory work without inspection and grievance routes.
6. Women’s lower participation cannot be interpreted as lower ambition without
   measuring care, safety, mobility, discrimination and household bargaining.
7. A successful pilot may depend on unusually strong mentors, employers or
   financing and fail at scale or in a different geography.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote the Tier 1 distinction that youth transition is a multi-stage,
gendered and geographically uneven conversion, and that job quality must be
measured beyond employment counts. Keep the following as Tier 2:
**youth-training-to-decent-work-and-local-agency conversion**. The next
evidence must follow named participants from entry through training, placement,
retention and job-quality outcomes, with a comparison group or credible
counterfactual where possible.

## Next test

Construct a corridor-level participant ledger covering one vocational or
industry-led programme, a matched non-participant group, employer records and
12–24-month follow-up. Record gender, disability, care, migration choice,
transport, credential, contract, wage, hours, safety, social protection, voice,
turnover, continued learning, household contribution and return/relocation
choice. Include non-completion and harmful-placement cases.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What is currently supported | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | youth unemployment, NEET, education/training and anxiety indicators | local meaning and individual route |
| Mechanism | skills, guidance, employer links, entrepreneurship and active labour-market tools | selection, care, networks, discrimination and employer power |
| Operation | programmes and school/work combinations can be measured | named participant, employer, contract and service encounter |
| Material consequence | employment, informality, temporary work, wages and protection are relevant outcomes | retention, safe conditions, fair pay, household security and wellbeing |
| Meaning | status, agency, belonging, mobility and gender norms shape the transition | participant attribution, voice, cultural fit and remedy |
| Power | credentials, employer choice, worker voice and mobility can alter bargaining | durable job power, substitution, local opportunity and institutional learning |

## Source boundary

The packet closes the discovery and framing stages for youth school-to-work
transition. Public sources support scale, gender and geography, programme
mechanisms, and the distinction between employment quantity and quality. They
do not by themselves establish a named participant’s retained decent work,
fair pay, protection, wellbeing, agency or durable local belonging.
