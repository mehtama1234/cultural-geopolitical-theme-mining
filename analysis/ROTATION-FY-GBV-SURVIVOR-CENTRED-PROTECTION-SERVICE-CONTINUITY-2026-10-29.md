# Rotation FY — gender-based violence, survivor-centred protection and service continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** social, cultural, geographical, health, justice and humanitarian systems

## Why this rotation exists

Gender-based violence is often represented by prevalence, laws, hotlines,
awareness campaigns, shelter capacity or the number of people reached. Those
signals matter, but a survivor experiences a chain: safe disclosure, informed
choice, confidential care, protection from retaliation, coordinated referral,
health and psychosocial support, legal or economic options, recovery and
non-recurrence. The chain is spatial and cultural: transport, language,
disability access, household power, stigma, conflict, displacement, digital
exposure and trust in institutions can determine whether a nominal service is
actually usable.

## Research question

When governments, health systems, police, courts, shelters, employers, NGOs,
community institutions or digital services expand GBV response, under what
conditions does that capacity produce a named survivor's safe, confidential,
voluntary and coordinated protection-and-recovery pathway rather than a
contact, referral or case record without usable remedy or safety?

## Conversion chain

```text
prevalence / disclosure / risk / referral / service / protection / justice
  -> survivor reaches a safe and accessible entry point
  -> informed choice, confidential case management and coordinated care
  -> health, safety, legal, housing, income or child-protection outcome
  -> agency, recovery, non-recurrence, trust or institutional accountability
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. WHO's 2023 estimates report that 31.6% of women aged 15 and older—about
   840 million—have experienced intimate-partner physical or sexual violence
   or non-partner sexual violence. This is a prevalence signal, not evidence
   that a particular service or institution protected a particular survivor.
2. WHO's 2025 global overview identifies accessible survivor-centred services,
   multisectoral responses, better data, prevention and sustainable financing
   as action priorities. The policy direction itself does not establish
   completion, safety or remedy.
3. WHO Europe's 2025 policy review of 241 policies across 53 countries found
   that multisectoral strategies were common, but health-policy and clinical
   integration remained incomplete; it also flags the autonomy risk of
   mandatory reporting against an adult survivor's wishes. This makes consent,
   confidentiality and referral design part of the outcome, not administrative
   details.
4. UNFPA's 2026 assessment of 56 Indian One Stop Centres across 16 states and
   two Union Territories examines accessibility, staffing, survivor-centred
   care, case management, referrals, cross-sector coordination, data and
   disability inclusion. It is an operating diagnostic, not proof that every
   survivor received durable safety or remedy.
5. UNFPA's 2025 Nepal emergency reporting records 14,083 survivors supported
   through safe spaces and shelters in 2024, with case management,
   psychosocial support and referrals. The count shows service reach in a
   disaster setting; it does not close the later recovery, livelihood or
   non-recurrence stages.
6. The World Bank describes effective prevention and response as coordinated
   across health, social, legal, security and community actors, and identifies
   survivor-centred practice as a professional principle. Coordination is a
   mechanism requirement, not proof of a named survivor's successful outcome.

## Bounded proposition

**A gender-based-violence law, hotline, shelter, one-stop centre, health
protocol, police unit, case-management system, humanitarian referral,
prevention campaign or survivor fund is not by itself safety, agency, health,
justice, livelihood continuity, family security, cultural dignity or
non-recurrence.** A policy, disclosure, referral, service contact, case closure
or conviction does not establish that named survivors could safely access care,
control decisions, avoid retaliation, receive effective remedy, recover
economically or remain free from repeat violence.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The unit of analysis should be the survivor or household pathway, not only the
facility. Rural distance, transport cost, shelter location, conflict or
disaster displacement, language, disability, age, migration status, caste or
ethnic marginalization, LGBTQ+ identity, digital access and dependence on a
partner or employer can change the meaning of “available.” Community and faith
actors may reduce stigma and improve trusted entry, but can also reproduce
silence or pressure; this must be tested locally. A survivor-centred pathway
must preserve voluntary choice and confidentiality rather than treating family,
community or institutional reconciliation as the outcome.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Prevalence, survivor-reported service use and GBV information-management data
  are different units; service records cannot be used as population prevalence.
- A one-stop centre can reduce search and travel burden while still failing on
  staffing, accessibility, privacy, referral completion, safety or follow-up.
- Mandatory reporting may improve institutional visibility in some settings,
  but can reduce disclosure or autonomy for adult survivors when imposed
  against their wishes; child-protection rules require separate safeguards.
- A conviction or protection order is not the same as physical safety,
  compensation, housing, income recovery or non-recurrence.
- Emergency services can preserve access during displacement while remaining
  short-term; do not infer durable recovery from crisis-period reach.
- Digital reporting and communications can widen access, but can also expose
  survivors to surveillance, retaliation, doxxing or technology-facilitated
  abuse.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: GBV response is a multisectoral conversion
problem whose key gap is between institutional availability and safe,
survivor-controlled use. Keep the named survivor-to-recovery-and-non-recurrence
chain as **Tier 2** until case-level, longitudinal and geographically specific
evidence closes the later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | prevalence, disclosure, risk, policy or funding | strong global and system-level evidence |
| Mechanism | distance, cost, confidentiality, consent, stigma, referral, staffing and coordination | strong bounded evidence; place-specific variation remains |
| Operation | named survivor reaches a service, receives case management and makes informed choices | partial; One Stop Centre and emergency examples exist |
| Material consequence | health, safety, shelter, legal, child-protection or income result | not closed for a general cross-setting claim |
| Meaning | agency, dignity, trust, cultural safety and perceived control | not closed; requires survivor-informed follow-up |
| Power | non-recurrence, accountability, changed institutional practice and durable exit options | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Build a country-and-place comparison using One Stop Centre or equivalent
administrative records plus survivor-informed follow-up: referral completion,
wait and travel burden, confidentiality choice, repeat contact, protection and
health outcome, legal/economic result, retaliation, satisfaction, recurrence
and subgroup differences. Keep prevalence, service utilization and outcome
denominators separate. A useful test would compare urban/rural, conflict or
disaster and stable settings without exposing survivor identity.

## Source boundary

The public evidence closes the prevalence-to-response-design and
service-architecture stages. It does not justify a universal claim that a
hotline, shelter, one-stop centre, legal intervention or conviction produces
durable safety, recovery, cultural legitimacy or non-recurrence for named
survivors. Those remain acquisition targets.
