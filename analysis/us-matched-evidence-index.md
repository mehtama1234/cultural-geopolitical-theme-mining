# US matched-evidence passes

These are the deeper checks completed after the opening source packets. Each one joins several records, names the connection, and keeps the missing step visible.

## Reading order

### 1. Unpaid eldercare can become a second job before it becomes a bill

**Theme:** Cost / family / work / voice
**Route:** aging or illness → unpaid care → work and money loss
**Still open:** care quality, caregiver recovery, and public support

The US often pays for eldercare with family time before it pays for it with money. A worker drives a parent to appointments, manages medicines, prepares food, handles forms, or stays nearby in case something goes wrong. The care is real, but the cost may show up as fewer work hours, a job exit, lower savings, or exhaustion rather than as a care invoice.  The burden is not evenly shared. Women are more represented among unpaid providers in the BLS data, and NBER finds an earnings and employment decline after caregiving begins for women. This does not mean every caregiver has the same result. It means the system assigns measurable economic risk to a group already carrying much of the work.  The deeper finding is that unpaid care is a transfer from one person’s future to another person’s present. It can protect an older person and avoid a paid bill while narrowing the caregiver’s job, savings, health, and housing choices.  The care need rarely arrives as one clean task. It can begin with a ride, then add medicine management, meals, paperwork, home repair, and supervision. Each task may be small, but together they occupy the hours that make a job possible. A family may not recognize the change as a second job until a missed shift, a failed appointment, or an exhausted caregiver exposes it.

### 2. The cheaper monthly bill may require a large first payment

**Theme:** Cost / energy / customer
**Route:** appliance choice → upfront cost → energy bill
**Still open:** household payback, use, and access

An efficient appliance can lower the monthly bill, but the household must first pay for the product, installation, and sometimes financing. This creates a basic split: the household with the largest possible savings may be the least able to make the first payment.  DOE standards can improve the products available in the market. EIA's household survey can show which homes have which appliances and energy conditions. But neither fact proves that a particular family reaches the savings. Renters may not control the appliance. A homeowner may lack cash, wiring, credit, or a safe place to install it. Efficiency is therefore a cash-flow and control problem, not only a technology problem.  The first payment is only the first barrier. Delivery, removal of the old unit, electrical work, permits, and a repair that appears in month three can erase the expected saving. A rebate paid later does not help a family that cannot cover the purchase today. Financing can spread the cost, but interest and a missed payment change the result. The right comparison is not the sticker price against an annual energy estimate; it is the household's full cost until the saving becomes real.

### 3. When staying with a bank saves effort but gives up interest

**Theme:** Cost / finance / voice
**Route:** account habit → switching effort → lower return or stable funding
**Still open:** customer net return and bank stability

Many depositors leave money in the same bank even when another account pays more. That can be sensible. Moving direct deposit, automatic bills, savings, branch access, and trusted service takes time and carries error risk. It can also cost the customer interest while giving the bank steady, low-cost funding.  The important question is not whether an inactive customer is foolish or a bank is exploitative. It is whether the interest left behind is larger than the effort, risk, service value, and safety of moving. The answer changes with the account balance, bill complexity, digital access, age, disability, and need for a local branch.  The account is not just a place where money sits. It is a set of links: pay arrives, rent leaves, subscriptions renew, a card is connected, and an emergency transfer may be needed. A higher rate on one account may not compensate for breaking those links or for losing a branch that can resolve a problem. But the links can also give the bank a quiet advantage because the customer must do the work of moving them one by one.

### 4. When a bank fee becomes a wider credit problem

**Theme:** Cost / finance / voice
**Route:** low account cash → fee or overdraft → later cost
**Still open:** payment success and household recovery

Removing a bank fee gives a household immediate relief, but it may not solve the shortage that made the fee possible. A person with cash and savings may avoid the next cost. A person with almost no room may still need overdraft access, payday credit, family help, or another loan.  That creates two tests: did the charge disappear, and did the household gain lasting room? A fee waiver can stop one penalty while leaving the original bill unpaid. Overdraft can keep a payment moving while carrying the shortfall into another week. “No fee” is not the same as “safe payment.”  The deeper finding is that bank-fee policy works differently depending on liquidity. The people most exposed to the fee may benefit least from its removal if they still cannot repay the underlying shortfall.

### 5. When earning more leaves a family with less room

**Theme:** Cost / work / voice
**Route:** earnings rise → aid changes → real household room
**Still open:** same-family net resources and work outcome

A raise can increase wages while reducing food, health, child-care, transport, or cash help. The family may still be better off, but the gain can be smaller than the wage number suggests. Losing one program is not proof that total household resources fell.  The shape of the rule matters. A gradual phase-out can leave a family better off while making each new dollar worth less. A hard cutoff can create a sudden loss. A work rule can reduce program participation without increasing employment. Calling all of these a “benefit cliff” hides the choice and cost the family must carry.  The deeper finding is that a benefit rule changes the price of moving forward. It can reward work, make work possible, or make the next step too fragile. The wage is only one input into the decision.  The loss can be hard to see because programs recalculate on different schedules. A paycheck may rise today, food help may fall next month, Medicaid may change later, and child-care costs may begin when the new shift starts. The family has to make a decision before the full budget is known. A rule that is smooth in an annual model can feel like a sudden cliff when notices and bills arrive at different times.

### 6. Care time is a household price paid in work, money, and reachable places

**Theme:** US map
**Route:** connected evidence
**Still open:** next test

Care is not only a private feeling or a service category. It is a time claim on a household. When that claim arrives alongside higher prices, limited transport, or a fixed work schedule, the family has to decide what remains reachable and what gets deferred. The available evidence shows the pieces of that trade clearly: care is widespread and uneven; working caregivers report more price-related adaptation than otherwise similar employment groups; and low-resource households face a different transport option set. But the sources are not one longitudinal sample, so this is a bounded mechanism under test, not proof that care caused a particular loss of work, health, trust, or political action.

### 7. A cash benefit changes the budget—and the economic mood

**Theme:** Voice / cost
**Route:** cash benefit → household room → economic mood
**Still open:** trust, turnout, and voting

The 2021 Child Tax Credit changed more than a tax form. Monthly payments gave some families room to buy food, pay housing costs, and avoid an immediate shortfall. When the payments ended, household sentiment fell, especially among lower-income families with several children.  That is a material and emotional result, but it is not yet a voting result. A family can feel worse without changing its party choice. A person can blame government without turning out. The sound path is material change first, economic mood second, and political action only when measured directly.  The deeper finding is that payment timing changes how a household experiences the economy. A yearly credit divided into monthly support can protect a bill due this week. Ending it can turn the same family’s budget into a new loss even if wages and prices do not change that day.  The payment also changes the order in which the family handles risk. Cash can keep rent current, prevent a utility shutoff, pay for a car repair, or reduce a card balance. The same amount may buy food for one family and preserve childcare or medicine for another. A spending category is evidence of where money went, not proof of what would have happened without it.

### 8. Childcare can be a bill, a lost workday, or an unpaid family shift

**Theme:** Cost / family / work / voice
**Route:** child care need → paid or unpaid care → work change
**Still open:** same-family income, quality, and stability

When a child needs care, a household does not make one simple purchase. It pays a provider, asks family for help, changes a shift, misses work, or leaves paid work. The cost may appear as a weekly bill, but it may also appear as lost wages, a missed promotion, less rest, or a job chosen for its schedule rather than its pay.  This is why childcare belongs in both the family budget and the labor market. Paid care can be expensive and still make work possible. Unpaid care can be loving and useful while taking time from paid work. The real question is who supplies the hours and what the family has left afterward.  The deeper finding is that care is infrastructure for work. When it is unreliable, the family’s work path becomes a care path. A parent may look less attached to work when the real problem is that the care system has no dependable backup.  The hours have to match, not just the monthly price. A provider that is affordable but closes before a shift ends may force a parent to leave work early. A cheaper provider farther away may add transport time and late fees. A relative may cover the gap without a bill while losing paid work or rest. The usable price is the money, time, and risk required to cover every needed hour.

### 9. Credit can save today's purchase while shrinking tomorrow's room

**Theme:** Cost / finance / voice
**Route:** bill shock → cash gap → credit → future room
**Still open:** same-household repayment and later access

Credit is a bridge when cash arrives later. It can keep a car running, a bill current, or care available. But the bridge has a price: interest, fees, a payment schedule, a credit record, and less income available for the next surprise.  “Can borrow” and “can afford” are different. A card paid in full is not the same event as a balance carried for months. A buy-now-pay-later payment made on time is not the same as a late payment with added charges. A medical balance on a credit report is not the same as the illness and bill that caused it.  The hidden price is the loss of future choice. A payment that looks small can sit beside rent, insurance, childcare, and another installment. If those claims are already fixed, the household has less ability to respond to a new repair or a cut in hours. Credit converts an uncertain event into a known payment, but it does not remove the event. It moves the pressure forward and adds a lender to the household's monthly decisions.

### 10. Competition helps only when the customer can leave

**Theme:** Cost / customer / voice
**Route:** customer group → ability to leave → firm treatment and price
**Still open:** current comparable service and customer power

More sellers do not automatically mean fairer treatment. Competition helps a customer only when the customer can compare, switch, and receive a reasonably similar service somewhere else. Distance, money, information, identity checks, language, transport, and the cost of moving all decide whether choice is real.  This matters for discrimination because a firm’s incentive depends on the customer’s actual exit. A customer who can move to another lender or store can punish poor treatment. A customer who needs a particular branch, network, language service, credit product, or accessible location may have several logos but no cheap substitute. The market looks open from above while the customer is trapped inside a narrow path.  The deeper finding is that customer power is the missing link between competition and fair treatment. Seller count is a weak measure. The stronger question is: what could this person do today if the firm raised the price, denied service, or treated them worse?  Exit has a price even when no money changes hands. It can require another credit inquiry, a new deposit, a trip across town, a new account history, a different language, or the risk that the next firm will reject the customer too. A person may stay with a bad provider because the first provider already knows their records and payment history. That is not loyalty in the simple sense; it can be the cost of starting over.

### 11. The loss is only the first part of a fraud victim’s experience

**Theme:** Cost / finance / customer / voice
**Route:** scam → loss → recovery effort → institutional trust
**Still open:** same-consumer recovery and later use

Fraud is not finished when money leaves the account. The next questions are who will answer, who can reverse the payment, who will unlock the account, and who will carry the loss while the institutions investigate.  The payment route matters. Credit-card rules may offer a path to dispute a charge. A bank-transfer scam or other non-card payment may leave the household carrying more of the loss. An account can also be restricted while a firm reviews the event. The customer may lose money and access at the same time.  That is why trust should not be measured only by whether a person reports a scam. A customer may keep using a bank because there is no easy substitute while becoming more cautious, slower, or less willing to use transfers. The system can remain in use while confidence in it falls.  The first harm can be followed by a second one: the work of proving what happened. The customer may need to save messages, call several numbers, freeze an account, change passwords, file a police or regulator report, and explain why an authorized payment was still induced by deception. A person who is elderly, ill, working, or not fluent in English may have less time to complete that path. The recovery system can therefore sort victims by their ability to document the loss.

### 12. A financial record can become part of the next hardship

**Theme:** Cost / voice
**Route:** payment trouble → credit record → housing access
**Still open:** same-household housing result

A credit record is supposed to describe risk. It can also help create the next risk. A late payment, overdraft, eviction order, or missing credit history can change what a bank, lender, landlord, or utility asks the household to pay. The new cost may be a higher rate, a deposit, a co-signer, a worse location, or no account at all.  The household then has less room to recover, making another late payment more likely. The important shift is that the record is not only a report about the past. It can become a gate in the future.  That does not mean every record is unfair or every score is wrong. A lender may use a record to avoid a real loss. The research question is exact: what event made the record, who saw it, what decision followed, and what alternatives did the household have?  The record also has a time problem. A gatekeeper may see an old event after the household has recovered, while the household may still be paying the added rate or deposit. A later clean payment history may not remove the first barrier quickly. The record can preserve a past shock after the cash problem has ended.

### 13. A faster answer is not yet a remedy

**Theme:** Voice
**Route:** automation → service → remedy
**Still open:** human authority to change the answer

Automation can help a service worker answer faster and with a warmer tone. That is a real gain, but it is not the same as fixing a repeat problem, reversing a denial, explaining a charge, or reaching a person with authority.  The useful dividing line is not bot versus person. It is whether the customer can understand the decision, keep a record, correct an error, and reach someone who can change the answer. A human handoff without authority is only a change in voice.  The deeper finding is that service quality has two clocks: the time to the first reply and the time to a final remedy. A company can improve the first clock while leaving the second unchanged. The customer experiences the gap as repeated explanation, lost money, missed work, and declining trust.  There is a third clock too: the time before the customer gives up. A person may stop contacting the firm because each attempt takes work, the case is urgent, or the next channel appears no better. A short first response can therefore coexist with a long unresolved case and a missing complaint. The system should count abandoned cases, not only completed conversations.

### 14. The vote may respond more to lost buying power than to inflation alone

**Theme:** Cost / voice / work
**Route:** local prices + pay → buying power → blame → vote or turnout
**Still open:** individual burden, policy knowledge, and cause

“Inflation hurt the party in power” is too blunt. A local price rise and a loss of buying power are not the same thing. Two counties can have the same inflation rate while pay rises in one and falls behind in the other. The second county’s households have less left after rent, food, transport, debt, and care.  That makes real pay—a measure of pay after prices—a better starting point for political research than the inflation rate alone. It is still not a vote explanation. Party identity, local industry, immigration, social issues, news, and trust can move with the household budget. A county result can show where pressure and political change sit together, but not what any one person believed.  The deeper finding is that people may judge the economy through the room left after necessary costs. An official rate can improve while the household still feels poorer because its pay did not catch up with its own basket.  The feeling has a budget shape. A household may keep paying rent and still lose room for food quality, repairs, care, savings, or a short trip. Another household with the same pay growth may have a paid-off car, family help, or lower housing costs. People living in the same county can therefore give different answers to “How is the economy doing?”

### 15. Energy assistance can restore service without proving stability

**Theme:** Energy / cost / housing / voice
**Route:** energy shock → assistance or restoration → next bill and household stability
**Still open:** whether help ends or delays the sacrifice

Energy assistance clearly reaches households in crisis and can restore service. That is an important immediate result. It does not prove the household became stable. The next bill, a broken heater, a rent shortfall, unsafe temperature, skipped medicine, or a missed shift may still be waiting.  The useful unit is the same household over time: service on, a safe temperature, no sacrificed food or medicine, and enough room for rent and work. A restored connection answers what happened today. It does not answer whether the family can make it through the next bill cycle.  Waiting is part of the benefit's real value. A payment that arrives before a shutoff can preserve food, medicine, and work. The same payment after service is cut may require a reconnection fee, spoiled food, a missed shift, or a night in an unsafe home. Application date, approval date, payment date, and restoration date are separate events. A program can be generous on paper and still arrive too late for the crisis in front of the family.

### 16. An energy bill can take food, medicine, or safe temperature first

**Theme:** Cost / housing / work / voice
**Route:** home condition + energy price → bill pressure → sacrifice
**Still open:** health, work, housing, and trust outcome

Energy burden is not only the share of income spent on power and fuel. The 2024 EIA survey records a more concrete cost: 32.89 million US primary homes reported reducing or forgoing food or medicine to pay energy costs. It also records disconnect or delivery-stop notices, unhealthy home temperatures, and homes unable to use heating or air conditioning. The deeper pattern is a contest over control: the household may pay the bill, while the renter or low-income owner cannot quickly fix the building that creates it.  A ratio can miss the moment of harm. A household may have a modest annual burden but face a winter bill before payday, a deposit after moving, or a repair during a medical crisis. Another may spend a large share on energy because it chooses a warmer home and can afford it. The percentage identifies pressure; it does not say whether the home was safe or which other need lost the contest.

### 17. Family help can catch one fall by moving it to another household

**Theme:** Cost / family / work / voice
**Route:** household shock → family help → helper cost
**Still open:** receiver and helper's later stability

When one household loses money, health, housing, or care, relatives may fill the gap with cash, a room, rides, food, child care, or time. That help can prevent a missed bill or keep a person housed. It does not make the cost disappear. It moves the cost to a parent, child, sibling, or partner who may lose savings, work hours, rest, or the ability to handle a later emergency.  Family support is therefore a hidden safety net with uneven membership. A household with nearby relatives who have cash and time has a different system from one whose family is also short of money, far away, ill, or already in debt.  The deeper finding is that private help can hide public need. A family may avoid an eviction or payday loan because a relative stepped in. That does not mean the system worked; it may mean an unpaid transfer absorbed a cost that never reached a formal program.  The transfer also depends on trust and power inside the family. Help can be a gift, a loan, a place to stay, or care expected in return. Those forms are not equal. A no-interest loan may protect the receiver but create a future obligation. A room may prevent homelessness but add crowding or conflict. Care may save a paid-care bill while taking hours from the helper’s job. The record must show the terms, not just label all help as support.

### 18. A medical cost can change care, work, and debt in different ways

**Theme:** Cost / health / work / voice
**Route:** medical cost → care choice → debt or work shock
**Still open:** same-household health, income, and trust result

“Medical debt” sounds like one problem. It is several problems that can arrive in a different order.  The need for care comes first. Then comes the price, the insurance decision, the payment, and the question of what the household can no longer afford. A person may delay care before any bill arrives. A hospital visit may create a bill and make work harder at the same time. An unpaid balance may later become a collection record. Removing that record can help a credit decision without treating the illness or replacing the lost income.  These are not small distinctions. A policy can improve one layer and leave the others untouched. A family can have less medical debt because it received coverage, because it avoided care, or because a record was removed. The number alone does not tell us which happened.  The timing of the bill can be as important as its size. A deductible may be manageable over a year but impossible when treatment begins. A prescription may be affordable once and unaffordable every month. A bill can arrive after the worker has already lost hours, so the household faces lower income and higher cost together. A person may delay care not because the total cost is known, but because the first payment is not possible today.

### 19. Health coverage can hold a job in place

**Theme:** Work / cost
**Route:** health coverage → job choice → household security
**Still open:** the full value of the job tradeoff

A job can provide wages and a route to health care. That makes leaving more than a career decision. A worker may be choosing between a better job with a coverage gap, a familiar plan with a poor workplace, or a move that risks a family’s doctors and medicines.  The evidence does not say employer coverage is bad. It shows that coverage can change job behavior. The deeper question is what the worker gives up to keep it: higher pay, training, a better workplace, self-employment, a move, or time at home. A family may be protected by the plan and constrained by the way protection is tied to the job.  Coverage has a calendar as well as a price. A worker may lose the old plan on the last day of a job, wait for enrollment at the new one, and face a deductible that starts over. A family may have to keep a doctor, refill a medicine, or continue treatment across that gap. The transition cost can make a job offer look worse even when its annual wage is higher.

### 20. The first price shapes the choice before the full price appears

**Theme:** Cost / customer / finance / voice
**Route:** headline price → late cost → choice → final burden
**Still open:** same-household cost, quality, and repeat choice

A shopper cannot compare a cost that is hidden until late in the purchase. The first number on a listing or ticket attracts attention, starts the search, and can make the shopper spend time choosing. If taxes, service charges, shipping, or late fees appear only after that commitment, the customer is no longer comparing the same products from the start.  That does not make every separate fee wrong. Some costs are real and may be useful to show separately. The problem is timing. A customer who sees the full mandatory price early can leave or compare. A customer who sees it late may accept a worse deal because backing out now costs time, a deadline, a trip, or a needed service.  The deeper finding is that price display is part of the market, not decoration around it. The first number changes who enters the comparison and how much effort the customer has already spent before the final number arrives.  The sunk time is not equal for every customer. A family booking a necessary trip, a person buying a ticket for a fixed date, or a small business ordering a part may not be able to start over after a late charge appears. A customer with more flexible time can compare again. The same fee display can therefore have a larger effect on people who have less room to delay.

### 21. Insurance can turn a place risk into a staying problem

**Theme:** Energy / housing
**Route:** risk → insurance → ability to stay
**Still open:** move, default, or loss of coverage

Owning a home does not mean the cost of staying is fixed. In a high-risk place, insurance can become a second housing payment. The household must keep coverage to protect the home and often to satisfy a mortgage, but the premium, deductible, or loss of coverage can make the home harder to keep.  There is a second price in the record. A lower credit score can raise the insurance price even when two homes receive identical coverage. The household may therefore pay more because of where the home sits and because of what its financial history says.  The result is a pressure point between property and household cash. A higher premium may lead to a repair, a larger deductible, borrowing, a state-backed plan, a sale, or no coverage. The evidence does not tell us which choice every family makes. It shows why “Can I afford the mortgage?” is no longer enough.  The premium is only the entry price for protection. A deductible is the amount the family must produce after the loss. An exclusion can remove the event the family thought it had insured. A policy limit can leave rebuilding short. A claim can take months while the household pays for temporary housing, transport, storage, or repairs. The real question is whether the policy turns a disaster into a manageable bill or leaves the family with a second crisis.

### 22. The same energy bill means different things in different homes

**Theme:** Energy / housing / cost / customer
**Route:** housing type and tenure → bill and repair control → recovery path
**Still open:** whether control changes affordability and safety

Energy burden is usually presented as a percentage: energy spending divided by household income. That is useful, but incomplete. Two households can spend the same share of income and have very different ways to lower the bill.  A renter may pay the monthly bill but need a landlord to replace a window, furnace, or air conditioner. A manufactured-home resident may face a small, older structure with costly heating needs and fewer repair or financing choices. A homeowner may control the repair but lack the cash or credit to make it. The bill is visible; the power to change it is not.  That difference matters when a shock arrives. The renter can ask for a repair but cannot order one. The manufactured-home resident may pay more per square foot because the building or equipment is less efficient. The homeowner can choose an upgrade, but that choice may mean debt, a second mortgage, or postponing another need.  The point is not that one housing type is always worse. It is that “energy burden” describes pressure, not the route out of pressure.  The percentage can also hide the fixed amount left after energy. A household with a lower income may spend the same share as a higher-income household but have much less money for food, medicine, transport, and repairs. Two homes with the same percentage can therefore have different safety margins. The burden measure needs a remaining-cash measure beside it.

### 23. When a yearly income hides an unsafe month

**Theme:** Work / cost
**Route:** unstable pay → cash buffer → spending room
**Still open:** the same worker's later household choices

Two workers can earn the same amount over a year and still have very different lives if one cannot predict next month's pay. Rent, food, medicine, care, and car repairs arrive on dates. ### 26. When slower inflation still feels like a continuing loss

**Theme:** Cost / voice / customer
**Route:** price and wage change → buying power → trust pressure
**Still open:** same-household basket, substitution, and vote

People notice the price of a repeated item, a rent renewal, an insurance bill, or a smaller package more easily than a broad average. Those visible changes can dominate how the household judges the economy, even if another category became cheaper. That does not make the judgment complete, but it tells us which prices are doing the political and emotional work.

**Still open:** state policy forms, prices, and post-claim outcomes

The legal object is more local: a state license, a carrier, a policy or bond form, a notice rule, a claim process, and a repayment obligation.  That difference matters at the moment of failure. Two tenants with the same rent and the same product name may not have the same right to challenge a denial, the same kind of insurer behind the promise, or the same duty after the provider pays the landlord. A lower quote can be a worse deal if it carries narrower protection or a larger debt path.  The state is therefore not background paperwork. It helps decide whether the product acts like housing support, landlord-risk protection, or a new way for a tenant to finance a security deposit and later loss.  The key split is between the landlord’s protected loss and the tenant’s remaining obligation. If the provider pays the landlord after missed rent or damage, the tenant may still owe the provider, face collection, or lose the chance to renew. A guarantee can prevent an immediate eviction while creating a later credit or debt problem. Both results must be followed.

### 47. Rent aid can buy time; a lawyer can change the case

**Theme:** Cost / housing / voice
**Route:** rent shock → aid or counsel → housing case
**Still open:** lasting housing, credit, work, and health effects

Rent trouble has two separate parts. One is a money gap: rent is due and cash is missing. The other is a power gap: a landlord can file, a court can issue a judgment, and a tenant may not know how to answer. Cash addresses the first. Legal help can change the second.  That is why a rent payment is not automatically a housing victory. It may stop one missed payment while leaving the next month, the court record, the lease, and the tenant's bargaining position unchanged. A proper result asks whether the household stayed housed after the immediate crisis.  The difference is practical, not academic. A payment may keep the tenant in the apartment long enough to work more hours, find a new job, or arrange a move. It may also leave a balance that the tenant cannot repay. A lawyer may stop a judgment but not make the rent affordable. A landlord payment plan may prevent court while turning one missed month into a larger bill spread across the next six months. The same intervention can buy time and create a later trap.

### 48. The person paying the bill may not control the fix

**Theme:** Energy / housing / cost
**Route:** renter bill → landlord control → upgrade or delay
**Still open:** bill savings, comfort, rent, and housing stability

Renters often pay for energy while landlords control major building and appliance upgrades. A tenant can see the high bill every month and still be unable to replace a window, improve insulation, or change the heating system. The landlord may pay for the work while the tenant receives the savings, so the repair can be delayed even when it would help both people.  This is called a split incentive, but the plain problem is a split between cost and control. A household solution has to ask three questions: who pays the bill now, who can change the home, and who keeps the saving afterward? If an upgrade raises rent, creates a new on-bill charge, or leads to a move, a lower energy bill alone is not enough to show success.

### 49. Repeated help is a signal, not yet a diagnosis

**Theme:** Energy / cost / housing / public power
**Route:** first crisis → intervention → next bill and repeat need
**Still open:** cause of repeat need and the fix that lasts

A second energy crisis can mean several different things: a short income gap, a benefit that was too small or too early, a home that wastes energy, broken equipment, a landlord who controls the repair, or a bill that stays too high. HHS already separates energy burden, restoration, and prevention in its LIHEAP performance system, which is useful. But public totals do not yet show the same household's path from first aid to next crisis. The deeper finding is that repeat assistance should open a case review, not be counted as proof that the program failed or that the household failed.  The interval between requests matters. A second application two weeks later may mean the first payment was too small, the utility applied it to the wrong charge, or the household never regained service. A second application after a hot summer may point to a broken air conditioner or a home that cannot keep heat out. A second application after a job loss may be a new income shock. “Repeat” is a time pattern, not a diagnosis.  Energy aid also has two different jobs. It can keep a service from being shut off or restore it after a shutoff. It can lower the future bill by fixing equipment or the home. The first job protects the next few days; the second may protect the next season. A program can do the first well and still leave the household exposed to the same bill later. The records need to say which job was done.

### 50. Replacement is a second price after repair fails

**Theme:** Cost / customer / energy
**Route:** repair failure → replacement → waste or reuse
**Still open:** replacement timing, household burden, and disposal

Replacement can look like an ordinary customer choice while hiding a forced decision. When repair is too costly, too slow, or unavailable, the household pays again to recover a function it already bought. The old product then enters a second system: storage, resale, collection, recycling, or disposal.  The cost is split. The household pays the new purchase, interest, lost time, and sometimes a collection fee. The local waste system handles the old product. Batteries and electronics can carry safety risks or valuable materials. Repair access and waste policy are therefore part of the same household story.  The deeper finding is that replacement is often a liquidity event disguised as a shopping event. The new item restores work, care, or communication, but the household may lose the emergency cash that would have handled rent, food, or a medical bill.  The timing makes the choice look voluntary. A product fails on a day when the job, school, or care duty cannot wait. The household then compares the repair quote with the cost of being without the product, not with the product’s original price. A replacement can be the rational choice and still be a forced use of cash or credit.

### 51. The cost of a bad choice begins after the review

**Theme:** Customer / cost / voice
**Route:** review and search → purchase → return or complaint
**Still open:** joined post-purchase quality and remedy

Reviews and search reduce the work of choosing. The real customer cost appears after purchase if the product is wrong, late, poor quality, or hard to return. A low price is not a low-cost choice if the buyer pays return shipping, loses work time, waits for a refund, or buys the product again.  This makes trust a two-part question. Did the information help the customer choose? And, when the choice failed, could the customer recover money and time? A platform that measures only the sale sees success where the customer may be carrying the loss.  The deeper finding is that a review system can move risk forward without reducing it. It helps a buyer decide, but the buyer may still carry the cost of bad quality, weak seller support, and a slow remedy. The quality of the return path is part of the product.  The review arrives at the wrong point in the story if it is treated as the final measure. Before purchase, it is a forecast made from other customers’ experience. After purchase, the customer learns whether the item matched the description and whether the seller will fix the problem. The useful question is whether the early signal predicts the later outcome.

### 52. Repair access is part of a product’s real price

**Theme:** Cost / customer / work
**Route:** product break → repair access → household choice
**Still open:** repair price, quality, time, and replacement

The price of a product includes what happens when it breaks. A phone, car, appliance, or work tool can be cheap to buy and expensive to keep alive. If parts, tools, repair information, software access, or a usable warranty are unavailable, a failure can become a forced replacement or a long period without the product.  Safety and quality are real reasons to control some repairs. The customer still needs a fair, workable choice: an authorized shop, an independent shop, a part, a manual, a warranty claim, or a replacement with a clear price. Without that choice, the purchase price hides a future cost and shifts risk toward people with the least cash to replace the item.  The deeper finding is that repair access is financial slack. A repairable product gives a household time to absorb a shock. A product that can only be replaced turns a small failure into a second purchase, debt, missed work, or lost care.  This is a timing issue as much as a price issue. A $150 repair due today can be harder than a $400 replacement paid next month, while a two-week wait for a work tool can cost more than either option. The real product price is the money and time needed to restore its use before the next job, meal, trip, or care duty.

### 53. A safe temperature can compete with food and medicine

**Theme:** Energy / health / cost / housing
**Route:** energy cost or equipment failure → unsafe temperature or sacrifice
**Still open:** same-household health, work, and housing recovery

Energy insecurity is not simply an unpaid bill. It is the moment a household has to decide which basic need can wait.  The preliminary 2024 EIA survey counts several breaks: a household leaves home at an unhealthy temperature, gives up food or medicine to pay energy costs, receives a disconnect notice, or cannot use heating or cooling equipment. These categories can overlap, but they describe different losses. A family may keep the electricity on by skipping medicine. Another may pay the bill and still live with a broken air conditioner. A third may receive a shutoff notice before service actually stops.  This distinction matters because the fix is different in each case. A payment can prevent a shutoff. It cannot repair a furnace. A weatherization job can improve a building. It may arrive too late for a household facing a bill today. Calling all of these cases “energy burden” makes the problem sound smaller and the response less precise.  The same dollar bill can create different risks depending on the home. A well-insulated apartment with working equipment may need less energy than an older house with leaks. A renter may not be allowed to replace a window unit or fix insulation. A person using medical equipment may need power even when the bill is high. The bill is real, but it is only one part of the exposure.

### 54. A benefit can be lost without the need going away

**Theme:** Cost / time / voice
**Route:** need → rule and access → benefit kept or lost
**Still open:** same-household food, work, debt, health, and trust effects

Eligibility is not access. A household can qualify for food or health help and still lose it because a form was late, an office closed, a notice was missed, or the proof demanded more time than the family had. A falling caseload can therefore mean higher earnings, a successful exit, or an administrative failure. The number alone cannot tell us which.  The key question is what happened after the benefit ended. Did work rise? Did food security improve? Or did the household buy less food, borrow, skip care, or return later? Exit is an event to explain, not a success label.  The deeper finding is that administration is part of the benefit. A program’s real value depends on the hours, travel, internet, language access, childcare, and attention required to receive it. A rule can be formally neutral and still remove more help from families with less spare time.  There are three different states that are often reported as one: a family is not eligible, a family is eligible but has not applied, and a family is enrolled but loses the benefit. Each points to a different problem. Income policy addresses the first. Outreach and trust affect the second. Notice, renewal, verification, and office design affect the third. A caseload number cannot separate them.

### 55. A recovery loan can keep a local option open—and create another bill

**Theme:** Work / cost / place
**Route:** disaster → recovery credit → firm and local options
**Still open:** worker, customer, household, and repayment effects

After a disaster, a small firm may need cash before customers return or insurance pays. A recovery loan can keep the firm open, protect jobs, and preserve a local service. But survival is not the same as recovery. The loan creates repayment risk, and a firm that stays open may still cut hours, raise prices, delay repairs, or weaken the owner’s household.  The public question is therefore bigger than the number of firms that avoided closure. Did money arrive soon enough? Did workers keep good jobs? Did customers keep a useful service at a fair price? Did the owner household remain stable while repaying the debt?  The deeper finding is that a loan can protect a place by moving part of the disaster into the future. That can be wise public support when the firm is viable. It can also postpone failure if demand, insurance, or infrastructure never returns.  There are two clocks in a recovery. The firm must pay wages, rent, utilities, and suppliers now, while the benefit of repairs may arrive months later. A loan fills that gap only if the business can earn enough after reopening to cover both ordinary costs and the new payment. “Open” is an early status, not the final result.

### 56. When a wider small-business rule crowds out the smallest firms

**Theme:** Work / cost / voice
**Route:** eligibility rule → public demand → firm survival
**Still open:** customer, worker, and owner-household effects

When a public program lets larger firms qualify as “small,” it can reach more firms while leaving less room for the very smallest ones. A larger company may have more staff, cash, bidding experience, and delivery capacity. It can win public work that once helped a young or local firm grow. The eligible list gets wider, but the market opportunity may become more concentrated.  The question is not whether larger firms can deliver value. It is what the program is trying to buy: low price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, innovation, or resilience. A rule can improve one goal and damage another. “More firms qualify” is not a result until we know which firms received the demand and what customers and workers received afterward.  Size is also a rough measure of capacity. Two firms with the same number of employees may have different cash, equipment, owners, contracts, and ability to survive a late government payment. A firm just below the cutoff may be growing quickly; a firm just above it may be old but fragile. A single line is easy to administer, but it can create a sharp change in opportunity where the underlying businesses are not sharply different.

### 57. A tariff reaches the household through a chain, not one price

**Theme:** Cost / customer / voice / work
**Route:** trade rule → import cost → retail price → household choice
**Still open:** firm margins, household exposure, and political response

A tariff does not move straight from a government announcement to one checkout price. There are at least five steps: the rule announced, the duty actually paid, the importer’s cost, the firm’s price and product choices, and the household’s response.  Recent US evidence shows much of the cost reached importers, while retail effects arrived more slowly and unevenly. Firms and households also changed behavior: they shifted suppliers, cut quantities, traded down, or stopped buying. The political meaning of that chain is a separate question.  The deeper finding is that policy can be felt long after it is announced and through a product different from the one first affected. A tariff may show up as a higher retail price, a smaller package, a missing option, a delayed purchase, or a firm’s changed wage or supplier decision.  The delay is not just an accounting detail. A retailer may have bought inventory before the duty, a supplier may have fixed a price for a season, and a firm may wait before changing a shelf label. A customer can therefore feel no immediate effect, then face a rise when the old stock is gone. The policy date, import date, price date, and purchase date are different clocks.

### 58. The same dollar can do different work depending on how it arrives

**Theme:** Cost / time / voice
**Route:** aid form → spending choice → household security
**Still open:** food security, care, trust, and long-run security

A dollar of public aid is not experienced as the same dollar when it is food or cash, or when it arrives once or every month. NBER finds larger short-run food-store spending responses from in-kind and recurring aid. A separate US cash experiment found more spending on housing, food, and cars, but little average change in net worth.  The deeper finding is about timing and choice. A label may protect food spending. Cash may cover rent, medicine, transport, or a repair. Recurring aid may help planning. None of these short-run responses alone proves lasting household security.  The form of aid can change who controls the household decision. A food benefit protects one category but leaves the family to find cash for rent, utilities, or transport. Cash allows the family to rank its own emergencies, but it can be harder for a program to defend politically or easier for a landlord, lender, or other creditor to claim indirectly. A restricted benefit is not simply less flexible; it directs the money toward one risk and leaves another risk exposed.  Timing changes the value again. A payment at the start of the month may cover rent, while the same payment near the end may cover groceries after rent has already consumed the cash. A one-time payment can solve a sudden repair but does little for a continuing rent gap. Recurring aid can make planning possible even when the monthly amount is small. The household experiences the schedule as part of the benefit.

### 59. A manageable yearly cost can still fail at the due date

**Theme:** Energy / cost / finance / housing
**Route:** rate design and season → monthly bill → cash gap or arrears
**Still open:** whether timing or structure causes the break

An annual energy average can hide the month that breaks a household. EIA shows that residential use and bills move with weather and that fixed bill components become a larger share when use falls. EIA also publishes average monthly bills by place, while GAO shows that utility costs can still make assisted renters rent-burdened. EIA's household survey records the final breaks: disconnect notices, unsafe temperatures, and unusable equipment. The deeper finding is that affordability is not only the price of energy; it is the bill that arrives, the money available that week, and the cost of falling behind.  The due date is part of the price. A household paid weekly may receive a large bill just before the next check, while another household has savings or a monthly pay schedule that matches it. A late fee, deposit, or reconnection charge can turn a short timing gap into a larger balance. Budget billing can make payments predictable, but it may also hide a true-up when actual use exceeds the estimate. The same rate can therefore produce different risk depending on the calendar.

### 60. An old utility balance can make the next move more expensive

**Theme:** Energy / cost / finance / housing
**Route:** old utility debt → collections or utility record → next deposit or connection
**Still open:** whether the old balance changes the next move

An unpaid utility bill does not necessarily end when a household leaves an address. It can become part of the next move.  The first problem is simple: the household owes money for power, gas, water, or telephone service already used. If the bill is not paid, the utility may send it to a collection agency. The second problem begins later: when the household applies for service at a new address, a utility may review past utility payment history and ask for a deposit or a payment arrangement.  That creates a two-part penalty. The old balance can damage the household's credit record, while the next utility account can require cash up front. The same household may therefore need money for a housing deposit, moving costs, and a utility deposit at the same time.  This is not a claim that every unpaid utility bill blocks a move. The sources do not show how often that happens. The point is that the debt has a path beyond the old meter, and that path is easy to miss if we look only at the original bill.  Moving can make the timing worse. A household may need a new utility account before the old bill is settled, while also paying a housing deposit, movers, transport, and the first month’s rent. The old debt and the new deposit can arrive in the same week. A rule that treats each account separately can create one large cash barrier for the household.

### 61. The cost of entering utility service can become a credit gate

The deeper finding is that access to basic service can depend on a record that is mostly invisible when positive and visible when negative.  The deposit arrives at a difficult moment. A household may be moving because of eviction, a job change, family separation, or a safer home. It may already owe a security deposit, first month's rent, moving costs, and an application fee. ### 62. When a smoother utility bill moves the problem instead of ending it

**Theme:** Energy / cost / finance / voice
**Route:** payment plan → smoother bill → true-up or stable service
**Still open:** whether smoothing lowers burden or delays it

Budget billing changes when a household pays, not necessarily how much it owes. A utility estimates seasonal use, divides the expected cost into monthly payments, and later adjusts for the difference between estimated and actual use. That can help a family with steady income avoid a winter or summer spike.  But a smaller monthly number can hide a running balance. If use is higher than expected, the household may reach a true-up owing more just when another bill arrives. An automatic withdrawal can also land before payday or alongside rent, food, and medicine. The fair question is not whether the monthly payment is lower. It is whether the total cost, balance, and next adjustment remain manageable.  The plan can also change what the customer thinks has happened. A lower payment may feel like relief, even when it is only a different schedule for the same cost. If the account does not show actual use, estimated use, and the balance in plain view, the customer cannot tell whether the plan is working or merely postponing the bill. A surprise true-up is not only a money problem; it is a breakdown in the customer's ability to plan.

**Theme:** Energy / health / cost
**Route:** weatherization → actual savings → comfort and health
**Still open:** long-run household, work, and health outcomes

Weatherization can lower energy use and improve safety and comfort. But projected savings can overstate what a household actually receives. A badly targeted or delayed upgrade can leave the family paying the same risk in a different form.  The work also has a time cost. A household may wait through a winter, move before the contractor arrives, or take time off for an inspection and repair. A tenant may qualify but lose the lease before the owner approves the work. A retrofit that is free at the point of service can still be hard to reach if the family cannot wait or cannot control the building.
