# Rotation FU — public integrity, procurement and visible citizen value

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question
**Research lane:** governance, economic, social, geographical and geopolitical systems

## Why this rotation exists

Anti-corruption work is often measured through laws, strategies, e-procurement,
audits, red flags or prosecutions. These are necessary signals, but citizens
experience integrity through whether a school, hospital, road, water system,
defence service or benefit is delivered at the promised quality and price, and
whether an affected bidder, worker or resident can challenge abuse. The gap
between clean procedure and usable public value is the central conversion.

## Research question

When a state, public enterprise, donor or contractor introduces procurement
controls, beneficial-ownership disclosure, e-procurement, audit, whistleblowing
or anti-corruption enforcement, under what conditions does it produce fair
competition, competent delivery, safe public goods, citizen trust and effective
remedy rather than compliance theatre, retaliation or displacement of risk?

## Conversion chain

```text
rule / tender / ownership disclosure / red flag / audit / complaint / sanction
  -> procurement and delivery decision
  -> supplier competition, price, quality, time, workforce and oversight
  -> named public good, service, burden, remedy or exclusion
  -> trust, legitimacy, voice, local benefit or geopolitical confidence
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD's Anti-Corruption and Integrity Outlook 2026 identifies procurement as
   a core government function and reports that legal integrity provisions are
   generally established in OECD members and partners, while supplier
   integrity, targeted tools and digital risk management remain underdeveloped.
2. OECD notes that procurement accounts for approximately 13% of GDP in OECD
   countries and is exposed to risks before tendering, during evaluation and
   after award. Single bidding, non-competitive procedures and contract
   modifications can be useful red flags, but they have multiple possible
   causes and require contextual investigation.
3. OECD links integrity failures to higher prices, lower quality, delay,
   reduced competition and public-safety risk, while noting that only a small
   share of OECD countries measure the effect on citizen trust. Public value
   cannot be inferred from procurement compliance alone.
4. UNCAC Article 9 and the UNODC implementation-review material frame public
   procurement around transparency, competition, objective criteria, records
   and challenge mechanisms. Reported reforms include e-procurement, tender
   publication and conflict-of-interest controls, but implementation varies by
   country and sector.
5. The World Bank uses beneficial-ownership disclosure in certain internationally
   competed procurements and publishes the information through contract award
   notices. Disclosure can reveal control and conflict pathways, but it does not
   prove collusion was prevented or that the final service was delivered.
6. OECD and World Bank material emphasizes that corruption and collusion can
   reinforce each other, while red-flag indicators can also reflect limited
   supplier pools, geography, restrictive specifications or low administrative
   capacity. Treating a flag as proof creates false positives and weakens fair
   enforcement.

## Bounded proposition

**An anti-corruption law, procurement code, e-tendering system, beneficial-
ownership register, audit, red-flag dashboard, integrity certification,
whistleblower channel, prosecution or contract cancellation is not by itself
fair competition, competent delivery, safe public goods, affordable services,
local benefit, citizen trust or effective remedy.** A published tender, clean
audit, risk score, sanction or award does not establish that named residents
received the promised road, medicine, school, water, housing or service at fair
quality and price, that workers were protected, or that retaliation and
recurrence were prevented.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

- **Visible value:** people judge integrity through usable services, not only
  procurement records; unfinished or inaccessible infrastructure can undermine
  legitimacy even when procedure appears compliant.
- **Local and peripheral geography:** remote areas may have few suppliers and
  higher delivery costs; a single bid can be a capacity constraint or a risk,
  not automatic proof of corruption.
- **Informal power:** connections, subcontracting, political finance, customary
  authority and administrative discretion can shape outcomes beyond the formal
  tender.
- **Voice and remedy:** bidders, workers, whistleblowers and residents face
  different risks; a complaint mechanism without confidentiality, response and
  protection may be unusable.
- **Gender and social inclusion:** procurement can influence women-owned firms,
  minority suppliers, local employment and who receives public goods, but
  aggregate contract value hides distribution.
- **Geopolitical confidence:** transparent procurement can support donor,
  alliance and investor confidence, while external conditionality can also
  displace local priorities or create dependency.

## Counterexamples and cautions

1. A single-bid contract can reflect a thin market, remote geography or
   specialized need rather than corruption.
2. An open tender can still produce collusion, tailored specifications,
   beneficial-owner concealment or poor post-award supervision.
3. An audit finding may identify a control failure without proving loss, intent,
   service harm or a responsible person.
4. Digital procurement can improve traceability while excluding small suppliers
   lacking connectivity, language access or technical capacity.
5. A prosecution or cancellation can signal accountability while delaying the
   medicine, road or service people need.
6. Beneficial-ownership data can be incomplete, outdated or difficult for
   citizens and investigators to interpret.
7. Public participation can be symbolic unless comments change specifications,
   awards, delivery, monitoring or remedy.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote the Tier 1 distinction that integrity controls are necessary but do not
close the delivery-to-citizen-value arrow. Keep the following as Tier 2:
**public-integrity-to-fair-delivery-and-citizen-trust conversion**. The next
evidence must name the procurement, supplier, beneficial owner, delivery unit,
affected group, price/quality/time result, complaint, response and recurrence.

## Next test

Build a sector-level procurement ledger for one place—health supplies, water,
schools or transport. Link tender, bidders, ownership, red flags, award,
subcontract, payment, inspection, delivery, price, quality, worker and resident
outcomes, complaints, enforcement and later performance. Compare a flagged and
unflagged procurement and include a cancelled or failed project.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What is currently supported | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | laws, strategies, tender records, red flags, audits, ownership and sanctions | local salience and under-detection |
| Mechanism | competition, conflict, disclosure, supervision, complaint and enforcement channels | informal power, retaliation and supplier capacity |
| Operation | procurement, supplier, award, contract, project and complaint can be named | post-award delivery and repeated performance |
| Material consequence | price, quality, delay, safety, service and public-resource effects are relevant | named citizen receipt, affordability, inclusion and recurrence |
| Meaning | fairness, trust, dignity, legitimacy and local benefit are central | resident attribution and durable confidence |
| Power | competition, challenge, exit, sanction and public scrutiny alter leverage | sustained accountability, substitution and geopolitical effect |

## Source boundary

The packet closes discovery and framing for public integrity and procurement.
Public sources support procurement risk, red-flag limits, transparency,
beneficial ownership, review mechanisms and citizen-value concerns. They do not
by themselves establish a named public good’s quality, a resident’s receipt,
fair competition, protection from retaliation or durable trust.
