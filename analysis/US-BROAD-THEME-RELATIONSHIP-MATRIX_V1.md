# US broad theme relationship matrix v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-25  
**Purpose:** map the major outward relationships among the 14 research themes.

This is a mechanism map, not a claim that the themes form one causal chain or
describe the same people. “Evidence status” means the relationship has a
bounded source comparison or synthesis; it does not mean the whole path is
proven.

## The strongest recurring relationship clusters

### Material life ↔ culture and politics

Themes 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 11 form the household-to-public-meaning
cluster. The evidence repeatedly shows that money, time, care, health, place,
identity, trust, and political action are related but non-interchangeable
currencies. The current atlas can compare them across sources; it rarely follows
one dated episode through all of them.

### Consumer systems ↔ institutional power

Themes 3, 4, 9, 10, and 12 form the participation-and-recourse cluster. A
person's ability to search, buy, share, report, appeal, recover, switch, or
leave is shaped by platform design, firm power, public rules, and social status.
The visible record is often a complaint, transaction, or response label rather
than a restored outcome.

### Work ↔ care ↔ household security

Themes 1, 2, 5, and 6 form the social-reproduction cluster. Work schedules,
health, paid and unpaid care, benefits, and household room move together in
real life but are usually measured by separate sources and clocks. This is where
the project must resist calling a population comparison a same-family chain.

### Place ↔ firm capacity ↔ state capability

Themes 7, 10, 12, 13, and 14 form the capacity-and-dependence cluster. A place
can receive firms, projects, public money, or technical infrastructure without
showing affordable service, local control, durable jobs, replaceability, or
strategic autonomy.

### Identity ↔ legitimacy ↔ geopolitical room

Themes 8, 9, 11, and 14 form the meaning-and-legitimacy cluster. Domestic and
foreign audiences can see the same capability, migration change, public rule,
or cultural shift differently. Political identity and public legitimacy are
conversion stages, not automatic consequences of material conditions.

### Realization clocks across every cluster

The clusters also share a cross-cutting timing problem. An announcement,
allocation, contract, correction, payment, or identity change can be visible
before actual use, adequate protection, durable capability, belonging, trust,
or external response. This overlay links themes 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 12, 13, and
14 especially, but it also appears in household room and political action:

```text
formal offer or capacity
  -> implementation or reported receipt
  -> actual use and adequacy
  -> durable protection/capability
  -> meaning, trust, action, switching, exit, or external response
```

The “middle” is not one universal stage or a pooled estimate. It is a control
question for each source: identify who acts, what unit is held constant, which
clock is being measured, who carries delay or residual risk, and what evidence
would show conversion to a protected outcome. A green administrative status,
firm-reported correction, worker time saving, domestic supplier share, or public
representation should not be promoted across those stages without that test.

The new CEV layer adds a civic-capacity timing distinction: formal volunteering
and informal neighbor help can recover in reach while hours per formal volunteer
fall. This connects theme 2 (time as a hidden price) to themes 9 and 11 without
claiming that shorter service causes weaker solidarity or that more volunteers
produce more political power. Organizational reach, time intensity, online or
in-person mode, efficacy, and later collective action must remain separate.

The latest breadth additions sharpen four more cross-theme boundaries. Poverty
improvement and Medicaid continuity connect themes 1, 6, 8, and 10 while
remaining separate population clocks. Median income and percentile movement
connect themes 1, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 without treating the midpoint as the
representative household. Multigenerational living and paid/unpaid care connect
themes 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 by showing protection and burden transfer in the
same social arrangement. Finally, neighbor goodwill and expected reciprocity
connect themes 2, 9, 10, 11, and 14: willingness to help is not delivered,
adequate, or durable protection. These are relationship mechanisms, not pooled
effects or closed same-unit chains.

The care-capacity evidence adds an upstream constraint to the work–care–household
cluster: a subsidy, fee discount, or worker incentive can be real while a
family still cannot obtain a dependable slot. The atlas should therefore keep
provider recruitment, retention, staffing, waitlist movement, price, quality,
family use, and parent work outcomes as separate stages.

The low-density infrastructure and geopolitical rotation adds two additional
controls. First, domestic investment or procurement should be followed at the
application level through qualified output, maintenance, supplier switching,
and an observed external response before it is called autonomy or leverage.
Second, broad alliance approval should be separated from informed consent for a
named deployment: host-country approval, mission knowledge, exercise burden,
and support for future use may move differently. These are distinct clocks,
not one legitimacy score.

## How to use the matrix

## New cross-cutting overlay: unequal replaceability

The [dependence, control, and leverage synthesis](US-BROAD-DEPENDENCE-CONTROL-AND-LEVERAGE-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
adds a relationship that cuts across themes 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 13, and 14.
Workers, consumers, communities, firms, and states may all face a formal
alternative whose practical cost is unevenly distributed. This is a shared
mechanism, not a pooled outcome:

```text
dependence on a route, employer, platform, supplier, or infrastructure system
  -> cost of refusal, switching, delay, or non-use
  -> visibility of status and available alternatives
  -> bargaining, adaptation, contestation, or acceptance
  -> observed change in autonomy, trust, political meaning, or external leverage
```

| Relationship | New cross-source evidence | Safe interpretation | Required boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work ↔ firm power ↔ political action | Employer concentration, reported employer communication, and voting analysis | Job dependence can enter political meaning and behavior | The same worker's message, belief, vote, coercion, and welfare are not jointly observed |
| Consumer ↔ platform power ↔ exit | Search defaults, trial exposure, privacy controls, and connected-vehicle unenrollment routes | Alternatives and information can soften or intensify practical lock-in | Market share, a published control, or an unenrollment route is not durable exit or verified privacy |
| Infrastructure ↔ household/public cost ↔ firm power | PJM operating stress and Virginia large-load allocation/reliability rules | Dependence can produce operating and regulatory response before local incidence is visible | No named facility payment, household bill, feeder effect, or community acceptance is established |
| Domestic capacity ↔ geopolitical dependence ↔ state leverage | SIPRI U.S. arms-transfer concentration and allied supply exposure | Supplier concentration can create dependence and bargaining exposure | Transfer volume is not delivery, sustainment, readiness, obedience, deterrence, or leverage |

The matrix should therefore distinguish three stages in future records:

```text
dependence = exposure to the cost of refusal
control = ability to shape another actor's available route
leverage = observed change in that actor's behavior, decision, or autonomy
```

The strongest next records are attributed responses, corrections,
substitutions, refusals, accepted concessions, or durable changes—not another
concentration, adoption, capacity, or use-rate snapshot.

## New cross-cutting overlay: distribution-to-meaning conversion

The [distribution-to-attribution bridge](US-CROSS-SOURCE-TREND-SYNTHESIS_V1.md)
connects material, institutional, civic, and geopolitical themes while keeping
their units separate. It asks how an offered benefit, service, protection, or
capability is distributed and interpreted before treating it as legitimacy,
solidarity, dependence, or control:

```text
offer or capability
  -> receipt, burden, delay, visibility, alternatives, and response
  -> attribution and audience interpretation
  -> action, non-use, acceptance, resistance, or exit
  -> later trust, belonging, recovery, legitimacy, or external adaptation
```

| Relationship | What the bridge adds | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Household room ↔ public systems ↔ cultural meaning | Assistance, care, housing, or energy support may change a next-period condition for some units while others remain in hardship; the meaning of the system depends on reach, adequacy, explanation, and alternatives | SIPP, SHED, CIRCLE, CDC, and administrative records do not jointly observe one household’s receipt, attribution, trust, and political action |
| Consumer/platform control ↔ trust/action | A complaint route, remedy, privacy control, or social-media exposure becomes meaningful through status visibility, effort, correction, recovery, switching, and the user’s interpretation of responsibility | FTC, CFPB, platform experiments, and surveys observe different cases, users, and stages; formal remedy or reported loss is not durable trust or exit |
| Infrastructure/state capacity ↔ local legitimacy | A project, staffed service, operational response, or alliance capability can be publicly visible while burdens, usable access, local consent, or partner acceptance remain uneven | GAO, DOE, NATO, and host-government accounts establish selected capacity or official framing, not universal receipt, public consent, deterrence, or changed external behavior |
| Unequal exposure ↔ political judgment ↔ geopolitical power | Identity, place, status, and replaceability shape who can refuse, who can attribute a cost, and whose interpretation becomes institutionally visible | Cross-sectional subgroup contrasts and official narratives cannot establish a common audience, causal attribution, or a single legitimacy gradient |

The matrix should therefore label each future edge as one or more of:
**incidence**, **visibility**, **attribution**, **action**, **institutional
response**, or **verified outcome**. A relationship may be strong at one stage
and entirely open at the next; that is a result to preserve, not a gap to fill
with inference.

For a new source, identify:

1. its starting theme and unit;
2. the connected theme it might inform;
3. the mechanism between them;
4. whether the link is observed, reported, estimated, compared, inferred, or
   open; and
5. the smallest next record that could confirm, weaken, or reverse the link.
