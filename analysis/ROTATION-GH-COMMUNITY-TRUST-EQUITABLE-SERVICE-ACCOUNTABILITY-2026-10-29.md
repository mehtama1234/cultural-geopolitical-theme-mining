# Rotation GH — community trust, equitable service and accountability

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** social, cultural, institutional, geographical and civic systems

## Why this rotation exists

Trust is routinely treated as a score, a communications objective or a proxy
for institutional legitimacy. People experience trust through interactions:
whether a service is accessible, information is clear, treatment is fair,
complaints change delivery, data are handled legitimately and public decisions
reflect local voice. An institution can have high stated favourability while a
particular group faces delay, exclusion, corruption, surveillance or no remedy.
Conversely, a low aggregate score can conceal a trusted local service or a
specific relationship that is working.

## Research question

When a public institution, service provider or civic programme seeks to build
trust, under what conditions does improved accessibility, fairness, responsiveness,
voice, integrity and visible delivery produce named residents’ confidence and
cooperation rather than a survey shift, communication campaign or institutional
claim without changed experience or accountability?

## Conversion chain

```text
policy / institution / service / information / participation / complaint
  -> person encounters a fair, accessible and understandable process
  -> response, correction, delivery, remedy or visible public value
  -> perceived reliability, dignity, voice and procedural fairness
  -> trust, cooperation, legitimacy, accountability or constructive refusal
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. OECD's 2025 Government at a Glance framework identifies reliability,
   responsiveness, fairness, integrity and openness as governance drivers of
   trust, while individual and group-based cultural and socioeconomic factors
   also shape trust. Trust is therefore multi-causal and not a universal index
   of service performance.
2. OECD's 2025 trust survey reports that satisfaction with administrative
   services is an important driver of trust, and that clarity, accessibility,
   fair treatment and preferred-channel access matter in day-to-day encounters.
   In 2025, 53% across OECD countries considered fair treatment of a benefit or
   service application likely; this is an expectation measure, not proof of
   actual fairness for every group.
3. OECD reports that only 39% across OECD countries trust their national
   government, and about four in ten believe innovative ideas or complaints
   will be reflected in public-service adjustments. Low expectations about
   responsiveness are a governance mechanism, not merely a communications gap.
4. World Bank's 2025 GovTech Maturity Index tracks 48 indicators across core
   systems, online services, digital citizen engagement and enablers. Platform
   maturity and digital-service adoption are useful capacity signals, but do
   not establish equitable offline experience, trust or remedy.
5. UNDP's 2025 SDG 16 progress report emphasizes experience-based surveys,
   legal aid, public legal education, community justice, transparent budgeting,
   digital service delivery and whistle-blower protection as accountability
   pathways. Institutional reform must be assessed through affected groups and
   actual experience, not only formal adoption.
6. OECD's 2026 trust results report an average of 40% with high or moderately
   high trust in national government across OECD countries, while low or no
   trust exceeds high trust. This reinforces the need to separate aggregate
   confidence from local, group-specific and service-specific relationships.

## Bounded proposition

**A trust survey, favourability score, consultation, transparency portal,
service standard, digital platform, integrity policy, complaint channel or
public engagement campaign is not by itself fair treatment, reliable delivery,
voice, accountability, remedy, cooperation or legitimate public authority.** A
reported confidence level, participation event, resolved complaint or service
transaction does not establish that named people experienced accessible and
respectful treatment, saw correction, received visible value, could influence
decisions or would trust the institution under future uncertainty.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Trust is relational and situated. The same institution can be experienced as
reliable by a connected urban resident and inaccessible by a rural, migrant,
disabled, minority-language or low-income person. Historical discrimination,
corruption, conflict, gendered safety and data misuse shape whether a new
service or consultation is interpreted as opportunity or risk. Local
intermediaries can translate and repair institutional relationships, but can
also filter voice or reproduce exclusion. Trust should therefore be measured
through named encounters, group differences, place, time and the possibility of
safe refusal—not only through national averages.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Trust can rise after better service, but it can also rise through partisan
  identity, fear, short-term transfers or a temporary crisis; attribution needs
  evidence.
- Satisfaction is not trust, and trust is not compliance, cooperation or
  legitimacy; these clocks should remain separate.
- A complaint channel can improve accountability when complaints change
  decisions, but logging complaints without response can reduce trust.
- Digital engagement can expand voice while excluding people without devices,
  language access, time, safety or confidence to participate.
- Transparent information does not automatically create fairness if people
  cannot understand, contest or change the decision.
- Aggregate trust can improve while a subgroup suffers unequal treatment;
  disaggregate by place, identity, service and experience.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: trust is an outcome of repeated, situated
interactions involving fairness, responsiveness, integrity, voice and visible
delivery. Keep the named service-to-trust-and-accountability chain as **Tier 2**
until longitudinal, subgroup and encounter-level evidence demonstrates change.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | trust score, satisfaction, consultation, policy, service or complaint | strong OECD, World Bank and UNDP evidence |
| Mechanism | access, clarity, fairness, reliability, integrity, voice, response and data use | strong bounded governance evidence |
| Operation | named person uses service, participates, complains, receives response or remedy | partial; survey and platform evidence exist |
| Material consequence | service receipt, correction, remedy, resource allocation or reduced burden | not closed for a general trust claim |
| Meaning | dignity, belonging, confidence, legitimacy and willingness to cooperate | not closed; requires situated qualitative follow-up |
| Power | agenda influence, institutional learning, refusal, accountability and durable legitimacy | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one public service and compare named user journeys across places and
groups. Measure access, wait, clarity, cost, fairness, respect, complaint,
response, correction, outcome and later willingness to use or cooperate. Pair
survey trust with administrative records, service observations and interviews;
test whether complaints or resident proposals changed policy, delivery or
resource allocation. Report aggregate and subgroup results separately.

## Source boundary

The public record closes trust drivers, service-satisfaction mechanisms,
participation, digital-capacity and accountability pathways. It does not justify
a universal claim that trust scores, portals, consultations or integrity reforms
produce fair service, remedy, cooperation or durable legitimacy for named
communities. Those later outcomes remain acquisition targets.
