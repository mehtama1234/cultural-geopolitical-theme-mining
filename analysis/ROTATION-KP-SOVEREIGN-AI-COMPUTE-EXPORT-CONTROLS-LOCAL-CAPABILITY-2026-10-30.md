# Rotation KP — Sovereign AI compute, export controls and local capability

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Follow-up to:** [HP AI compute controls and local capability rotation](ROTATION-HP-AI-COMPUTE-CONTROLS-LOCAL-CAPABILITY-GEOPOLITICAL-ACCESS-2026-10-30.md)  
**Research question:** When governments treat AI compute as strategic infrastructure and control access to advanced chips, does this create broad local capability and fair international participation—or consolidate power in a small number of firms, states, clouds, energy systems and export-control jurisdictions?

## Why this follow-up matters

Compute is increasingly described as a sovereign asset, but sovereignty has multiple meanings: ownership of hardware, ability to train or deploy models, access for universities and small firms, control over data and energy, resilience to supply disruption, and power to set standards. A national supercomputer or AI factory can close an investment or infrastructure stage without producing usable local capability.

```text
strategic AI goal / export-control risk / industrial policy
  -> chips, data centres, energy, cloud, network and skills
  -> allocation, licensing, access price, uptime and model-development opportunity
  -> local firm, researcher, public service or community uses compute
  -> productivity, scientific capacity, cultural/linguistic inclusion and bargaining power
  -> durable autonomy or new dependency
```

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| KP1 | OECD’s 2026 compute policy brief describes compute as a strategic asset and notes that countries pursue domestic capacity for local deployment, supply-chain security, lower latency and international participation. | Strategic rationale and policy instrument. | Operational access, affordability, local users, resilience or sovereignty outcomes. |
| KP2 | OECD reports compute access is a bottleneck for AI R&D and that national strategies increasingly name compute pillars, targets and budgets; 13 of 18 strategies adopted or updated in 2025–26 address compute as a dedicated pillar. | Policy diffusion and R&D bottleneck. | Whether targets are built, accessible, maintained or useful outside frontier firms. |
| KP3 | OECD identifies concentration across chips, cloud and infrastructure, including high fixed costs, vertical integration, barriers to entry and dependence on a small number of suppliers. | Market-structure and dependency mechanism. | Competition remedy, switching, open access, local procurement or user-level benefit. |
| KP4 | OECD’s AI-market analysis reports the three major cloud providers held 74% of global cloud market in 2023 and identifies compute, data, skills and energy as reinforcing concentration inputs. | Cross-layer concentration and compounding advantage. | Current causal effect on local innovation, public services or cultural inclusion. |
| KP5 | The EU’s AI Factories programme lists facilities and antennas across member and partner countries, while AI Gigafactories require over 100,000 advanced processors, reliable power, supply chains, networking and energy efficiency. | Regional infrastructure distribution and scale requirement. | Actual access rules, queueing, price, SME/research use, language coverage or community benefit. |
| KP6 | BIS revised 2026 policy to review certain advanced-chip exports to China case by case, conditioned on customer compliance, supply-capacity protection and independent performance/security testing. | Dynamic export-control and conditional-access mechanism. | Strategic stability, diversion prevention, Chinese or third-country capability, civilian spillovers or downstream users. |
| KP7 | OECD reports AI firms captured 61% of global venture-capital investment in 2025, with infrastructure and hosting receiving the largest volumes and U.S.-based firms attracting about 75% of global AI deal value. | Capital concentration and geopolitical investment geography. | Productive local capability, public return, labor effects or durable market contestability. |
| KP8 | OECD describes Brazil’s proposed roughly $1 billion national AI infrastructure programme and shared-access ambitions with Latin America, the Caribbean and Africa, illustrating a sovereignty-plus-cooperation model. | South–South access and shared infrastructure pathway. | Delivered capacity, governance, affordability, uptime, partner control or reduced dependency. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Compute sovereignty is relational. A country can own a facility and still depend on imported accelerators, foreign cloud software, external maintenance, overseas standards, subsidized power or a narrow set of customers. Conversely, a shared regional facility can provide more practical capability than nominal national ownership if access, pricing, data governance and skills are genuinely distributed.

The geography of AI is not just where data centres are built. It includes where electricity and water are allocated, where engineers are trained, where models are tuned to local languages and institutions, and who can afford inference. AI factories may spread hardware across a map while reproducing a hierarchy of model ownership and application dependence. The relevant local unit is a named university, startup, public agency, language community or service user with observable access and outcome.

Export controls add a geopolitical clock. A licensing rule can prevent diversion or protect military advantage, but it can also change research collaboration, cloud access, secondary markets, supply-chain investment and the bargaining power of countries outside the main blocs. The 2026 BIS case-by-case approach shows that controls are not static walls; they are negotiated conditions whose effectiveness depends on verification and market response.

Local capability also has a cultural dimension. Compute access without local data authority, language coverage or community governance may increase extraction rather than autonomy. A regional AI facility should therefore be evaluated by who can use it, under what terms, for which public or commercial purposes, and whether users can switch, audit, protect data and retain value.

## Counterexample and source boundary

An AI factory, national strategy, chip license, investment announcement, data-centre megawatt figure, cloud share or export-control rule is not operational sovereign capability, affordable access, resilience, innovation, public-service improvement or fair international participation. A facility can be built but underused; shared access can be nominal but rationed; export controls can reduce one risk while increasing dependency or fragmentation; and domestic compute can intensify local energy, water or fiscal burdens.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 deepened finding:** AI compute is now an infrastructure, market-power and geopolitical-control problem, not only a technical input. The evidence clearly separates strategic intent, investment, access architecture and market concentration from delivered local capability.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** **sovereign-compute-to-accessible-local-capability-resilience-and-reciprocal-international-autonomy**. The next acquisition should join one facility, cloud or export-control decision to hardware supply, power/water, licensing, queue/price, named user, model/data use, uptime, skills, local-language outcome, security event and later value retention, with a non-access or displaced-user comparator.
