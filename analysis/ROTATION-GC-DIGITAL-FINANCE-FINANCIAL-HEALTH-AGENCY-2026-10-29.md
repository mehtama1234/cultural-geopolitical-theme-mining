# Rotation GC — digital finance, financial health and user agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** technological, social, household, cultural and geographical systems

## Why this rotation exists

Digital-finance narratives often stop at account ownership, transaction volume,
mobile-phone penetration or access to credit. People experience finance through
the ability to make a payment, save, cope with a shock, understand a loan,
recover from fraud, protect privacy and retain control over household money.
Connectivity, device ownership and an account can expand options while also
creating exposure to scams, opaque fees, over-indebtedness, identity theft,
service outages and coercive household or agent relationships.

## Research question

When mobile money, digital payments, wallets, fintech credit, open finance,
personal-finance tools or digital social transfers expand access, under what
conditions do they improve a named person’s financial health and agency rather
than merely increase transactions, debt, surveillance, fraud exposure or
dependence on an inaccessible digital channel?

## Conversion chain

```text
phone / connectivity / account / payment / savings / credit / transfer
  -> user can authenticate, understand, transact and recover from error
  -> smooth daily management, shock coping, goal pursuit and secure choice
  -> household resilience, livelihood continuity and reduced financial stress
  -> agency, trust, bargaining power or refusal of harmful products
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The World Bank's Global Findex 2025 surveys about 148,000 adults in 141
   economies and adds globally comparable data on mobile phones, internet use
   and digital safety. It measures access and use, but those indicators do not
   by themselves establish financial health or agency.
2. Findex reports that 79% of adults globally own a financial account and 86%
   own a mobile phone, while gaps remain for women and poorer adults. The
   connectivity-account relationship is an opportunity and a possible new
   dependency, not a completed welfare outcome.
3. The IMF's 2025 Financial Access Survey reports digital transactions rising
   from 55 per adult in 2017 to 251 in 2024, but also highlights literacy,
   infrastructure, cost, over-indebtedness, fraud, identity theft and gender
   disparities. Transaction growth and financial health must therefore be kept
   as separate clocks.
4. CGAP's 2026 consumer-protection research reports widespread fraud exposure
   and links fraud, data misuse, downtime and lack of transparency to reduced
   use, switching and monetary loss. Its Peru and Rwanda examples show why
   consumer outcomes and recovery support matter more than adoption alone.
5. CGAP's 2026 India research on personal-finance-management tools explicitly
   treats financial health as the outcome question—managing daily needs,
   coping with shocks, pursuing goals and feeling confident—not merely having
   a product. It also raises questions about data aggregation and AI advice.
6. The IMF's digital-payment work in Sub-Saharan Africa stresses that cash
   access, infrastructure, literacy, trust, consumer protection, privacy,
   competition and rule of law remain necessary to prevent digital systems
   from excluding people who cannot safely use them.

## Bounded proposition

**A mobile phone, bank account, wallet, digital payment, fintech loan,
personal-finance app, social transfer, open-finance connection or financial-
inclusion target is not by itself financial health, resilience, affordability,
privacy, safety, household agency or livelihood continuity.** Account opening,
transaction growth, credit approval or app use does not establish that named
users understood costs, controlled the funds, avoided harmful debt, recovered
from fraud, received a service reliably or gained durable capacity to manage a
shock.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Digital finance is embedded in household, agent, language and place relations.
Women may own an account but lack private control over the phone or PIN; a
migrant worker may depend on a corridor with high remittance fees; a rural user
may face network or agent liquidity failure; an informal worker may have volatile
income that a scoring system reads as risk; an older or disabled person may need
human support. Cash is not simply an obsolete channel: it can provide privacy,
offline resilience and a way to avoid digital surveillance. Trust is built when
errors can be corrected, fraud victims receive help and users can switch or
refuse without losing essential services.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Digital finance can lower travel and transaction costs and help people save;
  the proposition is not that digitalization is harmful by definition.
- Account ownership or payment volume may coexist with dormant accounts,
  pass-through transfers, coercive use, debt stress or no meaningful savings.
- Credit can smooth a shock or deepen over-indebtedness; product purpose,
  pricing, income volatility and repayment consequences must be observed.
- Fraud exposure is not the same as fraud loss, and loss is not the same as
  successful complaint, reimbursement or restored trust.
- Financial-health self-reporting can capture confidence while missing hidden
  debt, household coercion or inability to meet essential expenses.
- Digital-only delivery can improve efficiency while excluding users through
  outages, inaccessible authentication, language, disability or lack of cash
  fallback.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: digital financial inclusion is a conversion
problem from connectivity and access to safe use, financial health and agency.
Keep the named user-to-resilience-and-control chain as **Tier 2** until
longitudinal user outcomes, loss recovery and subgroup differences are visible.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | phone, internet, account, payment, credit and transfer adoption | strong global supply and survey evidence |
| Mechanism | cost, literacy, device, connectivity, agent liquidity, privacy, fraud and recovery | strong bounded risk evidence |
| Operation | named user authenticates, pays, saves, borrows, receives or disputes | partial; user studies and surveys exist |
| Material consequence | expenses, savings, debt, loss, shock coping, income or household security | not closed for a general digital-finance claim |
| Meaning | confidence, control, dignity, trust and perceived choice | not closed; needs user-informed follow-up |
| Power | switching, refusal, bargaining power, data control and durable agency | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Follow named, anonymized users over six to twelve months across urban/rural,
gender, age, disability, language, income volatility and migration contexts.
Measure successful and failed transactions, fees, downtime, fraud attempts and
losses, complaint and reimbursement time, debt service, essential-expense
coverage, savings buffer, control over account decisions, cash fallback and
whether users can switch providers without losing benefits. Separate account
access, usage, financial health and agency measures.

## Source boundary

The public record closes connectivity, access, usage and consumer-risk
mechanisms. It does not establish a universal causal claim that digital
finance improves financial health, resilience, household agency or livelihood
continuity for named users. Those later outcomes remain acquisition targets.
