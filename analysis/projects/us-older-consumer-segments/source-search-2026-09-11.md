# Source search: US older consumers are not one market

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States consumer question; HBS comparison includes North America and Europe  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

How do health, wealth and independence change what older consumers need and buy?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HBS-OLDER-2026 | [HBS: What Brands Get Wrong About the Over-65 Market](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/what-brands-get-wrong-about-the-over-65-market) | HBS reports that older adults are a large and varied market, with segments differing in health and activity. It describes evidence that older adults can have high buying power, stronger repeat buying and influence on younger consumers, while companies often use age-based stereotypes. | Research and practice summary | The article combines market estimates, segmentation and advice; it does not show that better marketing improves older people's financial or health outcomes. |
| NBER-W30460 | [NBER: Explanations for the Decline in Spending at Older Ages](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30460) | US survey evidence finds that older adults often report greater economic satisfaction as spending declines, while health, widowhood and changing enjoyment may reduce the desire to spend. | Working paper using older-adult survey data | Lower spending can reflect choice, health, loss or limited resources; the study does not tell firms how to market products. |
| NBER-W27348 | [NBER: Old Age Risks, Consumption, and Insurance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27348) | US evidence finds that consumption responds to health shocks and that older households face income and health risks that affect how they use resources. | Working paper with US household data | It studies risk and consumption, not customer segments or marketing outcomes. |

## First pattern to test

```text
age, health, wealth and living situation
  -> product need, ability and willingness to pay
  -> firm design, service and targeting
  -> independence, care burden or exposure to bad advice
  -> household money and family decisions
```

The HBS evidence supports a market mismatch: age alone hides meaningful differences. NBER work supports different spending and risk paths, but does not show that a firm's targeting caused better outcomes.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Older customers may value simplicity, trust and continuity. Segmenting them more finely can improve fit, but it can also lead to higher prices, exclusion or pressure to buy products they do not need.

## Main gaps

- outcomes by age, health, wealth and living arrangement together;
- customer service, prices and product access;
- digital access and disability;
- influence on family purchases and care decisions;
- financial scams, insurance and medical costs;
- whether age-friendly design improves independence.

## Decision rule

Use age as a starting point, not a conclusion. Match customer data with health, financial and service outcomes before calling a market strategy helpful.
