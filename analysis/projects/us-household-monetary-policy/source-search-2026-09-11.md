# Source search: US households and the meaning of an interest-rate change

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

How do households translate a central-bank rate change into a decision about spending, saving or borrowing?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NBER-W35127 | [NBER: Monetary Policy According to Households](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35127) | A survey of more than 25,000 US households with randomized information treatments finds that people expect higher rates to raise borrowing costs and inflation. They report reducing spending, especially on durable goods, and inflation expectations help explain portfolio changes. | Working paper with large survey and experiments | Reported beliefs are not the same as actual spending, debt or portfolio records; the paper's mechanisms may differ across households. |
| NBER-W35090 | [NBER: Information Treatments, Hypotheticals, and Event Studies](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35090) | The August 2026 paper compares hypothetical, randomized-information, and actual-announcement event-study responses around the September 2025 Federal Reserve rate cut. It reports 4,067 valid vignette observations, 1,308 recontacted RCT respondents, and 6,968 pooled event-study observations; one-year inflation expectations fall by about 2.04 pp in the 25 bp vignette versus no change, 0.83 pp in the full RCT, and 0.72 pp in the balanced panel, with larger responses among previously unaware or correctly informed respondents. | Full-paper extraction promoted; pre-registration alignment and action extension remain open | The designs estimate different reduced-form expectation responses, not a structural shock or realized spending/debt/portfolio effect; event-study exposure is not randomized. |
| NBER-W26778 | [NBER: Forward Guidance and Household Expectations](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26778) | A randomized US survey finds that information about current and next-year rates changes household expectations, while information about rates beyond one year adds little. | Randomized survey study | Expectations do not always become action, and the study predates the newer rate cycle. |
| NBER-W25482 | [NBER: Monetary Policy Communications and Their Effects on Household Inflation Expectations](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25482) | In a randomized study of nearly 20,000 US individuals, simple information about the Federal Reserve's inflation target changes inflation expectations and affects later spending. | Randomized survey study | The information setting is controlled and does not measure every channel of public communication. |

## First pattern to test

```text
rate decision and public message
  -> household belief about prices, jobs and borrowing
  -> spending, saving, debt or portfolio choice
  -> different burden by balance sheet and income
  -> views of the Fed, banks and the economy
```

The NBER evidence supports a household expectations channel. It does not show that every rate increase reduces spending in the same way, or that households understand the policy's intended mechanism.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Higher rates can help savers, lower some asset prices and slow demand. A household with deposits may gain while a household with a variable-rate loan loses. People may also respond to a rate change because of the news around it, not the rate itself.

## Main gaps

- actual spending, debt and portfolio records matched to beliefs;
- effects by income, age, race, debt, home ownership and liquid savings;
- whether people distinguish inflation from borrowing costs;
- how messages travel through news and social media;
- which households gain interest income while others pay more;
- whether repeated policy surprises change trust in the Fed.

## Decision rule

Pair household beliefs with financial records and rate exposure. Do not describe one “household response” when borrowers, savers and renters may face opposite effects.
