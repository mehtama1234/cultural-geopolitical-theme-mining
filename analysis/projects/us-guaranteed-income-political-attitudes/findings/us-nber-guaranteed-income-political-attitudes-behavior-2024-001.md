# Finding 001: More income did not automatically produce more political participation or institutional trust

**Status:** bounded U.S. randomized political-economy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 33214 analyzes a randomized experiment in which 1,000
low-income Americans received $1,000 per month for three years and 2,000
controls received $50 per month. The authors find no detectable effect on
political participation or engagement, largely no change in political
preferences, and no detectable change in trust in government, polarization, or
support for democracy. They report small improvements in affect toward racial
groups and some politicians, plus evidence that treated participants viewed
work as more important or as a requirement for accessing government programs.

```text
guaranteed income
  -> household resources and relationships
  -> [tested] political participation, preferences, trust, affect, work meaning
  -> no automatic political mobilization or trust shift detected
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a direct causal counterexample to a simple resource model in which
more money automatically creates more civic participation, changed ideology,
or trust in government. It complements the separate intrahousehold analysis:
the same broad intervention can change household work, relationships, and
healthcare reports in different directions while leaving measured political
participation and institutional trust largely unchanged.

## Limits and next test

- The result applies to the study population, intervention, duration, and
  measured outcomes; a null estimate is not universal proof of no political
  effect.
- It does not establish the household mechanism connecting cash to work, care,
  relationships, health, identity, or political judgment.
- Small affect and work-attitude changes should not be promoted to turnout,
  ideology, trust, or durable realignment.
- Other transfer designs, tax treatment, shocks, messengers, or political
  contexts may produce different responses.

The next decisive test is a protected household panel that joins dated cash
receipt, material use, intrahousehold redistribution, work/care/health trade-
offs, perceived fairness and attribution, trust, participation, vote, and
post-payment persistence.
