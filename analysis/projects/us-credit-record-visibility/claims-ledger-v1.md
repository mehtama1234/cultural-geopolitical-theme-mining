# Claims ledger: US credit records and financial visibility

**Check date:** 2026-09-11  
**Scope:** United States; payment trouble, credit records, housing access, and later financial room

| ID | Claim | Evidence type | What supports it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | Credit access differs sharply by race, class, and hometown. | Population-linked administrative data | NBER Working Paper 34053 links Census and credit-bureau data and finds differences that emerge in young adulthood and persist. | Group gaps do not identify every cause or prove lender intent. |
| C-002 | Many repayment gaps begin with events in young adulthood and are not explained by observed income and wealth alone. | Decomposition and place analysis | NBER reports delinquencies tied to early credit, student-loan, and bill payments; observed income and wealth explain only part of the gaps. | The paper offers several mechanisms; it does not identify one cause for every person. |
| C-003 | An overdraft process can create fees, account closure, and a separate bank record. | HBS research summary and policy variation | HBS describes high-to-low transaction ordering, multiple fees, ChexSystems records, and improvements after bans at affected banks. | The evidence concerns a specific bank practice, not every account record. |
| C-004 | An eviction order can reduce later credit access and increase other hardship. | Judge-assignment natural experiment | NBER finds effects on homelessness, hospital visits, earnings, durable-goods consumption, and credit access; longer-run debt rises and credit scores fall. | The study covers Cook County and New York City; pre-trends require care. |
| C-005 | A record can be both a report of hardship and a force that shapes the next choice. | Our inference | C-001 through C-004 show how events become signals, and how later gates can add cost or reduce options. | The same household's event, record, gatekeeper decision, and housing result are not joined here. |

## Mechanism under test

```text
late payment, overdraft, eviction, or thin record
  -> bank, lender, or landlord sees a signal or a blank
  -> account, credit, housing, or loan access changes
  -> household pays more, moves, borrows elsewhere, or loses a buffer
  -> the record becomes part of the next hardship
```

## What would weaken the finding

- The record does not change the next offer, deposit, rent, interest rate, or access decision.
- Households can correct errors and switch providers at little cost.
- Higher prices after a denial do not worsen later repayment or housing outcomes.
- Credit differences disappear after the full underlying hardship and local context are measured.

## Next test

Follow households from the first payment event through three years of bank, credit, rental, housing, work, and location decisions. Record the alternatives after a denial and whether the record could be corrected.
