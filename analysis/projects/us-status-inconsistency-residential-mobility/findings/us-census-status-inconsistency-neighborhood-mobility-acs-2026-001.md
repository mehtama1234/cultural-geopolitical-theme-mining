# Finding 001: People’s relative status inside a neighborhood is linked to how they move

**Status:** bounded cultural-status, place, and mobility finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The same household income can mean something different depending on the
neighborhood around it. A Census CES working paper separates two measures:

- neighborhood status: how the tract’s median family income compares with
  similar neighborhoods; and
- individual status: where the household’s income ranks inside its tract.

Using restricted American Community Survey data, the paper finds that status
mismatch is related to residential mobility. High individual status in a
disadvantaged neighborhood is associated with more within-county mobility.
Low individual status in a high-status neighborhood is also associated with
more mobility, consistent with relative deprivation. Neighborhood status appears
more important for short-distance than long-distance movement.

The practical chain is:

```text
household income and neighborhood context
  -> relative status or mismatch
  -> comparison, belonging, opportunity, or pressure [not separated here]
  -> nearby or cross-county move
  -> housing cost, quality, safety, identity, and later security [not measured]
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This adds a cultural meaning layer to place and mobility:

> People respond not only to what they have, but also to where they stand in
> relation to the people and places around them.

It helps explain why the same income, housing stock, or neighborhood average
can produce different mobility patterns. It also prevents “move” from being
treated as one outcome: short-distance adjustment, long-distance exit,
displacement, aspiration, and search for belonging may look similar in a basic
mobility measure.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The source is a Census CES working paper, not an official Census endorsement.
- The models do not prove that status mismatch caused a move.
- They do not identify whether the reason was rent, work, family, school,
  safety, discrimination, illness, or housing supply.
- A move can improve conditions, reflect forced displacement, or leave the
  household in another constrained situation.
- The analysis does not directly measure housing quality, affordability,
  belonging, trust, safety, or later wellbeing.
- Status concepts are relative and contextual; they should not be read as a
  universal psychological response.

The next decisive artifact is a household-place panel joining income rank,
neighborhood context, tenure, rent or mortgage, housing search, discrimination,
social ties, move reason, destination quality, and later security or civic
meaning.
