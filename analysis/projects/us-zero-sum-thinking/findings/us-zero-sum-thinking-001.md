# Finding 001: Seeing politics as competition can connect history to policy preferences

**Status:** bounded U.S. cultural-political finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

An NBER study of 20,400 U.S. residents measured “zero-sum thinking”: the belief
that one person's or group's gain tends to come at another's expense. People
with more of this mindset were more likely to support government redistribution,
race- and gender-based affirmative action, and more restrictive immigration
policies. The study also connects the mindset with individual and ancestral
experiences involving mobility, immigration, immigrant exposure, enslavement,
and local exposure to enslavement.

In simple terms:

```text
perceived group competition
  + personal and historical experience
  -> different beliefs about who gains and who loses
  -> different policy preferences
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This gives the cultural-politics lane a concrete bridge between material
experience, historical memory, group identity, and policy judgment. It also
adds an important complication: zero-sum thinking does not point to one
ideological package. The same mindset can accompany support for redistribution
and restrictive immigration, depending on which groups are perceived to be
competing.

## Limits and next test

- The evidence is primarily survey association, not randomized causation.
- Historical and ancestral correlations do not identify one transmission path.
- Scarcity or exclusion may sometimes be real rather than merely perceived.
- The study does not measure later voting, organizing, policy implementation,
  intergroup contact, or durable belief change.

The next test would join repeated measures of material exposure, historical
experience, group attribution, zero-sum beliefs, contact, policy judgment, and
political action in the same people or places.
