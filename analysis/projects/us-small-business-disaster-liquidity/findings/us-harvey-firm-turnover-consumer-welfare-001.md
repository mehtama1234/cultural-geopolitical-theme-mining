# Finding 002: Firm recovery and customer welfare can diverge by neighborhood

**Status:** case-specific consumer-welfare evidence · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

The authors' Hurricane Harvey study uses payment-card transactions and a
consumer-demand model across 12 large store categories. It reports that about
1% of observed firms permanently exited and another 3% temporarily closed for
at least two months. After major closures, affected cardholders traveled about
15% farther to shop; same-chain trips rose about 50% in distance.

Entry was not a simple replacement: entrants contributed more modeled consumer
surplus than exiting stores, but entry did not fully replace exits in the
hardest-hit neighborhoods. The authors estimate average Houston-MSA welfare
losses around 0.29% of pre-storm retail spending, with much larger losses in
the most affected tracts. Their price appendix reports no meaningful
medium- or long-term price effect beyond a short-lived landfall-week spike.

## Event chain

```text
Hurricane Harvey flooding
  -> store closure and local firm turnover
  -> consumers travel farther or substitute to other stores
  -> average MSA welfare loss can look small while tract-level losses are large
  -> [open] income-specific burden, time sacrifice, worker effects, public
         services, political attribution, and durable place change
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

| Stage | Supported | Still open |
|---|---|---|
| Firm shock | Permanent exit and temporary closure are observed in the case | Damage, insurance, credit, and aid for each firm |
| Customer response | Longer travel distance after major closures | Whether trips were missed, more expensive, unsafe, or impossible |
| Market replacement | Entrants have higher modeled consumer-surplus contribution than exits | Whether they are nearby, affordable, culturally acceptable, or open at needed times |
| Welfare distribution | Aggregate MSA loss can hide much larger tract-level losses | Household income, disability, vehicle access, care duties, and substitution costs |
| Policy implication | Targeted aid can differ from subsidizing every damaged firm in the model | Actual aid receipt, public trust, political action, and long-term local capacity |

This is the customer-side complement to the disaster-liquidity firm record. It
shows why employment, revenue, or firm survival cannot stand in for customer
access: even with no lasting aggregate price movement, closure can impose time
and distance costs on a subset of neighborhoods. It also supplies a counterpoint
to a blanket “keep every firm open” rule: replacement quality and location
matter, not only the number of surviving firms.

## Interpretation rule

```text
no long-run price effect != no consumer burden
entrant surplus     != entrant accessibility
average MSA loss    != equal neighborhood experience
store closure       != household hardship for every customer
targeted aid model  != observed policy receipt or legitimacy
```

The payment-card design is valuable because it observes realized shopping
movement, but card coverage and model-based surplus limit the claim. Cash users,
non-retail services, people who stopped shopping, and households without a
vehicle may be underrepresented or differently represented.

## Next decisive test

Align store closure and flood exposure with customer income, vehicle access,
trip purpose, prices, and missed purchases; then connect firm aid or insurance
receipt to worker retention and owner-household recovery. A small tract-level
transaction summary or lawful replication table is sufficient; do not acquire
the raw payment-card panel by default.
