# Finding 001: Delegated medical-device review can add speed while the public audit clock remains underspecified

**Status:** bounded health-regulation and delegated-trust finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

FDA's voluntary Third Party Review Program lets FDA-accredited organizations
conduct initial reviews of selected low- and moderate-risk medical-device
510(k) submissions before FDA makes the final market decision. From fiscal years
2018 through 2025, third parties provided 617 reviews and recommendations,
about 2% of CDRH's annual 510(k) reviews. FDA reported 25 periodic audits of
third parties from 2000 through 2026, while nine third parties had active
accreditations as of May 2026.

GAO found that FDA's audit policies lacked target time frames for completing an
audit and communicating findings. Some recent audits involving deficiencies
took more than six months to close.

The route is therefore:

```text
accreditation
  -> delegated technical review
  -> FDA final decision
  -> periodic audit and correction
  -> device availability
  -> patient safety, effectiveness, and access [open]
```

The source supports a real delegated-capacity stage and a real audit-governance
gap. It does not show that delegation made review faster in a measured causal
sense, that a device was unsafe, or that any patient was harmed.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a health-regulatory version of the recurring capacity-versus-trust
tension. The state can use accredited private organizations to expand review
capacity, while the public assurance chain depends on who audits them, how soon
deficiencies are communicated, and whether correction is visible.

It also sharpens the difference between delegation and exit. A device sponsor
gets another route into FDA review, but patients do not thereby receive a
verified safety outcome. Firm access, regulatory speed, audit quality, market
availability, and lived health are separate stages.

## Limits and next test

- The 617 reviews are not all medical devices or all FDA 510(k) reviews.
- Third-party recommendations are not final FDA decisions.
- Audit delay does not prove noncompliance or patient harm.
- The source does not compare review quality, approval speed, device prices,
  access, or patient outcomes with ordinary FDA review.
- Audit practices changed across a period from 2000 to 2026.

The next decisive artifact is a device-level ledger joining sponsor, device
class, third-party reviewer, review time, FDA decision, audit status,
deficiency, correction, recall or safety signal, price/access, and patient-level
outcome where lawful. Until then, keep delegated review as capacity and audit
timing as a trust condition—not as a safety failure rate.
