# Finding 001: A wildfire can change both household income and who comes back

**Status:** bounded wildfire, household-economy, mobility, and community-composition finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census CES paper links property damage from more than 100 U.S. wildfires to
tax and Census records. Residents of burned areas were wealthier than the U.S.
population on average, but within the fire perimeters lower pre-fire income was
associated with a greater chance that a home burned.

For occupants whose homes were destroyed, income was lower for three years after
the fire. The cumulative loss was equal to 26% of their pre-fire annual income.
The study detected no income or employment effect for residents without direct
property damage.

Community recovery was slow: four years after a fire, half of destroyed
addresses remained unoccupied, and the addresses that were reoccupied housed
residents with substantially higher incomes than before.

The practical chain is:

```text
wildfire and property destruction
  -> rebuilding, income loss, debt, aid, or move
  -> delayed occupancy and changed return population
  -> neighborhood services, prices, belonging, and political meaning [open]
```

The simple social insight is that an affected region can look relatively wealthy
while direct losses fall unevenly on lower-income households inside it. Recovery
can also change the community, not just restore the old one.

## What this adds to the atlas

This complements the flood-risk and flood-firm records with an individual-level
damage-to-income path. It also gives the place lane a clearer composition
mechanism: return is not a neutral endpoint when destroyed addresses remain
empty and higher-income households disproportionately occupy the homes that
reopen.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is a Census CES working paper, not a Census Bureau endorsement.
- Property destruction is not the same as all wildfire exposure or community
  stress.
- The record does not identify insurance, aid, rebuilding cost, debt, health,
  displacement, or voluntary versus forced nonreturn.
- Higher-income reoccupancy can reflect housing prices, rebuilding barriers,
  insurance, aid, selection, or migration—not preference alone.
- No measured indirect income effect does not prove no service, social, or future
  climate consequence.

The next decisive artifact is a protected household-property panel linking fire
damage, insurance, aid, rebuilding, debt, income, employment, health, occupancy,
move reason, destination, social ties, and later community security.
