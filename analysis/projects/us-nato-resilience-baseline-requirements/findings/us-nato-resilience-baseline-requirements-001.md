# Finding 001: NATO now treats civilian resilience as part of military readiness, but implementation is still an open question

**Status:** bounded geopolitical and infrastructure policy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

NATO's 2026 Resilience Baseline Requirements connect military readiness to
civilian systems. The document calls for whole-of-government and
whole-of-society planning, sustained resource planning, and Host Nation Support.
It reaches into movement of people, food and water, mass-casualty and health
crises, communications, transportation, border crossing, supply chains, and
private-sector dependencies.

The practical route is:

```text
alliance resilience standard
  -> national governance and resource planning
  -> civilian infrastructure and essential-service continuity
  -> movement and reception of allied forces / Host Nation Support
  -> military enablement while mitigating population effects
  -> [open] spending, compliance, public consent, outages, and crisis results
```

This adds a geopolitical version of the atlas's recurring distinction:
capacity can be formally required and publicly visible while practical control,
fair burden sharing, and lived reliability remain unmeasured.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

The policy makes civilian infrastructure an explicit strategic interface. A
communications network, transport corridor, hospital system, food or water
network, border process, or private supplier can be asked to serve both daily
civilian life and military movement or continuity. That creates a real planning
connection, but not yet a measured outcome.

The document also contains a built-in tension rather than hiding it: civil and
military plans should support allied operations and Host Nation Support while
mitigating effects on the population and maintaining essential services. The
question is therefore not only whether capacity exists, but who controls its
use, who pays for redundancy, who waits during a crisis, and whether people
accept the trade-off.

## Limits and next test

- This is an official policy and requirements document, not a survey or
  country-by-country compliance audit.
- It does not show which states have funded or implemented each requirement.
- It does not measure civilian outages, service quality, military access,
  private-firm compliance, or Host Nation Support performance.
- It does not establish public consent, distribution of costs, or whether
  resilience improves deterrence or crisis outcomes.
- The requirements may improve civilian protection as well as military
  enablement; the text alone cannot determine the balance in practice.

The next decisive evidence would join the baseline to country implementation
plans, budgets, exercises, outage and recovery records, host-nation agreements,
and public or local-government responses. Until then, the safe claim is that
NATO has made the civil-military dependency explicit at the policy level.
