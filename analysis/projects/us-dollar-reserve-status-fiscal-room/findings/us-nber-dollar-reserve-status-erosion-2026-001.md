# Finding 001: Reserve-currency demand is a state-capacity asset with household incidence

**Status:** bounded modeled geopolitical/fiscal-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

The NBER paper documents a context in which foreign ownership of U.S. public
debt has fallen from roughly 45% to roughly 30% over two decades while foreign
holdings of private dollar assets remained steadier. Its calibrated
counterfactual model asks what would happen under a complete loss of reserve
currency status. The baseline implies an 8.8% real dollar depreciation, a
0.9-percentage-point rise in U.S. real interest rates, domestic reabsorption of
about 50% of GDP in dollar bonds, and an aggregate wealth loss around one year
of U.S. GDP (about $18 trillion in the summary's calibration).

```text
foreign demand for dollar safe assets
  -> borrowing cost, exchange rate, and public fiscal room
  -> household prices, asset values, investment, and firm financing
  -> policy response and external confidence
  -> [open] distribution, political legitimacy, and actual reserve-status change
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This makes geopolitical power materially consequential without treating
reserve status as a symbolic label. External demand can function as a state
capacity and household-price condition; conversely, an erosion of that demand
could redistribute adjustment through interest rates, exchange rates, asset
markets, and public budgets. It also gives the atlas a disciplined distinction
between early indicators and a complete-status-loss counterfactual.

## Limits and next test

- The numerical consequences are model estimates under a complete-loss
  counterfactual, not current outcomes or a forecast.
- Aggregate wealth loss does not identify who gains or loses, how policy reacts,
  or whether households face higher prices, taxes, or borrowing costs.
- Foreign ownership, private dollar-asset demand, convenience yield, and formal
  reserve status are related but distinct stages.

The next decisive test is a dated U.S. dollar-demand episode joining foreign
holdings, Treasury yields, exchange rates, fiscal issuance, import prices,
household portfolios, firm financing, policy communication, and external
official response.
