# Project: Minimum wages and factory automation

This project tracks what happens between a labor-policy change and a firm's
technology choice. The initial Census working paper reports that a 10% rise
in the minimum wage is associated with roughly an 8% increase in manufacturing
robot adoption relative to the mean.

The safe theme is not “minimum wages destroy jobs.” It is that a wage-floor
policy can change the production system before we know whether workers receive
higher pay, lose tasks, gain safer work, or gain any say over the new system.
The program must keep robot adoption, employment, wages, hours, productivity,
safety, bargaining power, and household security separate.

The decisive next artifact is a protected plant-worker ledger joining wage
policy exposure, robot installation, tasks, staffing, pay, hours, turnover,
injuries, training, union or worker voice, prices, productivity, and later
employment. The public working-paper page does not provide that full bridge.

## Source

- [Census CES Working Paper CES-26-42](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-42.html)
- [Machine-readable trend record](../../records/us-census-minimum-wage-robot-adoption-2026.json)

The [NBER poor-family minimum-wage record](../../records/us-nber-minimum-wage-poor-low-income-families-2026.json)
adds a household-positioned counterpoint: in SIPP evidence, initially employed
low-wage workers in poor and low-income families show adverse employment, hours,
and earnings effects, while the abstract reports no extra adverse gradient for
the poorest workers. This does not settle the policy debate; it makes family
income-to-needs position, worker selection, firm adjustment, and household
resources explicit next measurements.

The [NBER immigrant minimum-wage record](../../records/us-nber-immigrants-minimum-wage-labor-market-effects-2026.json)
adds a worker-subgroup boundary: minimum-wage increases reduce hours without a
reported employment effect for immigrants, with effects concentrated among
recently arrived, likely undocumented workers in high-turnover industries;
native workers show no comparable response. The abstract does not disclose
effect sizes or the full design, so the next test is hours, income, scheduling,
legal-status proxy, employer replacement cost, and household outcomes in the
same episode.
