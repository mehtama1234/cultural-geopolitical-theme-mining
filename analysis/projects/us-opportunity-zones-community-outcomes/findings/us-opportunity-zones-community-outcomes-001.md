# Finding 001: Tax incentives can attract investment without proving community improvement

**Status:** bounded place, firm, and public-benefit finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO's August 2026 review says Qualified Opportunity Funds held more than $108
billion in assets at the end of 2024, while nearly 9,000 designated Zones
covered about 10% of the U.S. population. The incentive was primarily used for
real-estate development according to states, fund representatives, and other
specialists. Investment was more likely in urban areas with infrastructure,
ongoing development, and community support.

The public-benefit evidence was thinner. States and selected funds were
generally not tracking specific outcomes tied to the incentive. Among 24 states
that reported being aware of Opportunity Zone investment, 14 said it affected
job creation, housing, or both. That is a state-reported perception, not a
measured job or housing effect.

The route is:

```text
tax advantage
  -> fund capital
  -> project or business activity
  -> local jobs, housing, and services
  -> affordability, displacement, and durable community improvement [open]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a place-and-firm version of a recurring subtheme:

> Capital can enter a distressed place without the public being able to tell
> who benefited, who paid, or whether the place became more secure.

The incentive may help make projects financeable, but the selection process
also favors places with infrastructure, existing development, and community
support. That means investment can follow local readiness rather than create
it. The same policy can therefore produce visible construction while leaving
the hardest-to-serve places and the distribution of gains unresolved.

The selected-fund follow-up is a useful counterpoint. By June 2026, 12 of 16
selected funds had completed all planned projects; two had completed some,
one had abandoned one planned project while continuing another, and one had
stopped pursuing the original incentive. Completion is real, but it still does
not establish local affordability, job quality, displacement, or durable
community wellbeing.

## Limits and next test

- The 16-fund cases and seven site visits are illustrative and not generalizable
  to all Opportunity Funds or Zones.
- The 54 responses out of 56 states, territories, and D.C. provide a broad
  administrative view, but the 14-of-24 outcome statement is conditional on
  states aware of investment and is based on reported perceptions.
- The report does not provide a causal comparison of Zones with similar
  non-Zone places.
- Fund assets and completed projects do not show who received jobs, housing,
  profits, tax benefits, or displacement costs.
- New reporting requirements may improve future evaluation, but their effects
  are not yet observed.

The next decisive artifact is a tract- or project-linked comparison joining
investment timing to jobs, wages, business survival, housing prices and rents,
occupancy, displacement, public services, and resident experience. Until then,
the safe claim is that investment capacity and project completion are visible
while durable community benefit remains uncertain.
