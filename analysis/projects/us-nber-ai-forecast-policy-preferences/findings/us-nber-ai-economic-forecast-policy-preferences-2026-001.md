# Finding 001: AI expectations divide over consequences more than capability speed

**Status:** bounded forecast-and-policy-preference finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35046 elicits forecasts from five groups: academic
economists, AI-company employees, AI policy researchers, highly accurate
forecasters, and the general public. The median respondent in every group
expects substantial AI capability advances by 2030. The main disagreement is
not primarily about how quickly AI will advance; it is about what advanced AI
would do to the economy.

Across groups, the median forecast is 2.5% annual GDP growth, above typical
medium- and long-run government or private-sector baseline forecasts of 2.0%
and 1.7%. Under a conditional rapid-progress scenario, respondents forecast
around 4% annualized growth and a fall in labor-force participation from 62% to
55% by 2050, with roughly half that decline—about 10 million jobs—attributed to
AI in the scenario.

Policy preferences also diverge. Experts strongly favor targeted worker
retraining, while the general public supports targeted programs as well as
broader interventions such as a job guarantee and universal basic income.

The atlas-relevant insight is:

> The political meaning of AI is formed before its full economic effects are
> observed. Shared expectations about capability can coexist with sharply
> different beliefs about distribution, labor displacement, and which forms of
> public insurance or redistribution are legitimate.

The route is:

```text
AI capability expectation
  -> belief about growth, labor demand, and distribution
  -> perceived exposure and acceptable policy
  -> retraining, income support, job guarantee, or UBI preference
  -> political legitimacy and action [not observed here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds an expectation-to-policy layer distinct from records measuring AI
adoption, coding activity, firm integration, or public trust. It helps explain
why the same technological trajectory can produce different claims about
fairness, urgency, and the proper role of the state.

The next decisive test is a longitudinal or repeated survey joined to actual
AI exposure, employment or task change, household security, media/information
exposure, policy contact, and later political participation or support.

## Limits and counterevidence

- Forecasts are not realized economic effects and rapid-progress assumptions may
  not occur.
- The abstract does not provide the full sample counts, question wording, or
  calibration error needed to compare forecast accuracy across groups.
- Policy preferences may reflect values and distributional concerns beyond the
  numerical forecasts.
- A preference for retraining, a job guarantee, or UBI does not establish policy
  adoption, adequacy, or household benefit.
- The study does not link respondents to later employment, income, trust, or
  political action.
