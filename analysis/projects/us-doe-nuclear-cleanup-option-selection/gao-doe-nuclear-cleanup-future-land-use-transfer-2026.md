# Nuclear-cleanup remedies can prefigure future land use while transfer remains open

**Checked:** 2026-10-20  
**Stage:** cleanup remedy and future-use assumption → transfer/reuse tradeoff; parcel outcomes and long-term safety remain open

GAO's October 16, 2026 review of six selected Department of Energy cleanup
sites adds a place-and-infrastructure route distinct from the earlier review of
early project option framing. DOE and regulators use future-land-use assumptions
to set cleanup standards, but those plans inform rather than bind later land-use
decisions. A cleanup remedy therefore shapes which future uses are safely
available even after land is transferred or leased.

At Oak Ridge, Paducah, Portsmouth, and Savannah River, cleanup levels aligned
with industrial-use plans. At nearly a third of Hanford and most contaminated
areas of Idaho, standards were more stringent than needed for anticipated
conservation, preservation, or industrial uses, although residential use is
unlikely. DOE has transferred or leased a small amount of land for industrial
use at five selected sites. Stakeholders described possible economic growth,
high-quality jobs, and local tax revenue, alongside diversion of cleanup
resources, pollution or recontamination, and future DOE liabilities. New DOE
initiatives include energy development, nuclear-fuel-cycle hubs, and
co-located AI data centers.

The evidence boundary is material. The selected-site review does not show a
common parcel-level record of transfer, occupancy, exposure, recontamination,
jobs, tax receipts, community consent, health outcomes, or realized liability.
The estimated $641–$840 billion cost to clean up 15 remaining sites by 2100 is
program scale, not an outcome of the selected reuse projects.
