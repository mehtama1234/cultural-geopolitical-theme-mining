# Finding: formal project review can begin after the practical choice has already narrowed

**Status:** bounded public-project governance and infrastructure finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

GAO reviewed 21 Department of Energy Environmental Management mission-need
statements for large projects estimated to cost at least $100 million. DOE's
own standards say a mission-need statement should define the problem without
choosing a particular solution, so that later planning can compare options.

GAO found that most reviewed statements identified a particular solution. In
some cases, that early preference meant that potential cost-saving options were
not considered or pursued later. GAO also described an Idaho example in which
experts said a cheaper technically sound treatment was not pursued because of
an existing regulatory agreement; DOE spent years on a solution it later
deemed suboptimal and suspended.

The review process did not include independent experts at the mission-need
stage. GAO recommended adding them before DOE agrees to a solution with
regulators. DOE later added outside experts for the largest projects—those
requiring a review at $750 million or more—but GAO said smaller large projects
remain exposed to the same early-framing risk.

The broader institutional insight is:

> A public system can have a formal review process while the earliest framing
> quietly determines which choices are treated as realistic.

## Limits and next test

- The 21 statements are a selected review set, not every DOE project.
- A predetermined solution can reflect real safety, legal, or technical
  constraints; the report does not prove contractor capture or waste in every
  case.
- Independent review can widen the option set but does not guarantee a cheaper,
  safer, faster, or more publicly acceptable result.
- The review does not provide a common project-level outcome series for cost,
  schedule, cleanup, environmental safety, or community effects.

The next decisive artifact is a named-project ledger joining the original
mission need, proposed options, contractor and regulator involvement,
independent review, cost and risk comparison, approval, delivery, safety,
cleanup result, and later public judgment.
