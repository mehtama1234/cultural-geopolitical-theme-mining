# Finding 001: A steady poverty rate can hide long poverty spells

**Status:** bounded household, inequality, and public-systems finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The Census Bureau's 2026 brief using the 2025 Survey of Income and Program
Participation reports that the monthly poverty rate was 11.9% in January 2024
and did not significantly change through the year. That stable monthly rate
does not mean poverty was short or evenly spread across people.

In 2024, 15.1% of people experienced episodic poverty, defined here as poverty
for at least two consecutive months. Among those who experienced episodic
poverty, 59.2% were in poverty for all 12 months. The rate was 18.9% for
children under 18. Among people who experienced episodic poverty, older adults
had the longest average duration at about 11 months and a 79.4% chance of being
poor in all 12 months. The brief also reports higher episodic-poverty rates for
non-Hispanic Black people (20.8%) and Hispanic people (21.3%) than for
non-Hispanic White people (11.6%) and non-Hispanic Asian people (12.7%).

The route is:

```text
monthly poverty average
  -> repeated or lasting poverty spells
  -> unequal time with too little pre-tax money income
  -> pressure on housing, food, care, health, work, and trust [open]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a time dimension to the household-pressure theme:

> Counting who is poor in a month is not the same as measuring how long people
> remain under pressure.

That distinction matters culturally and politically. A household that briefly
falls below an income threshold and one that stays below it for most of a year
may appear in the same monthly count but face very different choices about
rent, food, medical care, work, debt, and asking for help. The source measures
the unequal exposure and duration; it does not measure those downstream
experiences.

## Limits and next test

- The source uses the official poverty measure: pre-tax money income compared
  with monthly thresholds based on family composition.
- The thresholds do not vary geographically, and the measure does not capture
  assets, consumption, debt, noncash benefits, housing quality, food security,
  unpaid care, health, or services received.
- Episodic poverty is restricted to people in the poverty universe for all 12
  months, so it is not a rate for every person who was eligible to be observed
  in any month.
- The subgroup comparisons describe unequal exposure; they do not identify
  why the differences occurred.
- The brief follows monthly income status in a longitudinal survey, but this
  finding does not join a poverty spell to a specific assistance, bill,
  eviction, health, work, trust, or political-action outcome.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected longitudinal analysis that
links a dated poverty spell to income changes, benefits, housing or utility
payments, work and care, and later recovery. Until that exists, the safe claim
is that stable monthly averages can coexist with concentrated and persistent
economic pressure.
