# Finding 001: A safety policy can make harm harder to see

**Status:** bounded identity, institutional-trust, and public-safety finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census CES paper studies Secure Communities, an enforcement program that
increased deportation fears in immigrant communities. It finds that Hispanic
victims became less likely to report crimes to police, offending against
Hispanics increased, and the number of reported crimes did not change because
the opposing movements canceled each other out. The paper estimates that a 10%
drop in reporting increased offending by 7.9%.

The practical chain is:

```text
enforcement policy and deportation fear
  -> willingness to contact police
  -> reported crime record
  -> police response and deterrence
  -> actual victimization and community safety [partly observed]
  -> trust, support, and civic action [open]
```

The broader cultural point is simple: people’s relationship with an institution
can change the measurement of the problem itself. Silence is not proof that the
problem disappeared, and a stable official count is not automatically stable
public safety.

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a strong example of **institutional trust shaping what becomes visible**.
It links identity and state policy to voice, measurement, deterrence, and unequal
exposure. It also gives the atlas a sharper warning: the state may be most able
to count a problem where people feel safest using the state’s reporting route.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is a Census CES working paper, not a Census Bureau endorsement.
- The result concerns Secure Communities and the studied populations and places;
  it should not be generalized to every enforcement policy or immigrant group.
- The abstract does not separate every reason for nonreporting, such as fear,
  distrust, cost, perceived futility, or alternative community support.
- Reported crime, offending, and lived victimization are different measures.
- The finding does not show later trust, organizing, voting, remedy, or durable
  legitimacy.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected local panel linking policy
exposure, victimization, reporting choice, police response, alternative help,
case outcome, repeat harm, and later institutional trust or civic action.
