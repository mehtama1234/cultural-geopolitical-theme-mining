# Finding 001: A healthy-food price wedge can exist without a detectable maternal or newborn health improvement

**Status:** bounded Indigenous, food-policy, health, and place-implementation finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The Navajo Nation's Healthy Diné Nation Act used two levers: healthy foods and
beverages were exempted from sales tax, while unhealthy foods and beverages
faced a higher tax. Together, the policy created a 7–8% price difference
between healthier and less healthy products.

An NBER study uses difference-in-differences and event studies with birth-
certificate data for mothers and newborns. It finds no evidence that the law
improved the measured diet- and weight-related health outcomes. The estimates
are precise enough to rule out gestational-diabetes improvements as large as
those reported for some U.S. city sugary-drink taxes.

The authors identify incomplete retailer implementation and cross-border
shopping as possible explanations.

The route is:

```text
tribal fiscal policy
  -> healthy/unhealthy price wedge
  -> retailer pass-through and household shopping
  -> diet and exposure
  -> maternal/newborn health outcome
```

The study closes the last arrow as a measured null for its outcomes, but it
does not show which middle arrow failed or whether another outcome changed.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a place-specific counterexample to a simple price-incentive story:

> A government can change the formal price structure without changing health
> outcomes when the retail system, nearby markets, and actual shopping route do
> not carry the policy through.

It also protects against treating one community as a generic policy laboratory.
Sovereignty, retailer participation, border geography, food availability,
household room, and cultural meaning can all shape whether a fiscal rule feels
usable, respectful, or effective.

## Limits and next test

- The public abstract does not disclose exact sample counts, all comparison
  locations, retailer compliance, or store-level price changes.
- The null health result does not prove that purchases, prices, revenue, or
  diet did not change.
- Cross-border shopping and incomplete implementation are possible mechanisms,
  not fully isolated causal estimates in the abstract.
- The study does not establish community acceptance, sovereignty effects,
  household burden, or long-run health.

The next decisive artifact is a protected community-retail ledger linking tax
status, shelf prices, retailer participation, product availability, household
purchases, cross-border trips, dietary exposure, perceived respect, and later
maternal or child health. Until then, the safe claim is that the studied
health outcomes did not improve detectably despite a formal price wedge.
