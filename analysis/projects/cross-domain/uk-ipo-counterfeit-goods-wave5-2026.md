# UK IPO Counterfeit Goods Wave 5: affordability, platform trust, and weak consumer recourse

## Finding

The UK Intellectual Property Office's fifth counterfeit-goods study shows counterfeit purchasing as a consumer-room and platform-governance problem, not only a law-enforcement problem. In the 2025 nationally representative survey, 26% of adults had ever bought a counterfeit and 17% currently purchased them. Price and perceived comparable quality were central incentives, with counterfeits often 30%–85% cheaper than genuine products. The pattern was especially visible among younger and price-sensitive consumers.

Access is moving through large online marketplaces and social media, while unintentional purchasing rose sharply among 18–24 year-olds. More than two-thirds of recent counterfeit purchasers who were asked about harms reported at least one negative consequence, including product failure, scams, data-security risks, and health or safety risks. Yet 38% were unaware of reporting channels, and only about one in five people with negative experiences took action. Reporting was often contingent on the possibility of financial recovery.

The deeper theme is a broken conversion from consumer exposure to usable recourse. “Dupe,” “fake,” and “lookalike” language can reduce perceived seriousness, while platform convenience and low prices normalize the transaction. A buyer may simultaneously exercise agency as a bargain seeker and lack practical agency when the product fails, data is exposed, or safety is compromised.

## Theme and subthemes

- **Affordability substitution:** counterfeit demand can function as a low-cost substitute when branded goods exceed household room.
- **Platform authenticity gap:** search, social discovery, reviews, and convenience do not guarantee product verification.
- **Recourse conditionality:** consumers act when recovery appears possible, not necessarily when harm occurs.
- **Moral-distance language:** “dupe” and “lookalike” can soften legal and safety salience without proving intentional evasion.
- **Unequal exposure:** younger, lower-income, and highly online consumers may face greater exposure and weaker verification or remedy routes.

## What the source supports

The study supports descriptive findings from a 5,057-person Stage 1 survey, qualitative research, and a communications-testing stage. Its core tracker measures are designed for wave comparison, but the harms module is new and some category definitions changed. The source does not authenticate purchases, measure the total market, or establish causality.

## Next test

Connect a named marketplace, payment, product-safety, platform-disclosure, consumer-reporting, refund, or enforcement intervention to verified product authenticity, complaint completion, financial recovery, data protection, safety outcomes, and durable trust. The critical question is whether low-cost access is paired with a practical exit and remedy route.

## Boundary

The report uses self-report, conditional subsamples, and analytic segments. “More than two-thirds experienced a negative consequence” applies to the relevant purchaser subgroup, not all UK adults. The new harms questions do not establish a time trend, and communications-test responses are not behavior-change evidence.

Source: [UK IPO, Counterfeit goods research (Wave 5)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ipo-counterfeit-goods-research-wave-5/ipo-counterfeit-goods-research-wave-5)
