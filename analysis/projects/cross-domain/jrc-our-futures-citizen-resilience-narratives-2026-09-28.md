# Citizen-authored futures connect resilience to agency, institutional capacity, and unresolved trade-offs

**Checked:** 2026-09-28  
**Source:** European Commission Joint Research Centre, *Resilience: The future of Europe as seen by EU citizens*  
**Record:** [machine-readable source record](../../records/comparative-jrc-our-futures-europe-citizen-resilience-narratives-2026.json)

## Core finding

The JRC’s *Our Futures* analysis adds a different evidence mode to the atlas:
citizens wrote short stories imagining everyday life in Europe in 2040. More
than 4,000 stories were collected; 340 explicitly referenced Europe’s role or
institutions, and 899 surfaced anticipated shocks. This is not a public-opinion
forecast. It is a citizen-authored map of how people connect anxiety, hope,
trade-offs, and perceived room for action.

The stories make resilience relational. Climate disruption appears alongside
migration, affordability, social division, and polarization. AI appears
alongside work, human connection, democracy, and control. The same anxiety can
produce an optimistic or catastrophic future depending on whether the storyteller
believes institutions have the capacity to act.

## Cultural/social interpretation

The value of the method is that it reveals the public’s “open middle” before a
policy question has been reduced to a yes/no position. People do not imagine
climate, migration, technology, democracy, and affordability as separate
departments. They imagine whether a social order can protect people while
remaining open, autonomous, humane, and capable of change.

The narratives also resist a simple optimism/pessimism divide. Concern and
agency coexist. A pessimistic story can still contain a proposed solution, while
an optimistic story can depend on strong institutional reform. This makes
perceived capacity to act a cultural-political variable that should be connected
to later participation, trust, and delivery evidence.

## Open middle

```text
shock exposure / future imagination / institutional capacity belief
  -> hope, fear, trade-off judgment, and perceived personal agency
  -> political participation, policy demand, adaptation, and solidarity
  -> visible delivery, correction, protection, and durable legitimacy
```

The corpus is self-selected narrative evidence. It does not establish population
prevalence, representative preferences, future probabilities, realized resilience,
or causal institutional effects.
