# Cultural discoverability: when availability becomes intermediary-controlled visibility

**Checked:** 2026-09-28  
**Status:** bounded cross-source synthesis; no pooled causal estimate

## The recurring structure

Digital abundance does not automatically create cultural access. A work can be
available yet remain difficult to find; it can be cited by an AI answer yet
send no one to the institution that produced it. The relevant chain is:

```text
production and catalog availability
  -> metadata, translation, ranking, or recommendation
  -> user discovery and interpretation
  -> direct visit, listening, reading, attendance, donation, or participation
  -> audience relationship and cultural agency
```

The first two stages are increasingly measurable. The middle stages are where
platform power and cultural politics meet: the intermediary can make a work
visible while weakening the creator's or institution's control over the
relationship that follows.

## Two complementary evidence surfaces

### European cultural content: availability is not discoverability

The European Commission-commissioned study on diverse European cultural
content treats discovery as an ecosystem rather than a single recommendation
event. Its music analysis reports 184 million tracks available on digital
platforms in 2023, with 80 million streamed fewer than ten times. It also
reports that non-national EU works represented only 14% of chart entries even
though European songs represented 38% of top-chart entries. The study identifies
language silos, weak metadata, personalization, unequal promotion capacity, and
digital-skill gaps as possible mechanisms.

This is not proof that algorithms alone caused the distribution. Domestic
language, user preference, catalog quality, promotion, and measurement choices
can all matter. The defensible finding is narrower: cultural policy must now
separate production and formal availability from exposure diversity and usable
discoverability.

### UK cultural organizations: citation visibility can diverge from owned reach

One Further's repeated comparison of 100 UK cultural organizations reports an
18% year-on-year decline in estimated organic search traffic from January–July
2026 versus the same period in 2025, with 79 organizations showing a decline.
At the same time, the average share of relevant Google AI Overviews in which an
organization appeared as a source rose from 5.8% to 15.6% in the report's
comparison.

These are modelled search estimates for a selected panel, not organization
analytics, attendance, donations, or cultural participation. The value of the
comparison is the divergence it makes visible: an institution can be more
present inside an answer interface while receiving less direct traffic to its
own site. Citation is therefore not equivalent to audience ownership.

### Malta: data-informed visibility can reach the doorway, but not yet belonging

A peer-reviewed case study at Spazju Kreattiv, Malta's National Centre for
Creativity, supplies a stronger middle-stage observation. The project combined
anonymized computer-vision footfall monitoring, machine-learning forecasts, and
surveys of 280 respondents, including people who passed near the venue without
entering. Forecasts identified Castille Square as a high-potential outreach
location; Guest Relations Officers were then deployed at recommended times to
explain programming, accessibility, directions, and ticketing. On days when
AI-informed staffing recommendations were applied, average daily visitors rose
from 55 to 61—about 11%.

This is a useful bridge from discoverability to physical entry, but it is not a
causal or complete participation result. The staffing shifts were not randomly
assigned, only some recommended shifts could be implemented, and the study does
not show repeat attendance, demographic diversification, learning, revenue,
belonging, or durable audience ownership. The important cultural mechanism is
also human: the model located likely non-visitors, but a person supplied the
interpretation and invitation.

## The cultural-political implication

The key scarce resource is not only content. It is the ability to shape the
route by which people encounter content, interpret it, return to it, and join
the institution around it.

This creates four distinct political questions:

1. **Pluralism:** Do ranking and translation systems widen exposure across
   languages and communities, or repeatedly favor already-visible works?
2. **Attribution:** Can users tell which creator, institution, curator, or
   platform made the cultural encounter possible?
3. **Agency:** Can users search beyond defaults, understand recommendation
   logic, and choose a different route without excessive time or skill?
4. **Sustainability:** Does discovery produce a durable relationship—attendance,
   listening, reading, membership, donation, or participation—or merely a
   platform impression that can be monetized elsewhere?

The political issue is not that mediation exists. Cultural institutions have
always relied on curators, teachers, critics, broadcasters, librarians, and
community connectors. The issue is whether the new intermediary makes those
decisions more contestable and plural, or makes them less visible while
capturing the data and relationship value.

The Malta case adds a further distinction: **intermediation can be
conversion-oriented without being relationship-owning**. A system may improve
the probability that a passerby enters today while the institution still lacks
evidence about who returns, who feels represented, who can afford continued
participation, and who controls the data generated by the encounter.

## What the evidence supports—and does not

Supported:

- catalog presence and user access are different stages;
- language, metadata, digital skills, and promotional capacity shape who is
  likely to be discovered;
- AI answer interfaces can separate source citation from direct site traffic;
- cultural organizations face a new ownership problem around audience data,
  conversion, and repeat engagement.
- human interpretation and invitation can be the active bridge between a
  predictive visibility signal and an actual cultural encounter.

Not yet established:

- that AI summaries caused the reported traffic decline;
- that lower direct traffic reduced attendance, donations, or learning;
- that greater algorithmic diversity increases cultural belonging;
- that a transparency or prominence rule improves creator income or pluralism;
- that exposure diversity produces durable participation for any specific
  subgroup.
- that an 11% venue-footfall increase becomes repeat attendance, belonging,
  learning, revenue, or more equal participation.

## The next test

The next useful episode should link one identifiable cultural intervention or
platform change to the full route:

```text
eligible work or institution
  -> ranking, translation, or AI citation
  -> user click, listen, visit, or attendance
  -> repeat engagement or conversion
  -> creator/institution revenue and audience ownership
  -> exposure diversity, belonging, or civic participation
```

The minimum design needs platform impressions or referrals joined to first-party
analytics and a clearly defined participation outcome. A stronger design would
compare language, age, disability, geography, and digital-skill groups while
recording whether users can contest or bypass the default route. Until then,
the atlas should describe cultural discoverability as a **distribution and
agency problem**, not as proof of cultural decline or platform emancipation.
