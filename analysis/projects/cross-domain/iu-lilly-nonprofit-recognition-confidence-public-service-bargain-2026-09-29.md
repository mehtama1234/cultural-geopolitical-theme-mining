# Nonprofit infrastructure is widely used but institutionally unrecognized

## Finding

The Indiana University Lilly Family School of Philanthropy's September 2026 report finds a striking recognition gap. Only 7.4% of respondents said they or an immediate family member had received or used charitable-organization services in the past year, while 71.9% reported engaging in at least one activity typically provided by nonprofits. Nearly 90% of the people captured by the broader activity measure did not initially identify themselves as nonprofit users.

This is more than a communications problem. It suggests that parts of social infrastructure become ordinary background conditions: after-school programs, food banks, arts venues and other community activities may be used without the institution behind them entering public judgment. That invisibility can weaken the connection between service reliance, public support, funding, policy, and accountability.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`ordinary activity and care -> nonprofit infrastructure -> weak user recognition -> conditional confidence and political signaling -> funding, provider-role and legitimacy contest`

The source also shows that confidence is limited and politically permeable: 23.3% expressed high confidence in nonprofits' ability to solve societal or global problems, and 22.7% said their confidence would fall if a trusted political figure expressed a lack of confidence in the sector. The public-service bargain is therefore not simply nonprofit versus government. Many respondents say provider identity matters less than whether services are available.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Institutions can be socially essential without being culturally legible. Users may recognize the activity but not the organizational infrastructure that makes it possible.
- Public support for “community” does not automatically produce support for nonprofit funding, organizational overhead, or administrative capacity if the delivery system remains invisible.
- Service availability may be a more meaningful public criterion than provider identity, complicating narratives that assign care and basic needs cleanly to government or charity.
- The recognition gap creates both an accountability risk and a communications opportunity: making infrastructure visible could strengthen voice, but could also turn beneficiaries into political symbols or marketing targets.

## Boundary and next test

The public IU release does not provide the survey sample size, full questionnaire, activity classification, weighting or subgroup tables. The measures are reported attitudes and self-reports, not service quality, unmet need, realized giving, causal trust, or provider effectiveness. The next test is to join named nonprofit services to administrative use, funding, user outcomes, public awareness, political response, and the distribution of burdens when nonprofit capacity is strained.
