# Conflict is changing the composition of work that survives

**Checked:** 2026-09-29  
**Source:** World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11446, *Labor Demand for Digital Skills in Post-2022 Ukraine: Evidence from Online Job Vacancy Data*  
**Record:** [machine-readable source record](../../records/comparative-world-bank-ukraine-conflict-digital-skills-labor-demand-2026.json)

## Core finding

The World Bank study finds a robust positive association between attack
intensity and the digital-skill share of Ukrainian online job postings from
2021–2025. The shift is associated with changes within occupations, movement
between occupations, and remote-work adoption; within-occupation reskilling and
upskilling account for roughly half of the observed rise.

This suggests that conflict does not only destroy labour demand. It changes the
composition of work that remains visible and viable, making digital capability a
form of resilience and a possible gateway into reconstruction-era opportunity.

## Cultural/social interpretation

The finding reframes digital skill as more than an employability credential. In a
war economy, it can become a form of geographic flexibility: a way for workers
and firms to maintain activity when physical access, transport, and local
workplaces are disrupted.

But resilience is uneven. Digital hiring can privilege workers with connectivity,
recognized credentials, safe working space, language access, and occupations
that can be remote. It may also hide the labour needed to sustain households,
care, public services, and reconstruction on the ground.

## Open middle

```text
attack intensity and physical disruption
  -> occupation shifts, within-job digitalisation, and remote-work adoption
  -> changing visibility and demand for digital skills
  -> reskilling access, income continuity, and reconstruction participation
  -> unequal resilience or a more distributed labour market
```

The paper uses online vacancy data and conflict-intensity measures. It does not
establish individual causal skill acquisition, wages, job quality, household
welfare, or equal access to digital work.
