# Educational parity is advancing, but parity is not yet equal opportunity

## Finding

The World Bank's cross-country study of Sub-Saharan Africa uses microdata from roughly 736,000 people across 35 countries. It finds that gender gaps in educational attainment narrow sharply across birth cohorts. Among people born in the 1970s, female disadvantage was approximately 13–15 percentage points in primary and secondary attendance and about 1.9 years of schooling. Among cohorts born after 1999, females exceed males in secondary attendance and years of education in a majority of countries.

The convergence is not simple. Part of it reflects stagnation in boys' secondary attendance rather than only gains for girls. Learning gaps have no uniform direction across countries and often run counter to attainment gaps. Wider attainment gaps persist among rural, poorer, and Muslim populations.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a parity-to-capability mechanism:

`school access -> attendance and years -> learning -> credentials and skills -> work, income, health, voice, and household bargaining`

The evidence shows movement at the first stages but instability in the conversion chain. A society can approach gender parity in attendance while preserving unequal learning, unequal school quality, place-based exclusion, and unequal translation into paid work or political voice. Aggregate progress can therefore coexist with a culturally meaningful shift in younger women's expectations and agency, while rural, poorer, and religiously distinct populations remain outside the same opportunity set.

The “convergence or reversal” framing also matters politically. If girls' gains are described only as a reversal of male advantage, policy can miss the deeper issue: whether all children are receiving usable education and whether the labor market and household institutions can absorb expanded female capability without reproducing discrimination or care burdens.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Educational attainment is becoming a weaker proxy for equal opportunity when learning and labor-market conversion diverge.
- Generational female gains can alter family expectations, marriage timing, consumption, mobility, and political voice even before earnings equalize.
- Rurality, poverty, and religious identity remain routes through which aggregate convergence becomes unequal lived experience.
- Boys' stagnation should not be mistaken for successful gender equality; it may signal a broader secondary-education or transition problem.
- The next policy question is not simply “are girls in school?” but “what can they do with the education they receive, and who can contest the barriers after school?”

## Boundary and next test

This is descriptive cohort evidence. It does not establish that education reforms caused convergence, or that parity has produced equal learning, wages, work, health, household bargaining, or political participation. The next test is a named education-to-work episode that follows school quality and completion into skills, credentials, labor-market entry, care burdens, earnings, marriage/family decisions, and voice across rural, poor, and religious subgroups.
