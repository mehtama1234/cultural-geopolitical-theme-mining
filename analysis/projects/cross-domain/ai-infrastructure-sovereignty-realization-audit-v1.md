# AI infrastructure sovereignty and realization audit

**Status:** sixty-fourth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** public cloud, hyperscale data centers, financing, capacity,
operation, portability, local burden, workforce, public-service outcomes, and
geopolitical bargaining power

## Purpose

This audit tests whether AI and cloud infrastructure commitments become usable
state capability, or instead expand capability and external dependence at the
same time. It compares a Romanian public-cloud route and a Malaysian private
hyperscale route, while keeping completion, migration, operation, local impact,
and sovereignty as separate stages.

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Evidence with unit, date, and denominator | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public migration commitment | Romania's Authority for Digitalization/World Bank route reports public project value of 876,096,515 Romanian lei, a target to migrate at least 30 applications or IT systems, and an end date in 2026. | Public money and a concrete migration target create an auditable state-capacity intention. | A commitment does not prove applications migrated, uptime, portability, citizen access, local skills, or public auditability. |
| Public infrastructure completion claim | A separate Romanian completion announcement reports implementation through August 31, 2026, project value of 2,209,229,826.06 lei, and Tier IV/III-by-design data-center resilience objectives. | The physical infrastructure stage is reported as completed, distinct from application migration. | Infrastructure completion does not prove use, service speed/cost, uptime, security performance, vendor independence, or citizen outcomes. |
| Private hyperscale capacity and finance | Malaysia Johor case reports an IFC-supported 96 MW project, planned campus capacity of 300 MW or more, a maximum $150 million IFC loan, and an announced ownership transition. | Private capital and development finance can create substantial regional compute capacity. | Planned capacity is not operating capacity; financing and ownership do not show power/water incidence, local jobs, customer concentration, data control, portability, or state leverage. |
| Dependence is a separate capability stage | The comparative case record explicitly leaves provider chain, external capital, power, customers, data control, replaceability, and changed bargaining position unverified in both routes. | “Sovereignty” requires more than national location or public ownership; it includes operational alternatives and control. | The absence of public evidence is not proof that either country lacks control or that external dependence is harmful. |
| Local legitimacy enters before strategic benefit is proven | Public discussion connects AI/data-center growth to grid queues, affordability, energy security, water, land, pollution, jobs, transmission allocation, and host-community value. | Infrastructure capability creates a social and distributional legitimacy problem, not only a technology race. | LinkedIn commentary and poll relays are selected interpretation, not permitting outcomes, household bills, or representative local deliberation. |

## Cross-source finding

The strongest supported pattern is **capability with unresolved dependence**:

```text
funding and strategic announcement
  -> physical infrastructure
  -> application migration or compute operation
  -> reliability, workforce, power/water, and local burden
  -> portability, replaceability, and public-service outcome
  -> bargaining power, consent, and geopolitical leverage
```

Romania illustrates why infrastructure completion and application migration
must be separated. Malaysia illustrates why capacity and financing do not by
themselves establish domestic control. Across both, a state can gain a new
technical platform while remaining dependent on external operators, capital,
energy, customers, software, or specialized labor.

This also creates a legitimacy counterexample: national or firm-level support
for AI can coexist with local opposition to the energy, water, land, pollution,
and rate impacts of the infrastructure. Consent and sovereignty are therefore
downstream outcomes to measure, not assumptions attached to a project label.

## Bounded public discussion

Public discussion adds interpretation, concern, and proposed mechanisms:

| Platform | Signal | Analytical use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | An IEA relay frames AI infrastructure through electricity demand, grid queues, energy security, emissions, innovation, and affordability. | Connects compute to physical systems and household cost; it is a professional interpretation, not distributional data. |
| LinkedIn | A Taiwan semiconductor discussion treats allied fab investment as dependent on compliance, talent, permitting, suppliers, and technology-transfer coordination. | Adds the delivery and workforce layer to geopolitical capacity; investment commitments do not prove completed capacity. |
| LinkedIn | A data-center legitimacy relay links public concern to energy costs, water, land, pollution, jobs, transmission allocation, and host-community value. | Makes local benefit-sharing visible; commentary combines survey and advocacy claims. |
| LinkedIn | A poll relay reports cross-partisan opposition to new local data centers even alongside broader AI support. | Highlights national-support/local-consent tension; attitudes are not permitting or implementation outcomes. |

These signals suggest that people evaluate AI infrastructure through tangible
local costs and whether benefits are shared, not only through national
competitiveness. They do not establish the prevalence or causal basis of local
opposition.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not treat public-cloud completion as public-service improvement.
- Do not treat a migration target as completed migration.
- Do not treat MW capacity or an IFC loan as operating compute or domestic
  sovereignty.
- Do not treat national ownership as operational replaceability.
- Do not infer local consent from a project announcement or national AI support.
- Keep state agencies, operators, financiers, customers, workers, utilities,
  route communities, and citizens as separate actors.
- Public discussion is a legitimacy and mechanism layer, not a local sample.

## Stronger next test

Build one project-year capability ledger with:

1. contract, financing, ownership, operator, and delivery milestones;
2. physical capacity, utilization, uptime, energy/water use, and incident data;
3. applications or customers actually served, service quality, cost, and
   public-access outcomes;
4. local jobs, skills, procurement, land, environmental, and rate impacts;
5. portability, exit, substitute providers, foreign dependencies, and crisis
   response; and
6. permitting, public explanation, consent, complaint, political response, and
   later legitimacy.

Start with the Romanian public-cloud route if application migration and service
telemetry can be obtained; otherwise use the Malaysian campus for operating
availability and customer/utility incidence. Do not infer sovereignty from
location, financing, or ownership alone.

## Deeper question

**When does AI infrastructure give a state or community real capability and
choice, rather than a larger platform whose critical power, data, labor, and
operational dependencies remain elsewhere?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is complete only when one named project links infrastructure to
operation, served users, reliability, local incidence, substitute capability,
and public or geopolitical response—or when the source owner confirms the
missing fields cannot be obtained. The broader 14-theme program remains active.
