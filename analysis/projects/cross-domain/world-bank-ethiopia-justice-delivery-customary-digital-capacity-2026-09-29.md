# Ethiopia’s justice system shows that performance, access, and legitimacy can diverge

## Finding

The World Bank’s JUPITER assessment finds a justice system that can look efficient in administrative throughput while remaining difficult to access, slow from the user’s perspective, digitally incomplete, and institutionally uneven.

Federal courts had clearance rates above 95%, yet 67% of surveyed court users identified lengthy processes as discouraging them from bringing cases. The federal case-management system lacks a public-facing portal for filing, document sharing, and case follow-up. At the same time, customary justice is not peripheral: Oromia customary courts received more than 374,000 cases in 2023/2024, nearly half the caseload of the state court system.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`institutional throughput -> user-facing friction and geographic exclusion -> customary substitution or nonuse -> legitimacy and reform pressure`

The key shift is from “does the institution clear cases?” to “can people reach, understand, participate in, and trust the pathway that resolves their dispute?” Ethiopia’s plural system creates a possible access asset: customary institutions can provide lower-cost, community-proximate routes. But recognition must be paired with referral protocols, legal aid, legal information, quality safeguards, and links to statutory courts.

## Cultural, social, and political significance

- Administrative efficiency can coexist with practical exclusion when procedures are lengthy, federal courts are concentrated in two cities, and users cannot file or track cases remotely.
- Customary justice is social infrastructure as well as legal infrastructure; its legitimacy can derive from proximity and cultural familiarity, while its risks include uneven fairness, quality, and protection across communities.
- More than 30% of judge positions are empty at two federal court tiers, only 53% of Federal Supreme Court judicial-administration positions are occupied, and the judiciary’s budget is below 0.5% of the national budget.
- Legal fragmentation and weak access to current legal information make justice partly dependent on professional networks and the ability to obtain texts, counsel, and procedural knowledge.
- Digitalization can improve access, but only if designed around privacy, connectivity, disability access, digital literacy, and public-facing usability—not internal automation alone.

## Boundary and next test

This is a country assessment, not a causal evaluation of digitalization, customary-court recognition, staffing, budgets, or judicial independence. The next test is whether reforms improve fair resolution, user access, time, quality, and trust across statutory and customary pathways rather than merely increasing internal throughput.
