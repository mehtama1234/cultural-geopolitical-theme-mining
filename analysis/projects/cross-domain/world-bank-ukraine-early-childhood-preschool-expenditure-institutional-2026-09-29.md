# Ukraine’s preschool system turns demographic shock into a territorial care, labor, and recovery challenge

## Finding

Ukraine’s early-childhood and preschool system is not only an education service. It is part of the country’s care infrastructure, female labor-force capacity, human-capital recovery, and territorial settlement pattern.

Enrollment fell 31.1% between 2021 and 2024, from about 1.1 million children to 765,576. Participation among children ages 0–5 remains between 40% and 51%, with especially low participation among children ages 0–2. Hromadas with the highest enrollment serve nearly three times as many children as those with the lowest enrollment, while preschool occupancy ranges from about 52% to more than 100% across oblasts.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`war and demographic change -> locally uneven childcare demand -> rigid staffing and budget structures -> unequal access, women’s work constraints, and uneven recovery capacity`

The report’s central institutional insight is that Ukraine may be able to expand access within the current fiscal envelope, but only if preschool networks, staffing, and service models adapt to radically different local conditions. The binding problem is therefore not simply insufficient spending. It is the interaction between local fiscal capacity, inherited staffing norms, safety and displacement pressures, and limited ability to redirect resources toward demand.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- A child’s location increasingly determines whether a family encounters overcrowding, unmet demand, or underused preschool capacity.
- Childcare access becomes a labor-market infrastructure question: reliable ECPE can reduce family constraints on women’s work, while uneven access reproduces unequal household opportunity.
- More than half of ECPE staff are non-pedagogical, and fewer than half of pedagogical staff are classroom teachers; 64% of hromadas contain both an understaffed and an overstaffed preschool. This makes “staff shortage” partly a distribution and institutional-design problem.
- Local financing can improve responsiveness, but a nearly 50-fold spread in per-child spending shows how decentralization can convert fiscal capacity into unequal childhood conditions.
- The 2024 Preschool Law creates regulatory flexibility, but the politically consequential question is whether hromadas have the implementation capacity to use it.

## Boundary and next test

This is a public expenditure and institutional review, not a causal evaluation of wartime disruption, the Preschool Law, household welfare, child development, or labor-market outcomes. The next test is whether hromadas can use flexible staffing, pooled specialist roles, adaptive facilities, and more equitable funding formulas to convert excess capacity into safe, high-quality access without weakening care quality.
