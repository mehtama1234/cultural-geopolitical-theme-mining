# Teacher shortages expose education as a social-infrastructure and status bargain

## Finding

The OECD’s *Education at a Glance 2026* frames teacher shortage as more than a recruitment gap. Across OECD countries, 9.1% of primary and secondary teachers were not fully qualified in 2024/25, 6.4% of fully qualified teachers left during that school year, and shortages were more acute in rural, remote and disadvantaged communities and in mathematics and natural sciences. One in four secondary teachers is aged 55 or older.

The social-status layer is equally important: 85% of teachers report being satisfied with their jobs, but only 22% believe teaching is valued by society. On average, 68% of lower-secondary teachers are satisfied with employment terms excluding salary, while only 39% are satisfied with salary. This points to an occupational legitimacy problem that cannot be reduced to pay alone.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`skilled-labor scarcity + ageing workforce -> uneven teacher supply -> place- and subject-specific education access -> family and community opportunity divergence`

Teacher supply is social infrastructure. When qualified teachers are missing, the consequence is not only a staffing statistic: it changes the reliability of school relationships, the availability of advanced subjects, the burden on families, and the perceived future of remote or disadvantaged places.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- The profession’s public status is becoming part of education capacity. Teachers may remain attached to parts of the job while feeling that society does not value the work.
- “Shortage” is spatial and curricular: the same national system can preserve staffing in advantaged places while substituting less-qualified labor in remote schools or hard-to-staff subjects.
- Second-career pathways can widen entry by recognizing prior experience, but they also raise questions about training time, professional identity, and whether mid-career entrants are sent disproportionately to difficult schools.
- A projected 9% decline in the OECD 5–14 population by 2033 does not automatically solve shortages; reallocation, retirement, local politics, and school continuity determine whether demographic contraction creates capacity or abandonment.
- The OECD also flags a new uncertainty in the education-to-work bargain: between 2023 and 2025, unemployment among tertiary-educated 25–34-year-olds rose while it fell among those with upper-secondary attainment. The report explicitly treats this as too early to interpret as a lasting shift.

## Boundary and next test

This is comparative indicator and survey evidence, not causal proof that salary increases, AI tools, second-career pathways, or teacher reallocation improve learning, retention, equity or trust. The next test is a named education-system episode that follows staffing policy into teacher workload and stability, subject and place access, family burden, student learning, and durable community confidence.
