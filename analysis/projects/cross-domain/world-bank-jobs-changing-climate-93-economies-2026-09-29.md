# Climate adaptation becomes a labour-market conversion problem when resilience investment changes who can work, where, and under what conditions

## Finding

The World Bank’s *Jobs in a Changing Climate* synthesis draws on Country Climate and Development Reports covering 93 economies, representing 64% of the population and 77% of GDP of low- and middle-income countries. It estimates the equivalent of **43 million job losses by 2050 across 49 analyzed countries**, rising to 260 million when extrapolated across all low- and middle-income countries. It also estimates that adaptation investment could generate the equivalent of 25 million more and better-paid jobs in those 49 countries, or up to 150 million when extrapolated globally.

The distinct finding is not simply that climate change creates or destroys jobs. It is that resilience is a conversion problem: adaptation only becomes broadly protective when capital, human skills, business conditions, worker reallocation, and social protection connect the people losing work to the places and sectors receiving investment.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`climate exposure -> sectoral and regional job change -> adaptation and low-emission investment -> reskilling, reallocation, firm adaptation, and protection -> household income, place viability, and political legitimacy`

Aggregate employment can look stable while workers experience a sharp transition. A new adaptation sector may create jobs far from a declining agricultural, fossil, or climate-exposed livelihood; a firm may gain resilience while a household absorbs relocation, retraining, energy, or care costs. The report’s emphasis on human capital and worker reallocation therefore connects climate policy to household room, place attachment, firm legitimacy, and the politics of who is protected.

The geopolitical layer is the allocation of adaptation finance and development capacity. Countries with weaker fiscal space, infrastructure, skills systems, and bargaining power may face larger losses while capturing fewer emerging opportunities. The global extrapolation is useful as a scale signal but should not be read as a country forecast.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- “Green jobs” become credible only when workers can actually reach them through skills, mobility, recognition, and protection.
- Climate resilience becomes a household issue through income continuity, energy costs, relocation, care, and regional service viability.
- Firms and governments may be judged not only by emissions or investment, but by whether transition costs are shared and visible.
- Aggregate job gains can coexist with regional decline, skill mismatch, wage loss, or political backlash.
- Adaptation infrastructure is also a social contract: its legitimacy depends on who receives safety, work, voice, and remedy.

## Boundary and next test

The World Bank estimates are modelled and partly extrapolated; they do not establish realized jobs, wages, job quality, or fair distribution. The next test is a named country or sector episode tracing climate investment from financing and procurement through worker transition, firm adaptation, household income, regional services, and political response.
