# AI in education, learning, teacher agency, and equity audit

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** student AI use, teacher agency, classroom devices, learning transfer,
school capacity, digital inequality, and public-purpose governance

## Question

When AI and digital devices improve the speed or quality of schoolwork, do they
create durable learning and wider opportunity—or make output look better while
moving cognitive work, oversight, and implementation risk away from schools
with the least capacity?

```text
tool access and school capacity
  -> teacher/student use and task completion
  -> reasoning, feedback, and independent demonstration
  -> learning, confidence, attendance, and progression
  -> teacher workload/control and school legitimacy
  -> equity, correction, public accountability, or exit
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Comparable evidence | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adoption is ahead of integration | An NBER national principal survey finds rapid K–12 AI spread: students mainly use AI for homework and writing, while educators use it for lesson planning and administrative tasks. Teacher training, student-use guidance, and school policies lag adoption. A one-standard-deviation increase in student disadvantage is associated with a 0.07–0.11 standard-deviation lower school AI-integration index; district size explains about one-third of the public-school disadvantage gap. | National survey of K–12 principals; associations and decomposition. | The abstract does not disclose full sample, response rate, or causal identification. The index is not learning, safety, workload, or welfare. Lower integration may be deliberate caution rather than deprivation. |
| Teachers see a control problem in everyday devices | Gallup reports 96% of U.S. public-school teachers say students use learning devices in class, while 61% prefer less device time. Fifty-nine percent say tools help students learn more effectively, but 52% see equal harm and good and 29% more harm than good. | National probability-based panel of public-school teachers, May 6–29, 2026. | Perceptions do not measure minutes, achievement, behavior, or causal screen effects; “devices” covers different tools and tasks. |
| Operational control is associated with greater legitimacy | Gallup reports 67% of teachers with a lot of device oversight say digital tools help students learn effectively, versus 49% among those with little or no oversight—a descriptive 18-point gap. Strong support is higher for print/hands-on alternatives (58%) and filtering/monitoring (54%) than for a daily device maximum (34%). | Cross-sectional subgroup comparison and policy-preference survey. | Oversight may proxy for staffing, class size, training, grade, school policy, or product quality; it is not a causal effect. |
| Task performance can diverge from learning | OECD reports 37% of lower-secondary teachers used AI for work in 2024 and 57% agreed it helps write or improve lesson plans, while 72% believed AI can harm academic integrity. Its synthesis finds general-purpose GenAI can raise student output quality without learning gains; some performance advantages disappear or reverse when AI access is removed. | OECD Digital Education Outlook synthesis with TALIS 2024 and emerging education studies. | The evidence is heterogeneous and does not provide one universal effect size or prove long-term harm. |
| Pedagogical design can change the result | OECD finds education-specific GenAI used with intentional pedagogy is associated with more sustained learning improvements, and reports evidence that inexperienced tutors can improve tutoring quality and student learning with educational GenAI. | Synthesis of emerging studies and education-specific tools. | Examples do not establish equal effectiveness across subjects, languages, ages, schools, or products. |
| Personal contact can change take-up without a universal engagement effect | In three randomized Chicago Public Schools trials, personalized phone calls increased registration for virtual summer school. Weekly texts had no effect on summer days absent or Khan Academy use but improved a secondary math-course outcome. An instructional aide had no effect on primary summer attendance or platform use but had beneficial following-year math outcomes. | Three field RCTs during the remote-learning period with pre-specified primary and later outcomes. | Results are setting-specific; registration, platform use, attendance, and later grades are different endpoints. Family burden and long-run attainment are not established. |
| Teen use is widespread but verification is uneven | Google’s 2026 teen report says 94% of U.S. teens ages 13–17 used an AI tool in the prior year, 74% used AI for learning weekly, and 93% took at least one step to evaluate information. Yet only 50% regularly considered trustworthiness, 55% cross-checked facts, and 46% checked website or author credibility. | Sponsor-produced survey with state studies, focus groups, and digital journaling. | Reported use and evaluation are not behavioral traces, verified fact-checking, learning transfer, or equal access; the public materials describe inconsistent headline sample sizes. |
| Governance is a public-purpose question, not only a product question | UNESCO frames AI education governance around human agency, equity, inclusion, safety, ethics, digital sovereignty, age appropriateness, procurement, total cost, and the right to an explanation. Its 2026 consultation runs September–November, with policy briefs planned for Digital Learning Week 2027. | Normative discussion paper, consultation, and collection of 26 think pieces. | This is agenda-setting, not implementation, learning, public-attitude, or causal evidence; consultation participation may not be representative. |

## What we found

The evidence supports a **performance-to-learning and control gap**. AI can help
with planning, registration, tutoring, or the production of a polished answer.
Those gains do not automatically show that students learned, teachers gained
time, or disadvantaged schools gained equal capacity.

Four stages must remain separate:

1. **Access:** a student, teacher, or school can use a tool.
2. **Task performance:** a lesson plan, essay, registration, or platform action
   improves.
3. **Learning and agency:** the student can explain, transfer, question, and
   demonstrate the work without the tool; the teacher can shape the task.
4. **Institutional equity:** the school has training, oversight, alternatives,
   privacy, procurement control, and a way to correct or refuse the system.

NBER and Google show adoption and uneven school or student capacity. OECD and
Gallup show why task quality, learning, and teacher control can diverge. The
Chicago trials show that personal outreach and human support can matter more
than generic digital engagement. UNESCO supplies the governance test. None of
these sources follows the same student from AI use through independent learning,
teacher workload, progression, and later opportunity.

## Curiosity checks

### Where did the pattern fail?

- More AI integration is not automatically better; NBER's disadvantage gap can
  reflect deliberate caution or a different school mission.
- Weekly texts and an instructional aide did not improve their primary summer
  engagement outcomes, even though later or secondary math outcomes improved in
  parts of the Chicago trials.
- A student who checks one source may still fail to judge trustworthiness deeply;
  93% taking one evaluation step coexists with only 50% doing so regularly.
- Teachers favor filtering, monitoring, and hands-on alternatives more strongly
  than a blunt daily time cap, suggesting that task and context matter.

### What else could explain the differences?

1. **Capacity explanation:** training, district support, teacher control, and
   infrastructure determine whether AI becomes a learning aid.
2. **Task-design explanation:** output improves when a task rewards drafting but
   learning improves only when explanation, feedback, reflection, and independent
   demonstration remain visible.
3. **Selection explanation:** schools and students choosing more AI may differ in
   resources, motivation, policy, or prior achievement; adoption alone is not
   the cause of the result.

### What would change the answer?

A school-level longitudinal study or trial that measures AI exposure, teacher
control, task output, independent assessment without AI, learning transfer,
teacher time, student subgroup, privacy incidents, and correction routes would
show whether the observed gap is temporary implementation friction or a durable
distributional pattern.

| Platform | Signal | Limit |

| LinkedIn | A relay of the NBER K–12 paper links fast adoption to lower integration in more disadvantaged schools and highlights training, guidance, teacher champions, and policy scaffolding. | Selected professional discussion; associations do not prove district capacity causes the gap. |
| LinkedIn | UNESCO frames AI education as a common-good and public-accountability question, keeping teacher and learner rights visible. | Institutional statement, not classroom implementation or learning evidence. |
| LinkedIn | A practitioner discussion of teacher wellbeing stresses protected planning time and system workload rather than individual efficiency. | Practitioner framing is not a representative teacher sample or retention outcome. |

The discussion layer adds social meaning: teachers and learners are not only
users of a tool; they are asking who controls the classroom, who carries the
time cost, and whether education remains a shared public institution. It does
not establish prevalence or learning effects.

## Stronger next test

Follow one student, teacher, or school episode across the full route:

```text
need, task, and available support
  -> AI/device access, policy, and teacher control
  -> assisted output and human feedback
  -> independent assessment, transfer, and learning
  -> time, workload, privacy, and subgroup effects
  -> correction, alternative route, progression, trust, or exit
```

Compare high- and low-capacity schools, approved and unapproved tools,
AI-assisted and no-AI tasks, and students with different language, disability,
income, and home-support conditions. Preserve cases where AI reduces teacher
burden or improves learning, as well as cases where it improves output while
weakening independent performance.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** AI and devices are widespread, but learning, teacher
agency, and school equity are not automatic results of access. Human support,
pedagogical design, oversight, and institutional capacity appear to determine
whether technology becomes learning infrastructure or another uneven gate.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call AI in education beneficial, harmful, or equitable from adoption,
task output, device use, teacher attitudes, or policy principles alone. Require
a compatible school or student sequence with tool use, teacher control,
independent learning, subgroup conditions, correction or alternative access,
and a later progression or disengagement outcome, plus a counterexample.
