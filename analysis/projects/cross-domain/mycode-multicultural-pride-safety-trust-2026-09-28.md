# My Code Culture 360: pride, safety, recognition, and political trust

## Finding

My Code’s Wave 15 Culture 360 tracker adds a cultural-political distinction that is easy to miss in toplines: national pride is broadly shared, while perceptions of community safety are not. Among voters concerned about political violence, Hispanic, Black, and AANHPI respondents were more likely than non-multicultural respondents to say their own community faced greater risk. The same release also separates feeling understood from trust: recognition may open candidate consideration without reliably creating confidence.

## Evidence

- Wave 15 surveyed 3,219 U.S. eligible voters ages 18–64 online in July 2026; results were weighted, with subgroup bases reported in the release.
- Roughly three-quarters across communities reported pride in being American; 53% expressed concern about political violence.
- Among those concerned about political violence, 60% of Hispanic, 52% of Black, and 46% of AANHPI respondents said their community faced greater risk, compared with 38% of non-multicultural respondents.
- Feeling understood would increase consideration for 48% of Black voters and trust for 42%; among non-multicultural voters, the corresponding figures were 60% and 56%.
- The tracker’s reduced repeat-choice share splits into 44% repeating the 2024 choice, 19% not voting, 15% unsure, and 10% voting differently.

## Interpretation and limits

The useful signal is not that one group is “persuadable” or that pride is fake. It is that shared symbolic identity does not erase unequal risk perception, and cultural recognition is an intermediate step rather than a trust outcome. The source is sponsor-produced cultural intelligence; it reports weighted online survey findings and stated intentions, not verified safety, turnout, or causal campaign effects.
