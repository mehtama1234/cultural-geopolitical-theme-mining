# Tariffs become political through the price, value, and trust experiences of consumers

## Finding

The 28 September 2026 Ipsos Consumer Tracker shows tariffs becoming a lived consumer issue rather than an abstract trade instrument. Eighty-one percent of Americans surveyed agreed tariffs would raise prices on goods they buy, and 71% said they had already seen prices rise specifically because of tariffs. Those figures were higher than the comparable Ipsos readings in January 2025 and February 2025.

The deeper theme is a tariff-to-consumer-politics conversion gap. A policy becomes politically meaningful when households interpret it through receipts, product value, substitution, safety, and the credibility of public institutions. The tracker does not establish the causal share of price changes produced by tariffs or whether respondents changed purchases or votes; it shows the perception and attribution layer that can precede those outcomes.

## Cross-domain interpretation

```text
trade policy or tariff
  -> price visibility and product-value judgment
  -> substitution, delay, quantity, quality, or brand switching
  -> household room and perceived fairness
  -> trust in firms and public safety institutions
  -> political attribution and policy support or backlash
```

The same wave connects tariff perceptions with cost-per-wear, food-inspection trust, community safety, and consumer technology questions. That combination suggests a broader consumer legitimacy test: people evaluate public and private systems through whether they can explain costs, preserve value, and provide credible protection.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Trade policy is increasingly experienced through ordinary price and value decisions.
- Cost-per-wear language shows consumers adapting cultural ideas of value, durability, and responsible purchasing under pressure.
- Food-safety and community-safety trust show that consumer legitimacy includes protection systems, not only prices.
- Firms may face pressure to explain price changes and demonstrate fairness, but consumer perception is not proof of firm conduct.
- The decisive next step is linking tariff perceptions to actual substitution, quantity, debt, savings, brand switching, complaints, and political judgment.

## Boundary and next test

The Ipsos source is an online recurring tracker and public summary. It does not provide a product-level price index, causal tariff incidence, complete subgroup tables, observed purchases, inspection outcomes, or voting behavior. The next test should follow a named product category or household panel from tariff exposure through prices, substitution, household room, institutional trust, and political attribution.

Source: [Ipsos Consumer Tracker](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/news-polls/consumer-behavior-time-covid-19).
