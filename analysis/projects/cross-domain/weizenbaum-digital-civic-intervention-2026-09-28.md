# Weizenbaum 2026: digital hostility, civic intervention, and perceived democratic threat

## Finding

The 2026 Weizenbaum Report adds a comparative information-politics mechanism to the program: online hate and misinformation are not only trust problems or exposure measures. They are becoming ordinary civic conditions, and people respond through different age-coded repertoires. Younger Germans report especially high exposure and use platform reporting more; older respondents more often use direct interpersonal intervention. Separately, people who see violence against politicians as widespread report more demonstrations, politician contact, and petition signing. The source therefore leaves open a mobilization route alongside the expected withdrawal route.

## Evidence

- The 2025 Weizenbaum Panel surveyed 1,740 people in Germany aged 16 and older by telephone, with about 73% returning from the prior wave.
- 47% reported seeing misinformation in the prior 12 months; 32% fact-checked it, 25% warned others, and 12% reported it to a platform.
- Nearly 90% of 16–29-year-olds reported seeing hateful comments and up to 80% reported encountering misinformation.
- Around 81% viewed threats and violence against politicians as widespread. Among those respondents, 18% reported demonstrating in the prior year, versus 12% among people who did not view it as widespread.

## Interpretation and limits

The useful theme is not “young people are exposed” or “violence mobilizes” as a universal claim. It is that digital disorder may redistribute civic labor across platforms, peers, and formal politics, while perceived threat can coexist with participation. The report is descriptive and cross-sectional; it does not identify causal effects, objective exposure, or platform effectiveness.
