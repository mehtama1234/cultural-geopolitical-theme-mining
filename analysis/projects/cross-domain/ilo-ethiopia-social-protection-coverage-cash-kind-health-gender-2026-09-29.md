# Ethiopia’s social-protection expansion is uneven: meals and health coverage grow while cash security remains thin

## Finding

The ILO’s Ethiopia baseline shows a social-protection system expanding through specific services without yet producing broad income security. Community-Based Health Insurance reaches about 40.7% of the population, and school feeding reached 7.6 million children in 2024/25. But only 5.8% of the population is covered by a cash social-protection benefit, leaving 94.2% without cash protection.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`food and health programmes scale -> visible service protection -> cash and life-course gaps persist -> uneven household room and shock resilience`

The distinction matters because meals, insurance, public works, pensions, maternity benefits, and cash transfers protect households through different channels. A country can demonstrate real social-policy expansion while still leaving informal workers, older women, people with disabilities, and households facing income shocks without portable income support.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- School feeding reached 7,597,334 students in 2024, up from about 2.06 million in 2019/20; its home-grown model connects children’s welfare to local farmers, community labor, and mothers’ unpaid work.
- Only 4.2% of people over 60 receive a pension, with coverage of 7.1% for older men versus 1.8% for older women.
- Disability protection reaches only 0.3% of people with severe disabilities and 0.1% of women with severe disabilities.
- Social-protection coverage is partly a data-governance problem: fragmented registries make it difficult to know who is covered, coordinate agencies, track maternity benefits, or evaluate gaps in real time.
- The politics of protection increasingly concerns benefit composition: whether people receive cash, food, health-cost reduction, work opportunities, or only nominal registration in a programme.

## Boundary and next test

This is a national fact-finding baseline, not a causal evaluation or benefit-adequacy study. The next test is whether broader cash protection, integrated registries, domestic financing, and locally owned programmes improve realized resilience and equitable access without sacrificing privacy or excluding people poorly represented in administrative data.
