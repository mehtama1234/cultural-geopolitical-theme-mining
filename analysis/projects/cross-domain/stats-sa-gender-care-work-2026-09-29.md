# South Africa Gender Series XIII: care is a gendered conversion of time into economic room

## Finding

Statistics South Africa’s Gender Series XIII shows that care is not only a
private family value or a service sector. It is a conversion system linking
demographic dependency, household employment, school participation, paid-care
occupations, earnings, expenditure and basic infrastructure.

The official summary reports that the share of people aged 65 and older rose
from 5.9% in 2015 to 7.3% in 2026, while the old-age dependency ratio rose from
7.9 to 9.1 between 2015 and 2025. Female-headed households increased from
41.3% to 42.6% between 2015 and 2025 and were more likely to have no employed
member. Among children aged 7–18 not attending an educational institution,
8.4% of females cited family commitments as the main reason, compared with 0%
of males in the reported comparison.

Women also dominated several paid-care occupations, including domestic work,
nursing, teaching and social work. Infrastructure changes matter because
collecting water, cooking, cleaning and maintaining a healthy environment are
care work: 69.8% of female-headed households spent less than 30 minutes
fetching water compared with 79.6% of male-headed households, while stream or
river collection was more common among female-headed households.

The cross-source implication is that care infrastructure expands or narrows
optionality through time. A household may gain formal water access, but the
distribution of remaining care, education interruption, paid work and income
security can remain gendered. The report does not tell us how many hours were
provided, who made decisions inside households, or whether infrastructure
improvements produced durable employment or wellbeing gains.

## Boundary

The report uses available official statistics and proxy indicators because
recent time-use data are unavailable. The evidence is therefore a thematic
statistical synthesis, not a respondent-level time-use estimate or causal
evaluation. It does not establish that care caused non-attendance, non-
employment, occupational sorting or earnings differences.
