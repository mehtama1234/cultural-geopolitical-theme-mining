# Platform and data control: formal exits are expanding faster than verified outcomes

**Rotation date:** 2026-09-29  
**Scope:** platform agency, privacy, consumer recourse, attention, and practical exit  
**Status:** cross-source interpretation; no pooled estimate

## Question

When people are told they can delete data, opt out, disable collection, or
leave a platform, how much control is actually usable and verifiable?

## What the sources show

- California's DROP system creates a centralized deletion and opt-out route for
  participating data brokers. It uses hashed information, a consumer-facing
  status vocabulary, and a broker processing clock. The route makes control
  more observable, but the public implementation record does not show any named
  consumer's successful deletion, durable removal, exemption review, or later
  trust or switching outcome.
- The FTC's GM/OnStar order requires processes for connected-vehicle data-copy,
  deletion, opt-out, and disabling precise geolocation where technology allows.
  GM's published controls retain safety, emergency, vehicle-transfer, and
  technology-specific exceptions, while request volume, completion, residual
  collection, and service trade-offs remain unobserved.
- The broader digital-attention comparison shows high platform and chatbot
  reach alongside privacy concern, civic exposure, and FTC scrutiny. These are
  separate populations and endpoints, not evidence that reach caused harm or
  that scrutiny produced remedy.
- A U.S. AI-assistant choice study finds task-specific switching and substantial
  concern about conversation-data use, while knowledge of training practices is
  more closely associated with protective action than concern alone. This
  suggests that privacy control depends on usable information and tools, not
  only on stated worry.

## Interpretation

The platform-control problem is moving from “is there a policy?” to “can a
person complete, verify, and sustain the control without giving up an essential
service?”

Three gaps recur:

1. **Formal control versus successful completion.** A deletion button, request
   form, or opt-out rule is a route, not proof that data were deleted or stopped
   circulating.
2. **Exit versus service sacrifice.** Turning off location, leaving a connected
   service, or using a privacy-protective assistant may reduce safety,
   navigation, convenience, or personalization.
3. **Visibility versus power.** Status labels and disclosure can make the system
   legible while leaving the user unable to challenge an exemption, downstream
   transfer, or repeated collection.

This is a direct platform version of the atlas's broader unequal-optionality
finding: people with knowledge, time, technical skill, and alternative services
can exercise formal rights more effectively than people who cannot absorb the
cost of verification or exit.

## What this does not establish

These records do not establish population-wide deletion success, privacy harm,
platform dependence, causal trust change, or durable exit. Firm orders,
implementation descriptions, surveys, and cross-source comparisons must remain
separate evidence types.

## Next decisive tests

- Obtain an authorized implementation or administrative record preserving
  request, identity verification, response, completion, exception, and later
  reappearance of data for the same account or request.
- Measure service trade-offs when a user disables collection or exits.
- Follow a complaint from exposure through explanation, correction, remedy,
  switching, and later trust.
- Compare control completion by age, income, disability, language, digital
  skill, and dependence on the service.

The key open arrow is **formal control → usable completion → verified durable
change → service trade-off → trust, switching, or exit**.
