# Displacement, host capacity, livelihood conversion, and belonging audit

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** forced displacement, returns, host systems, aid, work, food, land,
services, legal status, public attitudes, and social belonging

## Question

When displacement becomes protracted, do humanitarian and development systems
convert protection into safe livelihoods, services, belonging, and durable
choice—or do falling aid and weak host capacity move the burden into food debt,
inactivity, exclusion, and contested public legitimacy?

```text
conflict, disaster, or forced movement
  -> protection, status, shelter, and host location
  -> food, health, education, land, work, finance, and mobility
  -> household coping and host-community capacity
  -> social belonging, public legitimacy, return, integration, or onward movement
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Comparable evidence | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| The global stock remains vast but changed | UNHCR estimates 117.83 million forcibly displaced people at end-2025: 41.6 million refugees, 9 million asylum-seekers with pending decisions, and 68.7 million conflict/violence IDPs. The total fell from the 2024 peak; 14.7 million people were reported to return during 2025. | UNHCR compilation of official government, UNHCR, partner, and IDMC statistics. | Categories can overlap; reporting quality varies. Return is not safety, voluntariness, durable reintegration, or no re-displacement. |
| Hosting is concentrated where capacity is often constrained | UNHCR reports 68% of refugees and other people in need of international protection hosted by low- and middle-income countries and 65% living in neighboring countries. Its 2026 appeal seeks $8.505 billion across 128 countries/territories, while the appeal's larger planning projection is not the same as the end-2025 stock. | Global location and planning measures. | Host share describes location, not burden per person, service quality, fiscal capacity, or community attitudes; appeal need is not funding received or outcome. |
| Aid contraction reaches both refugees and hosts | A World Bank/UNHCR/CEGA two-wave Kenya panel reports camp-refugee employment falling from 13% to 7% and urban refugee employment from 50% to 37% between 2022 and 2024; camp-adjacent host employment fell from 35% to 33%. Sixty-four percent of inactive camp refugees cited lack of jobs, up from 41%. | Longitudinal socioeconomic panel summarized by the World Bank; refugee and host settings. | The feature does not isolate aid cuts from food prices, weather, legal restrictions, skills, care, or local demand; settings are not all refugee-hosting economies. |
| Food and credit become coping channels | In the Kenya panel, 64% of camp households reported reducing food consumption and 44% relied on credit to buy food in 2024; aid represented about 75% of camp-refugee total revenue. Less than 1% held a work permit in the first wave. | Panel-based household coping and administrative/contextual synthesis. | Coping prevalence is not a complete welfare measure; credit, community help, remittances, and informal work may buffer some households, but relative protection is not estimated. |
| Productive inputs do not automatically become self-reliance | Uganda's Q2 2026 UNHCR dashboard reports $27.8 million received, 18% of the stated funding requirement, 9,723 households receiving agricultural inputs/assets, 8,128 receiving market-driven skills training, 2,824 graduates linked to employers, and 7,560 businesses receiving training. | Partner ActivityInfo operational reporting; funding and output indicators. | Training and linkages are not retained employment, income, profit, food security, or durable household room; partners and activities may overlap. |
| Land and formal systems constrain conversion | Uganda reports 5,553 acres accessed, averaging 0.6 acres per supported household versus a programme benchmark of 1.2 acres for self-reliance; formal registration reached 279 businesses and 1,895 village savings groups formed. Documentation, KYC rules, formal credit, and formal employment remain barriers. | UNHCR dashboard and sector narrative. | The 1.2-acre figure is a planning benchmark, not a universal causal threshold; land quality, household size, markets, water, security, and nonfarm income are not captured. Registration and savings groups do not prove survival or credit access. |
| Migration can be development infrastructure or unequal risk | IOM estimates 304 million international migrants by mid-2024 and more than 83 million people living in internal displacement at end-2024. It frames work, remittances, investment, skills, civic engagement, regular pathways, climate mobility, and local action as linked development routes. | IOM global compilation and thematic synthesis. | Stocks and frameworks do not establish individual safety, pathway access, causal development effects, or whether gains reach origin, host, and migrant households equally. |
| Protection support and confidence can coexist with suspicion | Ipsos/UNHCR reports 66% agree people should be able to seek refuge from war or persecution, but 61% believe many claims are not genuine, 49% support closing borders entirely, and 44% believe refugees will integrate successfully. In a conflict scenario, 28% prefer humanitarian aid, 24% diplomacy, 19% temporary asylum, and 6% permanent acceptance. | 2026 cross-national public-opinion survey and scenario questions. | Attitudes are not refugee flows, asylum decisions, willingness to host/pay, or service capacity; scenario answers may change under actual arrivals and local pressure. |

## What we found

The strongest pattern is a **protection-to-capacity conversion gap**. Displacement
counts, return counts, funding plans, training, land access, and public support
are all meaningful, but none alone shows that a person can safely build a life.

The route has at least five distinct endpoints:

1. **Protection:** legal recognition, safety, shelter, and non-refoulement.
2. **Usable life:** food, health, education, land, work, finance, transport, and
   documentation.
3. **Household resilience:** reduced negative coping, income, care, and recovery
   after a shock.
4. **Host legitimacy:** services and opportunities remain usable for host
   communities as well as displaced people.
5. **Durable choice:** safe return, local integration, resettlement, or onward
   movement is genuinely possible rather than forced by deprivation.

Kenya shows that aid and labor-market contraction can affect refugees and nearby
hosts together. Uganda shows that training and assets can be delivered while
land, funding, documentation, finance, and buyers remain too thin for self-
reliance. UNHCR's global appeal explicitly moves toward development and national
systems, but its own planning architecture does not prove that accountability
and funding connect across every stage.

## Curiosity checks

### What happened after returns or programme delivery?

The global return count rose while UNHCR warns that damaged homes, weak services,
infrastructure, and limited economic opportunity can make reintegration fragile.
Uganda's outputs show activity conversion stops before income, survival, or food
security. The current evidence therefore supports movement or delivery, not
durable recovery.

### Where did the pattern fail?

- Neighboring-country hosting can preserve family and cultural ties and may make
  return or cross-border support easier; host concentration is not automatically
  a failure.
- Smaller landholdings can support intensive farming or nonfarm livelihoods;
  acreage alone is not destiny.
- Aid contraction may encourage useful self-reliance where work authorization,
  capital, mobility, and demand are real.
- Support for the right to seek refuge can coexist with skepticism about claims;
  this may reflect demand for credible rules rather than simple rejection.
- A fall in displacement stocks can reflect return, integration, resettlement, or
  under-reporting; it cannot be read as one humanitarian outcome.

### What else could explain the observed stress?

1. **Resource explanation:** reduced aid, food prices, climate shocks, and weak
   local demand directly narrow household room.
2. **Institutional explanation:** legal status, work permits, documentation, KYC,
   mobility rules, and fragmented services prevent available resources becoming
   usable opportunity.
3. **Measurement explanation:** employment and return changes may partly reflect
   movement between locations, informal work, survey composition, or reporting
   changes rather than permanent exclusion or recovery.

### What would change the answer?

A host-place longitudinal ledger linking status, location, aid, services, work
authorization, land/credit, income, food and health outcomes, discrimination,
host-community conditions, and later return/integration choices would show whether
the system creates self-reliance or only shifts responsibility.

## Bounded public discussion

| Platform | Signal | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | The Joint Data Center discussion connects displacement statistics to inclusion and self-reliance, asking whether refugees are visible as participants in national systems. | Institutional discussion; statistical inclusion is not service or livelihood success. |
| LinkedIn | The UNHCR Global Appeal discussion makes funding, protection, and host-system capacity visible across more than 130 countries. | Professional interpretation without allocation or outcome data. |
| LinkedIn | IOM presents safe pathways, regional cooperation, and data as ways migration can support jobs and social cohesion. | Positive institutional framing; it does not resolve admission, rights, distribution, or local political conflict. |
| Reddit | An Australian settlement discussion describes unwritten social norms, workplace culture, communication, and everyday etiquette as additional settlement work beyond formal language and employment. | One self-selected discussion; Australia-specific and not a representative migrant account. |

The discussion layer adds a cultural point: belonging is not only legal status or
income. It can involve social fluency, recognition, safety, and whether people
are treated as contributors rather than temporary burdens. The posts do not
establish prevalence or causality.

## Stronger next test

Follow one displaced household and one comparable host household in the same
place across the full route:

```text
status and arrival/return
  -> shelter, documentation, services, land, work and finance
  -> food, health, care, income, debt and social contact
  -> host-market and public-system response
  -> discrimination, belonging, complaint, mobility, return, or settlement
```

Compare camps, integrated settlements, urban areas, and host neighborhoods;
preserve gender, disability, age, legal-status, and household-size differences.
Keep return, local integration, onward movement, and nonmovement separate.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** Protection and humanitarian planning remain necessary, but
the evidence shows that aid, training, land, work, and legal status often fail to
convert into durable livelihoods and household security when host capacity and
pathways are weak. Public support for protection is real but conditional on
credible rules, integration, and shared responsibility.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call displacement response successful, failed, integrated, or durable from
stock counts, return counts, funding requirements, training outputs, employment
shares, or public attitudes alone. Require a compatible host-place episode with
protection, usable services, work/food/health outcomes, host conditions, and a
later choice or recovery outcome, plus a counterexample.
