# Regional integration becomes real when systems interoperate: the hidden politics of making African markets work together

## Finding

The World Bank's *Integrating Africa: From Threads to Hubs* reframes African integration. The central bottleneck is not simply that African countries trade too little or that tariffs remain high. It is that customs, standards, transport, payments, energy, finance, digital platforms and data do not reliably work together across borders.

The report estimates that about 60 percent of African trade costs arise from unilateral or behind-the-border sources such as customs inefficiency, regulatory misalignment, logistics, transit fragmentation and poor infrastructure. Its strongest concept is interoperability: an integration agreement is meaningful only when data, money, goods, standards, institutions and firms can actually move through shared interfaces.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`continental agreement -> domestic and corridor interfaces -> interoperable movement of goods, money, data and services -> regional production scale -> jobs, consumer access and political legitimacy`

The framework has four pillars: build regional production networks; reduce trade frictions through interoperable systems; deepen and enforce agreements; and deliver regional public goods. The report assigns different work to different scales: national governments fix domestic barriers, Regional Economic Communities deliver coordination and shared systems, and the African Union/AfCFTA provide continental standards, legal coherence and dispute settlement.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and geopolitical significance

- Regional integration is experienced materially through whether a truck crosses a border, a payment settles, a permit is recognised, an insurance claim is honoured, a power pool supplies electricity, or a trader can understand the rules.
- Interoperability is a form of regional belonging. It converts a political identity—“African market”—into repeatable expectations about movement, recognition, payment and remedy.
- Behind-the-border costs make sovereignty visible as everyday friction. National control over customs, standards, transport and data can protect local discretion while also keeping neighbouring economies economically distant.
- Regional public goods reveal a collective-action problem: the benefits cross borders, but individual states may not finance or govern the shared infrastructure, standards, surveillance, insurance and digital interfaces needed to produce them.
- The politics of scale is central. Continental institutions can provide coherence, but Regional Economic Communities may be more legitimate and capable delivery platforms; smaller states and civil society need representation so integration is not experienced as an anchor-state or technocratic project.
- Consumer welfare and worker welfare enter through the same systems. Better corridors and interoperable payments may lower prices and expand choice, but compliance burdens, data requirements and corridor concentration can exclude informal traders or shift gains to larger firms.

## Boundary and next test

This is a World Bank analytical and policy synthesis, not causal evidence that any integration reform will work. The next test is a named corridor, power-pool, payment, insurance, customs or standards episode that follows formal agreement into actual interoperability, clearance time, cost, prices, firm entry, worker effects, household access, complaint/remedy and political trust.
