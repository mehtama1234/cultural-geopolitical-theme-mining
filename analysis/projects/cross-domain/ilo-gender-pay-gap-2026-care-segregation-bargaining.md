# The gender-pay gap narrows too slowly because equal pay is a care, work, and bargaining system

## Finding

New ILO estimates report that women still earn around 18% less than men globally, with the gap narrowing by only two percentage points since the previous estimates in 2018. The aggregate number is not just a wage-setting problem. The ILO links it to employment access, occupational segregation, career progression, unpaid care, discrimination, and the undervaluation of women’s work.

The distinct theme is a conversion gap between formal equal-pay commitment and lived economic power. Pay transparency, job evaluation, wage policy, and legal enforcement matter, but so do care systems, social protection, collective bargaining, and who has voice over how work is valued.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`unequal care and labour-market access -> occupational sorting and progression barriers -> undervaluation and bargaining asymmetry -> gender-pay gap -> household room, security, and economic voice -> transparency, collective bargaining, care, and social-protection reform`

The ILO reports variation across income groups: the gap narrowed by roughly 2.5 percentage points in high-income countries, 2 points in lower-middle-income countries, and 1 point in upper-middle-income countries over the past decade, while some countries saw no change or widening. This variation is a reminder that “global progress” is not a single social experience and that country-specific labour-market institutions matter.

The policy route is deliberately institutional. Employers and workers need pay transparency and gender-neutral job evaluation; governments need enforceable equality law and wage policy; social partners need bargaining power; and households need care and social-protection systems that do not make caregiving a private penalty. The test is whether these institutions alter actual pay, progression, hours, care allocation, and security—not only whether they produce equal-pay commitments.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Equal pay is also a struggle over which work is valued, measured, and made visible.
- Unpaid care becomes a distributional mechanism linking family time to wages and progression.
- Pay transparency can reveal inequality, but bargaining and enforcement determine whether exposure becomes correction.
- Collective bargaining is an economic-voice institution, not only a workplace procedure.
- Care systems and social protection are part of wage equality because they shape who can remain, advance, and negotiate in paid work.

## Boundary and next test

The source provides global estimates and a policy diagnosis, not a country-level causal decomposition or worker panel. It does not establish which reform works, who pays, or whether formal transparency changes realized pay. The next test is a named occupation, employer, or sector followed through pay-setting, care time, hours, progression, bargaining, transparency, enforcement, and household outcomes.
