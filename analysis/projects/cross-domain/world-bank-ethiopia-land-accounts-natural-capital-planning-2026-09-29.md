# Land accounts turn environmental change into a public-planning and accountability problem

## Finding

Ethiopia’s first national land accounts, produced using the UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting framework, compare land cover in 2013 and 2022 at national level and in the Abay (Blue Nile) and Awash River Basins.

Nationally, annual cropland increased 20% from 21.7 million to 25.9 million hectares; perennial cropland rose 50%; combined forest area increased by about 4.9 million hectares or 22%; and built-up area increased 50%. These changes reflect a mixture of agricultural development, population growth, urbanization, restoration, climate, and land-management practices.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`land-cover change -> standardized spatial accounts -> cross-ministry visibility -> agriculture, water, urban, biodiversity, and climate decisions`

The important shift is measurement as institutional infrastructure. GDP and conventional economic accounts do not show the stocks and flows of land, water, forests, ecosystem services, or environmental degradation. SEEA accounts create a shared evidence layer that can make trade-offs more visible—but they do not automatically resolve tenure, distribution, enforcement, or cultural-value conflicts.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- National land change can conceal sharply different basin-level pressures: Abay agricultural and shrubland change differs from Awash agricultural, urban, industrial, and grassland change.
- Natural-capital accounting can support public accountability by making restoration claims, land conversion, water pressure, and biodiversity trade-offs more auditable.
- What gets classified and counted becomes politically consequential; customary, cultural, and livelihood values may remain underrepresented.
- The next layer is not another map alone, but a decision trace from account evidence to land-use approval, watershed investment, restoration, compensation, and household outcomes.

## Boundary and next test

This is a first-edition account using two reference years, not a causal land-use study. The next test is whether continuous basin monitoring changes actual planning, enforcement, water allocation, restoration durability, tenure security, and household livelihood outcomes.
