# Crisis protection is a capability problem, not only a spending decision

## Finding

The World Bank's review of the 2026 energy crisis tracks 256 planned or implemented social-policy responses across 104 countries as of May 7. It compares the response with earlier crises and asks why governments do or do not scale protection when energy prices transmit through households, firms, inflation, and public budgets.

Subsidies accounted for nearly half of current responses. From 2022 through 2026, subsidies represented more than three times the share of cash transfers among crisis responses. The appeal is partly practical and political: subsidies can suppress visible prices quickly, support selected sectors, and appear easier to scale than administrative transfers. But the instrument also changes who receives relief, how visible the benefit is, and how much future fiscal room remains.

The paper's central design principle is that crisis-ready protection must “prepare seriously, scale up temporarily, and scale down credibly.” It sets out four illustrative reform scenarios rather than a causal evaluation.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a shock-scalability mechanism to the corpus:

`energy shock -> price transmission and uncertainty -> policy interpretation -> instrument choice -> reach and timing -> household room and political attribution -> scale-down or durable legitimacy`

The important finding is that a safety net is not crisis-ready merely because a government can announce spending. Readiness requires pre-existing delivery infrastructure, financing, triggers, communication, and a credible exit path. Otherwise governments may choose visible subsidies because targeted alternatives cannot expand quickly, even when subsidies are less distributionally precise.

The “temporary” part is politically consequential. Households need to know whether support will continue, who qualifies, and how withdrawal will work. A poorly explained or abrupt exit can turn a short-term protection measure into a trust and legitimacy problem. Conversely, a credible sunset may make emergency support more politically feasible before the next shock.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Crisis policy becomes a question of administrative imagination: what can the state actually deliver at speed?
- Households experience the instrument as a price, payment, service, or future tax/fiscal constraint—not as an abstract budget line.
- Subsidies are attractive partly because they make relief visible, while targeting and delivery failures remain less visible.
- Repeated shocks blur the boundary between emergency protection and permanent “crisis insurance.”
- Trust depends on both rapid inclusion and predictable withdrawal; scale-up without an exit story can undermine future consent.

## Boundary and next test

This is a descriptive global policy review, not evidence of household receipt, welfare, poverty reduction, or causal trust effects. The next test is a named energy or cost-of-living episode that follows the full chain: shock, policy announcement, instrument, retailer or utility price, payment receipt, household substitution, arrears, political attribution, and the eventual withdrawal or institutionalization of support.
