# Geoeconomic coercion, firm spillovers, household costs, and attribution audit

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** sanctions-context hostility, export controls, tariffs, firm response,
trade retaliation, household prices, and political legitimacy

## Question

When a state uses trade or financial dependence to pressure another state or
its firms, does the pressure produce a strategic concession—or mainly move
uncertain costs through firms, workers, supply chains, households, and public
attribution?

```text
state objective
  -> target selection and restriction
  -> firm/government response and retaliation
  -> trade, input, payroll, and supply-chain change
  -> prices, substitution, scarcity, or household room
  -> attribution, legitimacy, adaptation, or political response
  -> strategic concession, reversal, persistence, or escalation
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Comparable evidence | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target choice is strategic, not automatic | An NBER model distinguishes pressure on foreign governments from pressure on private firms. Private targets may internalize less of the national consequence; government targets may influence domestic firms that an outside power cannot directly reach. The optimal mix depends on the relative strength of those channels. | Theoretical model of trade and financial-linkage coercion. | It does not estimate which target works in a real episode, compliance, coalition behavior, public cost, or strategic success. |
| Coercion is becoming a larger part of measured hostility | An NBER bilateral panel decomposes monthly hostility into kinetic fighting, military posture, sanctions-context hostility, and routine diplomacy. The trade-weighted share channelled through sanctions contexts nearly doubled over the recent decade. Its comparison estimates about $334 billion in bilateral trade at risk relative to a pre-escalation baseline, with about half associated with the U.S.–China comparison. | GDELT event data calibrated against human-curated ground truth; gravity model and directed retaliation structure. | The $334 billion is a modeled counterfactual, not observed lost revenue or household harm. Event coding, reverse causality, specification, and third-country substitution remain open. |
| Retaliation compounds the route | The same NBER analysis finds directed hostility identifies a retaliation channel that compounds bilateral trade losses over several months, while routine diplomacy has no detected aggregate trade effect. | Estimated lagged bilateral trade response. | A trade decline does not prove coercive success or failure; diplomacy may matter politically or strategically without a measured aggregate trade effect. |
| Controls can spill beyond the target market | Census research finds the share of U.S. exports subject to controls doubled between 2010 and 2024. In the 2014 Russia restriction natural experiment, affected firms were less likely to export targeted goods to Russia and also reduced exports of other products and destinations; average payroll fell. | Firm-level export transactions linked to control classifications; natural experiment. | The abstract does not establish security benefit, national welfare, layoffs, household incidence, or whether firms later adapted through new products or destinations. |
| The supply-chain clock is delayed | An NBER analysis of the 2025 U.S. tariff episode estimates 26% of the tariff increase passed through to consumer prices relative to less-exposed goods. It attributes 64% of the consumer-price increase to direct foreign-variety effects and 36% to imported inputs and domestic-producer markups, with indirect effects arriving over nine to twelve months. | Working-paper comparison of exposed and less-exposed goods with channel decomposition. | Relative pass-through is not every household's burden; the result does not identify substitution, wages, margins, or welfare, and delayed effects may reflect adjustment and market power. |
| Foreign governments respond in more than one way | CRS records that in 2025 China used retaliatory tariffs, critical-mineral export restrictions, export controls, entity designations, and investigations; Canada and the EU announced, implemented, paused, or removed countermeasures while negotiating. | Dated review of official U.S. and foreign-government documents. | Announcements and temporary pauses do not establish compliance, policy change, alliance durability, or strategic success. |
| The cost becomes publicly legible | Ipsos Wave 148 reports 81% of Americans expected tariffs to raise prices on goods they buy, 71% said they had already seen tariff-linked price rises, 55% correctly identified tariffs as import taxes often raising consumer prices, and only 29% said tariff-related price increases were worth it to achieve national goals. Another 29% said it was fair for firms to pass tariff costs to shoppers. | U.S. online panel/partner-panel/river sample, September 22–23, 2026; calibrated population targets; credibility interval ±3.6 points, design-effect-adjusted ±5.1. | These are perceptions and policy judgments, not product-level incidence, firm margins, votes, or causal attribution. |

## What the sources show together

The evidence supports a **translation and attribution problem**, not a simple
coercion-success story.

Theoretical work says target selection changes who bears the immediate cost and
who can influence the next actor. The bilateral panel says coercive hostility
can reduce trade and trigger delayed retaliation. Census evidence shows that a
restriction aimed at one market can spill into other exports and payroll. The
tariff paper shows why the household effect may arrive later through inputs and
markups. The Ipsos evidence shows that once prices are visible, the public can
connect a foreign-policy instrument to everyday purchasing and judge whether the
cost is justified.

These are connected stages, not one estimate. The current record can support
the following bounded chain:

```text
restriction -> trade/firm response -> delayed price or payroll channel
            -> household interpretation -> legitimacy or political pressure
```

It cannot yet show that a household price increase caused a vote, that a firm
payroll decline caused a layoff, or that a retaliatory measure produced a
strategic concession.

## Curiosity checks

### What happened afterward?

The CRS timeline supplies a mixed answer: some measures were later reduced,
suspended, or removed under temporary arrangements, while other controls and
investigations remained. This is consistent with bargaining and adaptation, but
it is not evidence that the original pressure achieved its underlying goal.

### Who paid or gained?

The target government, targeted firms, firms in adjacent products and markets,
workers reflected in payroll, importers, domestic producers, and shoppers can
carry different parts of the cost. The sources do not identify the same firm or
household across those stages. The Ipsos fairness split—only 29% calling it fair
for companies to pass costs to shoppers—shows a legitimacy tension, not an
incidence estimate.

### Where did the easy story fail?

- A control can reduce targeted exports while security or diplomatic benefits
  remain unmeasured; a firm spillover is not proof of strategic failure.
- A tariff-linked price perception can rise without a measured product-level
  tariff effect; consumers may be responding to general inflation, exchange
  rates, supply shocks, or salient price changes.
- Retaliation can be announced and later paused without either capitulation or
  durable escalation.
- A lower average payroll is not the same as layoffs or lower household income;
  firms may change hours, composition, destinations, or accounting.
- Domestic substitution or third-country trade can reduce bilateral losses,
  meaning that trade decline is not identical to total economic loss.

### What else could explain the pattern?

1. **Strategic coercion explanation:** restrictions change the target's payoff
   and create pressure for policy accommodation.
2. **Adjustment explanation:** firms and governments redirect trade, change
   suppliers, or delay transactions, producing measured disruption without a
   lasting strategic change.
3. **Political-attribution explanation:** household prices become a salient
   accountability signal even when the causal share of the price change is
   small, shifting legitimacy more than material welfare.

The present corpus supports all three as plausible pathways. It does not yet
rank them for a named episode.

## Bounded public discussion

| Platform | Signal | Use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | An author relay of the NBER tariff paper emphasizes direct import-price pass-through and slower input, markup, and supply-chain effects. | Helps identify the delayed-cost interpretation; it is not the paper, and LinkedIn audiences are selected. |
| YouTube | Al Jazeera's U.S.–China discussion connects tariffs, critical technologies, rare earths, semiconductors, energy security, and shipping disruption, while treating short-term deals as distinct from structural rivalry. | Adds public framing around strategic interdependence; expert discussion does not measure future state behavior or household outcomes. |
| LinkedIn | An infrastructure industry post frames AI demand as arriving faster than grids, transformers, transmission, and labor can be delivered. | Shows how strategic technology rivalry is discussed through physical bottlenecks; it provides no utility bill, outage, or firm outcome. |

The public layer reinforces the idea that geopolitical policy is interpreted
through physical systems and everyday costs. It does not establish public
opinion, strategic success, or causal household harm.

## Stronger next test

Follow one named coercion episode with an episode ledger that keeps the actors
separate:

1. state objective, target, legal instrument, announced measure, and intended
   concession;
2. target-government, firm, ally, and third-country responses;
3. affected products, destinations, input costs, prices, quantities, payroll,
   employment, and substitution over time;
4. household exposure by income, place, product necessity, and liquidity;
5. public attribution, firm explanation, complaint or political response; and
6. concession, reversal, adaptation, persistence, or escalation.

Use a product- or firm-level price and trade panel when possible, then link it
to household spending or survey respondents. Preserve cases where controls
achieved a security objective with limited consumer cost, and cases where price
and payroll costs rose without observable strategic change.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** Geoeconomic pressure can alter trade and create firm,
payroll, supply-chain, and household-price channels. Public attribution is
becoming part of the policy route. The missing middle is a compatible named
episode that observes strategic objective, firm/household incidence, response,
and later concession or adaptation together.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call coercion successful, failed, consumer-funded, or politically
legitimating from a model mechanism, trade loss, export restriction, price
perception, or government announcement alone. Require one named episode with a
dated response chain, a credible counterexample, and explicit separation of
security benefit, firm cost, household incidence, and strategic outcome.
