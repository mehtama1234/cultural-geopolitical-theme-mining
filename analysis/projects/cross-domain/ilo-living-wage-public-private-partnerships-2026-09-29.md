# Living-wage governance moves from corporate promise toward shared wage-setting infrastructure

## Finding

The ILO has signed its first three public-private partnership agreements under the Living Wage Programme, with the Responsible Business Alliance, Unilever, and amfori. The agreements aim to translate a 2024 tripartite agreement on wage policy into evidence-based estimates, national and sectoral roadmaps, and company and supply-chain action.

The distinct finding is institutional: “living wage” is being moved from a voluntary corporate pledge toward a shared wage-setting infrastructure involving public data, national institutions, employers, workers, multinational firms, and supply-chain associations. The purpose is not only to calculate a number, but to connect household needs and family costs to bargaining, firm sustainability, and public wage-setting.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`household cost and family needs -> wage estimation and national data -> tripartite negotiation -> company and supply-chain roadmaps -> worker purchasing power, firm adaptation, and consumer-facing legitimacy`

The architecture attempts to solve a recurring problem: companies may announce living-wage intentions while using incompatible estimates, while governments and workers lack common data or leverage to operationalize them. A shared reference point can reduce methodological contestation, but it does not resolve who absorbs the cost, whether purchasing practices change, or whether subcontracted and informal workers are covered.

The consumer and geopolitical significance is indirect but real. Wage conditions in global supply chains can become part of brand legitimacy, responsible sourcing, trade governance, and household purchasing power. Public-private cooperation may scale action, yet the same architecture can concentrate agenda-setting in large firms unless worker representation, local ownership, collective bargaining, and remedy remain substantive.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- A living wage is being framed as a household and family standard, not simply an individual payroll threshold.
- Wage estimation becomes a political technology: the data categories chosen shape which costs, dependents, regions, and forms of care count.
- Supply-chain responsibility is shifting from audit and promise toward common methods, roadmaps, and national wage-setting institutions.
- Consumer-facing brand legitimacy may increasingly depend on whether wage claims are locally grounded and worker-negotiated.
- Public-private wage governance can widen coordination while still leaving the core distributional question—who pays and who gains—open.

## Boundary and next test

The ILO source documents agreements and intended mechanisms, not realized wage changes or worker welfare. The next test is a named sourcing country or sector episode following the estimate, tripartite process, company roadmap, purchasing practices, worker bargaining, payroll change, household consumption, and remedy.
