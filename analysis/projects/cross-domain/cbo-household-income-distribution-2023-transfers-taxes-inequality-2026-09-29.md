# Market income, transfers and taxes tell different stories about household security

## Finding

The Congressional Budget Office's *The Distribution of Household Income, 2023* separates three layers that are often collapsed in public debate: market and social-insurance income, means-tested transfers, and federal taxes. In 2023, the roughly 134 million U.S. households received about $20 trillion in income, and the distribution was more equal after transfers and taxes than before them.

The year was not uniform across the distribution. Inflation-adjusted income before transfers and taxes rose 5% for the lowest quintile and fell 9% for the top 1%, largely because of lower realized capital gains. After transfers and taxes, average income rose 4% for the lowest quintile and fell 5% for the highest quintile. These are distributional averages, not a complete account of household welfare.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`market income -> transfers and tax liability -> income after transfers and taxes -> unequal household room -> judgments about fairness, dependence and public legitimacy`

The long view adds a second tension. From 1979 to 2023, average income grew for every quintile, but much faster at the top: the lowest quintile rose from $19,000 to $28,500, while the highest rose from $179,700 to $409,300 in 2023 dollars. Transfers and taxes became more important in reducing inequality, but did not eliminate widening relative distance.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- “The economy” is not one household experience. Market income, public transfers, taxes, capital gains, health benefits, and inflation-adjusted averages produce different stories about security and deservingness.
- A lower-income household’s room can depend on a public-transfer system that is politically described as support, dependency, insurance, or redistribution. The same accounting change can therefore carry different cultural meanings.
- Growth without equal relative gains can weaken the legitimacy of macroeconomic success even when absolute incomes rise. People may judge the system through distance, volatility, and access to correction rather than through aggregate income alone.
- The CBO finding that means-tested transfers fell as a share of bottom-quintile income while federal tax rates also fell shows why “tax relief” and “benefit support” cannot be treated as interchangeable forms of household protection.
- Medicaid’s role in the long-run growth of means-tested transfers makes health access part of the distributional architecture, not a separate social policy silo.
- The political next step is attribution: whether households understand changing income, taxes and transfers as fair, deserved, administratively reachable, and durable. The CBO report measures distribution, not those meanings or responses.

## Boundary and next test

This is a CBO distributional analysis, not a causal evaluation of a particular program and not a household-level panel of material hardship. The next test is a named U.S. tax, transfer, Medicaid, SNAP, SSI or labor-market episode that follows distributional change into take-up, adequacy, consumption, debt, health access, administrative friction and political legitimacy.
