# Time conversion: family care, work flexibility, and participation

**Checked:** 2026-09-29  
**Status:** bounded cross-source synthesis; no pooled causal estimate

## Time is a conversion problem

Households do not experience time as a fixed resource. They convert money,
leave, childcare, transport, information, health access, and schedule control
into time that can actually be used for care, work, rest, and participation:

```text
care or life-stage need
  -> money, leave, service, information, or schedule option
  -> usable time and continuity
  -> work, health, family, and community outcome
  -> adaptation, substitution, exhaustion, or exit
```

The current evidence shows why stated preferences are insufficient. Parents may
want more time but lack leave or affordable care; working parents may value
flexibility while having little access to it; unpaid work can be economically
large while remaining invisible in ordinary production measures; and a health
information gap can become a work and community-participation constraint.

## Five evidence surfaces

### Parents want time that money and institutions do not reliably provide

The [New America National Parent Survey record](../../records/us-new-america-national-parent-survey-2026-time-care-money-sandwich.json)
surveys 5,472 U.S. parents and primary caregivers of children ages zero to
five. It reports that 72% wanted more quality time with their children, 55%
said post-birth time was less than wanted, and 52% cited at least one financial
barrier to spending time as desired. Among parents whose childcare fell short of
their ideal, 54% cited cost; 75% said their current work arrangement did not
fully match their ideal scenario.

The survey also shows plural routes rather than one universal family model:
parents value higher wages, remote or alternative work locations, flexibility
for sick children and appointments, and different amounts of paid work. Lower-
income parents reported more additional jobs and basic-needs strain, while
many parents also provided regular eldercare. These are self-reported barriers
and preferences, not verified hours, childcare prices, employer behavior, or
child outcomes.

### Public provision can return household room only when access becomes usable

The [New York City universal school-meals episode](../us-care-school-meals/nyc-universal-school-meals-access-learning-2026-09-28.md)
adds a public-provision counterexample. A historical longitudinal evaluation
found that universal free lunch increased participation among previously
eligible and non-eligible students and was associated with estimated learning
gains. The current system reports meals at large scale and offers all-student
access plus family and student feedback routes. Removing an eligibility and
stigma gate can therefore make food and school time more usable, potentially
returning money and coordination capacity to families.

The later stages remain open. Meals served are not unique students or consumed
meals; current access does not establish equal queue, schedule, disability,
cultural, quality, waste, or feedback experience; and the historical evaluation
does not estimate current household food-security or work-time effects. The
episode shows how a public service may convert formal universality into
participation without proving that provision has become durable household room.

### Flexibility is valuable because it absorbs shocks, but access is narrow

The [Pew working-parents record](../../records/us-pew-working-parents-2026-work-family-childcare-flexibility.json)
combines Census employment analysis with a 2,242-parent survey. In 2025,
52% of different-sex couples with children had two full-time working parents.
Among surveyed parents, more than 80% of non-self-employed workers considered
paid family or medical leave highly helpful, but only about half reported access.
Seventy-one percent considered work-from-home and choosing when to work highly
helpful, while only 23% reported substantial work-from-home flexibility.

Among parents with a child age five or younger, 48% found it difficult to find
care meeting their cost expectations and 39% used more than one care arrangement
for the same child. The source does not show whether multiple arrangements are
stable, preferred, or crisis-driven, and reported helpfulness is not observed
use or causal benefit. It does show a structural gap between the flexibility
that would make care workable and the flexibility people can actually access.

### Unpaid work is infrastructure, not residual activity

The [GAO unpaid-work record](../../records/us-gao-unpaid-household-work-time-value-care-2026.json)
uses pooled 2021–2024 American Time Use Survey diaries. It reports that 87.3%
of people performed at least one type of unpaid household work on a typical
day, averaging 3.72 hours; including secondary childcare raised the average to
4.74 hours. GAO estimates an annual market-equivalent value of roughly
$5.6–$6.0 trillion excluding secondary childcare and $6.7–$7.4 trillion when
it is included.

The value is imputed replacement cost, not income received or welfare created.
The stronger finding is institutional: employment and GDP measures depend on a
large production system that households perform without a standard wage or
service invoice. The ability to absorb that work through money, paid work,
transport, or services is unequally distributed. Diary data describe selected
days, not full longitudinal care histories, and unpaid activity can be both
burden and relationship value.

### Information and care continuity can determine participation

The [Gallup-Pivotal menopause record](../../records/us-gallup-pivotal-menopause-information-care-work-2026.json)
surveys 3,845 U.S. women. Only 37% said they had a good understanding of
menopause symptoms and 25% understood treatment options well; 37% of women who
experienced menopause learned about it only when symptoms began. Informal
sources were common, while 44% had ever discussed menopause with a healthcare
provider.

Among women who experienced menopause, the release reports limits on work
effort, reduced community involvement, promotion decisions, hours, and job
changes. Among those seeking or receiving treatment, some had to push to be
taken seriously, see multiple providers, or could not obtain needed care.
These are retrospective self-reports and do not establish clinical severity,
treatment effectiveness, employer response, income loss, or a universal
menopause pathway. They do show how information and recognition can become
time, work, and civic-participation infrastructure at a predictable life stage.

## Cross-source finding

The recurring pattern is unequal conversion of formal options into usable time:

1. A policy or employer may offer leave, flexibility, or a service.
2. A household still needs money, eligibility, transport, replacement care,
   information, and schedule control to use it.
3. If conversion fails, unpaid work and coordination labor expand.
4. The result appears as extra jobs, stacked care arrangements, delayed care,
   reduced work, lost promotion, lower community participation, or exit.
5. When public provision is universal, it may remove an eligibility or stigma
   cost, but time and room are realized only if the service is reachable,
   acceptable, reliable, and responsive in everyday use.

This is why time is a consumer and cultural variable as well as a labor or care
variable. The ability to attend, maintain relationships, pursue health, buy
convenience, or participate in community life depends on whether institutions
return usable time—or merely announce nominal access.

## What is not established

These sources do not establish causal effects of paid leave, remote work,
childcare, unpaid work, or menopause treatment on family wellbeing, earnings,
health, or civic participation. They do not justify treating all unpaid work as
harm or all flexibility as benefit. Survey preferences, diary estimates,
imputed values, and retrospective life-stage reports are complementary rather
than statistically interchangeable.

## Next test

The strongest next episode should follow one family or worker through a dated
care need:

```text
care or health need
  -> leave, schedule, service, information, and replacement options
  -> actual hours, cost, waiting, and continuity
  -> work, health, family, and social participation
  -> later adaptation, exhaustion, retention, or exit
```

It should preserve income, gender, age, family stage, job type, geography,
care intensity, and whether the option was actually usable. Until that join
exists, the atlas should describe time as hidden infrastructure whose value is
revealed by who can convert formal choice into continuity.
