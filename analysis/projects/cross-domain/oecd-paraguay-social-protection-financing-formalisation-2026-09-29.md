# Social protection is a financing-and-formalisation bargain

**Checked:** 2026-09-29  
**Source:** OECD, *Financing Social Protection in Paraguay: The Role of General Revenues and Social Security Contributions*  
**Record:** [machine-readable source record](../../records/comparative-oecd-paraguay-social-protection-financing-informality-2026.json)

## Core finding

The OECD report makes social protection a visible bargain among work status,
taxation, contribution rules, benefit adequacy, and institutional reach. The
problem is not simply whether a country spends more: it is whether contributory
and non-contributory routes can connect workers and households that move between
formal, self-employed, and informal livelihoods.

That creates a cultural and political tension. People are asked to trust a
protection system and contribute to it while the system must demonstrate that
eligibility, benefits, and financing are fair across different kinds of work and
family dependency.

## Cultural/social interpretation

Social protection is often described as a technical fiscal system, but its social
meaning is distributive: it tells people whether irregular work, dependency,
age, illness, and poverty are recognized as legitimate claims on collective
resources. A contribution-based route can feel earned and reciprocal, while a
general-revenue route can reach people excluded from payroll systems. Each route
also creates disputes over fairness, compliance, and deservingness.

The key consumer insight is that benefit adequacy and administrative reach shape
whether households experience taxation as a credible exchange or as extraction
without protection.

## Open middle

```text
informal and varied work patterns
  -> contribution and tax design
  -> eligibility, adequacy, and delivery of health, pension, and assistance programmes
  -> household risk-sharing and perceived fairness
  -> formalisation incentives, trust, and social-protection legitimacy
```

The OECD report assesses financing and reform options. It does not establish
public acceptance, behavioral responses, realized reforms, or causal effects on
formal employment and household welfare.
