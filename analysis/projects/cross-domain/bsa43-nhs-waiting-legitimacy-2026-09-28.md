# NHS legitimacy is increasingly an access-time problem

**Checked:** 2026-09-28  
**Source:** National Centre for Social Research, *British Social Attitudes 43: Waiting for treatment*  
**Record:** [machine-readable source record](../../records/bsa43-nhs-waiting-legitimacy-2026.json)

## Core finding

The chapter identifies a public-service paradox: people are more positive about
the quality of NHS care than about their ability to reach it. In the latest
survey, 50% were satisfied with the quality of care, but only 16% with the time
before a hospital appointment, 14% with A&E waiting, and 27% with GP appointment
waiting. Waiting was the most commonly cited reason for NHS dissatisfaction in
2023, at 71%.

This is not merely a service-performance finding. Waiting is a legitimacy
experience. It converts institutional capacity into household time, uncertainty,
work disruption, care coordination, and a judgment about whether public provision
is actually reachable. The historical comparison shows that satisfaction can
recover when improvements become visible, but resources alone do not guarantee
that conversion.

## Cultural/social interpretation

The central distinction is between “good care” as an ideal and “reachable care”
as a lived public bargain. A system may retain moral legitimacy for the quality
of its clinicians while losing political legitimacy because the route to those
clinicians is slow, opaque, or repeatedly interrupted. That helps explain why
public admiration for the NHS can coexist with anger at the system that delivers
it.

The policy lesson is also a measurement lesson. Headcount, spending, targets, and
technology are inputs. The public experiences the conversion: appointment access,
waiting, continuity, coordination, and whether the service explains or corrects
failure. That conversion is where care legitimacy is won or lost.

## Open middle

```text
capacity / demand / staffing / social care / productivity
  -> appointment access and waiting time
  -> household time, work/care disruption, treatment continuity
  -> satisfaction, attribution, political judgment, and durable trust
```

The BSA chapter measures public judgments and uses historical administrative
indicators; it does not establish patient-level outcomes, causal policy effects,
clinical quality, or the time burden of waiting for the same person.
