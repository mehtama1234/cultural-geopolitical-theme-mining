# Public-sector reform ideas diffuse faster than state capacity can sustain them

## Finding

The World Bank reviews public-sector reform projects financed in Sub-Saharan Africa between 2010 and 2023. More than one-third included at least one New Public Management intervention, such as performance-based incentives or greater administrative autonomy. Only 40% of the interventions were fully implemented.

Performance-based public financial-management reforms performed comparatively better than agencification and performance-based pay reforms. The latter two attempt more directly to rewire authority and incentives inside bureaucracies and were more difficult to implement. Four qualitative cases—Madagascar, Benin, Burkina Faso, and Burundi—show that these reforms are particularly difficult to sustain in fragile and neo-patrimonial contexts.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a reform-design-to-lived-capacity mechanism:

`reform model -> administrative design and incentives -> implementation capacity and political coalition -> staff behavior and institutional continuity -> service reliability, feedback, and public legitimacy`

The main finding is not simply that reform “works” or “fails.” It is that reform models travel faster than the conditions that make them durable. A performance target, autonomous agency, or pay incentive can exist formally while staff, budgets, authority, data, political backing, and citizen-facing remedy remain unchanged.

This creates a recurring legitimacy risk. Citizens encounter the reform through whether a permit, benefit, school, clinic, or complaint route becomes more reliable. When the visible reform promise is not converted into usable service, responsibility becomes difficult to assign: donors can point to project architecture, ministries to political constraints, and citizens to institutional failure.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Administrative reform is experienced as time, documentation, discretion, waiting, and correction—not as an organizational chart.
- Performance metrics can improve fiscal routines while leaving authority, pay, staffing, and frontline discretion unresolved.
- Autonomy reforms may be more politically difficult precisely because they redistribute control inside the state.
- Imported management language can become a status signal without becoming durable institutional practice.
- Trust depends on repeated service encounters and credible correction, not only reform adoption or target design.

## Boundary and next test

This is project-review and qualitative-case evidence, not a representative causal evaluation of African public-sector reform or citizen outcomes. The next test is a named reform episode that follows design into budget release, staffing, frontline behavior, service waiting time, user feedback, correction, political attribution, and durability after external project support ends.
