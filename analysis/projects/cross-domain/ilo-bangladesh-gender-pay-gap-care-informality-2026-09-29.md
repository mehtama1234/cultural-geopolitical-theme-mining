# Bangladesh’s gender pay gap is a labor-market sorting and care infrastructure problem

## Finding

The ILO’s September 2026 study uses Bangladesh’s 2023 Labour Force Survey alongside stakeholder consultations and policy analysis. Women’s employment rate is 40.2%, compared with 77.9% for men. Only 19% of employed women are wage employees, compared with 48% of employed men; women represent about 17% of all wage employees and 6.2% of managers.

Among wage employees, women earn approximately 22–25% less per month than men—about 750–780 taka for every 1,000 taka earned by men. The gap widens with age, narrows with education, and is larger in informal employment.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`unequal access to wage employment and management -> sector and occupation sorting -> unpaid-care/time constraints and informality -> lower pay, bargaining power, and protection`

The report’s important move is to treat pay inequality as an institutional and household-time system, not only an equal-pay comparison. The majority of the gap remains unexplained by observed education, occupation, and sector differences. Women in wage employment also report almost four times more weekly household-chore time than men, while feminized sectors tend to pay less for all workers.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Formal equal-pay rights do not automatically become realized equality when employment is overwhelmingly informal and complaint or inspection systems are weak.
- Unpaid care functions as labor-market infrastructure: it shapes hours, job choice, mobility, bargaining, and access to higher-paid roles.
- Sector “choice” can reflect the undervaluation of feminized work and the absence of care-compatible, safe, well-paid alternatives.
- Pay transparency, wage-setting, labor inspection, complaint access, care policy, and social dialogue are linked parts of the same legitimacy chain.

## Boundary and next test

The decomposition’s unexplained component is not by itself proof of direct discrimination, and the report does not estimate the causal effect of a particular reform. The next test is whether a named intervention combining wage transparency, inspection or complaint access, care support, and bargaining changes realized pay, job mobility, retention, and household time—not merely legal text.
