# Statistics Canada 2026: home-based child care as constrained time infrastructure

## Finding

Statistics Canada’s 2024 provider survey shows that home-based child care is a major but capacity-constrained component of Canada’s care system. Approximately 17,200 licensed and 11,700 unlicensed home-based services cared for about 151,300 children aged five and under who were not attending school. Nearly four in five homes were already at maximum capacity: 81.8% of licensed and 74.7% of unlicensed homes. Waitlists were reported by 61.8% of licensed and 44.3% of unlicensed homes.

The mechanism matters for the broader program: fee reduction or formal policy commitment does not automatically create usable care. Families still need a place, schedule, disability accommodation, geographic fit, and a provider system capable of sustaining supply.

## Flexibility and provider capability

Only 3% of licensed and 7% of unlicensed homes offered evening, weekend or overnight care. Drop-in or flexible care was offered by 8% of licensed and 18% of unlicensed homes. Accommodations for children with disabilities were reported by 33% of licensed and 16% of unlicensed homes.

The provider system is also institutionally differentiated. About 99% of providers were women+. No ECE-related training was reported by 11.4% of licensed providers and 50% of unlicensed providers. About 72% of licensed homes were approved or registered through an agency or coordinating office rather than licensed directly by a province or territory.

## Limits

This is a nationally representative provider survey using a cross-sectional 2024 snapshot. It does not measure parent-level unmet demand, actual wait duration, affordability, child outcomes, or the causal effects of Canada-wide early-learning and child-care agreements. Licensing is not a complete proxy for quality, and capacity does not guarantee that a place matches a family’s schedule, location, age group, fee or accessibility needs.

## Program placement

The source extends `care_health_reproduction`, `time_hidden_price`, `household_room_consumption`, `work_control_bargaining`, `public_systems_feedback` and `unequal_exposure_status`. It makes care-system reachability concrete: provider capacity, regulation, schedule, training and accommodation mediate whether public investment becomes released household time and employment continuity.

The next tests are whether new spaces reduce actual wait times, whether nonstandard-hour and disability-responsive provision expands, whether provider compensation and training stabilize supply, and whether families experience improved work continuity and household room rather than only lower nominal fees.
