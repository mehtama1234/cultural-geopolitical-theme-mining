# Automation may break the expected industrial relocation ladder

## Finding

The World Bank's new multi-country model asks whether the classic “flying geese” pattern—manufacturing moving from older, higher-wage economies toward younger and lower-wage economies—still holds when protectionism, population aging, and automation interact.

The answer is conditional. A doubling of robot productivity raises China's modeled real GDP by 0.77%, compared with 0.23% on average across developing economies. In the trade-cost scenarios, doubling robot productivity offsets the modeled negative GDP effects of higher trade costs in China and Türkiye, while a tenfold increase is required to eliminate those effects across all developing countries. A 10% decline in China's labor supply can be offset by doubling robot productivity in vehicles, but computer and electronics requires about a 220% increase, and metals becomes infeasible beyond a 14% labor-force decline because too few tasks are automatable.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds an automation-to-relocation mechanism:

`aging/protectionism -> higher wages or trade costs -> expected industrial relocation -> automation and task substitution -> preserved incumbent competitiveness -> altered places of work, development paths, and bargaining power`

The deeper implication is that automation may preserve industrial production in an aging incumbent economy instead of releasing a predictable next rung for younger economies. That changes the cultural promise of industrialization: lower-wage countries may no longer be able to assume that rising wages elsewhere will create an automatic opening for labor-intensive manufacturing. Industrial opportunity becomes more dependent on robot capability, electricity, capital, skills, sector task structure, and geopolitical access.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- “Development through industrialization” may become less portable when automation allows incumbent manufacturing centers to remain competitive despite aging.
- Robot productivity can protect exports and GDP without preserving jobs, wage growth, bargaining power, or local household security.
- The same technology has unequal sectoral meaning: vehicles can absorb a demographic shock more readily than electronics or metals, so national averages hide distinct worker and place trajectories.
- Protectionism and automation may reinforce industrial concentration rather than simply bringing factories home or spreading them to new countries.
- Consumers may experience the result through prices, product availability, supply-chain geography, and political claims about national manufacturing, while workers experience it through task substitution and weaker or more selective entry routes.

## Boundary and next test

This is a calibrated model, not a worker-level evaluation or observed prediction of employment, wages, household consumption, or political response. The next test is a named sector-country case that follows robot adoption into firm hiring, task redesign, wage distribution, worker mobility, local supplier ecosystems, energy demand, and public narratives of industrial decline or renewal.
