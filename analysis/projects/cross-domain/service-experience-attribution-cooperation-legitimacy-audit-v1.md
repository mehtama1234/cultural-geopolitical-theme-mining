# Service experience, attribution, cooperation, and legitimacy audit

**Status:** active depth artifact; comparative legitimacy-stage audit, not a trust or service causal estimate  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** public or international service experience -> responsibility and fairness -> institutional trust -> cooperation, consent, or withdrawal

## What we found

Legitimacy is not a single response to whether a service worked. The newest
evidence separates personal delivery, responsibility mapping, procedural
fairness, institutional trust, long-term expectations, and willingness to
cooperate. A person can receive a good service and still believe government does
not act in their interests; a public can demand major change while doubting
that institutions can deliver it.

```text
service, policy, or international outcome
  -> experience, visibility, attribution, and perceived fairness
  -> institution-specific or general trust
  -> consent, cooperation, compliance, participation, or withdrawal
  -> durable institutional capacity and legitimacy
```

## What this connects to

Three distinctions are especially important.

First, **good delivery is not national trust**. The England study gives a
direct counterexample: service users often report good delivery while overall
trust in government remains low. People appear to judge performance and
whether an institution acts for people like them as separate questions.

Second, **attribution is not simply knowledge**. People may distinguish some
local responsibilities while assigning other outcomes to the wrong level of
government. Credit and blame are asymmetric: national government can receive
more blame for negative conditions than credit for positive ones. This changes
how a service outcome becomes political meaning.

Third, **support for cooperation is conditional on results**. Across the global
surveys, people still want cooperation, democracy, climate action, security,
and aid, but confidence depends on delivery, fairness, national-interest fit,
and visible effectiveness. A public can support an institution's purpose while
questioning its current competence or future reliability.

## Bounded public discussion

Public discussion adds interpretations and contested mechanisms, not
prevalence:

| Record | Discussion signal | Use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| [Institutional-confidence discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0054-institutional-confidence-linkedin-001.json) | Low confidence is interpreted as broad but uneven, with small business and the military retaining more legitimacy than some other institutions | Supports institution-specific trust; selected professional comments are not representative or causal |
| [OECD citizen-participation discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-oecd-citizen-participation-policy-cycle-linkedin-001.json) | Participation is framed as useful only when public input reaches the policy cycle and receives a response | Identifies a voice-to-response mechanism; discussion does not show implementation |
| [Dignity and trust discussion](../../social-discussions/records/trust-meaning-001.json) | Fair participation, respectful disagreement, and dignity are presented as routes to rebuilding trust | Adds procedural meaning; affirmation is not evidence of changed trust or behavior |
| [International cooperation discussion](../../social-discussions/records/geopolitical-state-linkedin-001.json) | Cooperation and autonomy are discussed through capacity, reciprocity, and unequal dependence | Connects domestic capability to legitimacy; advocacy is not observed geopolitical behavior |

The discussion layer suggests that legitimacy is experienced through being
heard, correctly addressed, fairly treated, and able to see what happened next.
It does not prove that any single service user or country changed behavior.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not equate a good personal service rating with generalized institutional
  trust or political support.
- Do not treat responsibility knowledge as correct attribution of a specific
  outcome.
- Do not treat blame or credit as evidence of actual government causation.
- Do not combine country medians, respondent attitudes, service users, and
  policy frameworks into one legitimacy score.
- Do not treat cooperation support as cooperation behavior, compliance, aid, or
  changed foreign policy.
- Keep national, local, regional, international, citizen, service-user, and
  affected-loser units distinct.
- Preserve the counterexample that institution-specific trust can remain high
  even when generalized government trust is low.

## Stronger next test

Build one service or policy episode with:

1. named institution, level of government, change date, and affected group;
2. baseline service condition, alternatives, and exposure distribution;
3. public explanation, responsibility mapping, participation, and negotiation;
4. actual delivery, correction, remedy, and individual or community receipt;
5. prior and later institution-specific trust, attribution, cooperation, or
   non-use; and
6. durable implementation, unequal burden, collective action, or exit.

For international cooperation, add an observed allocation, compliance,
coalition, or dispute-resolution action after the stated commitment. If that
episode is unavailable, keep performance, process, and public opinion as
separate evidence layers.

## Deeper question

**When does a service or cooperative commitment become legitimate in the eyes
of affected people, and how do delivery, attribution, fairness, and voice shape
whether they cooperate, contest, or withdraw?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is sufficient when service experience, responsibility, procedural
fairness, institutional trust, cooperation, and behavior have been separated
by unit, date, method, and limits; public discussion is bounded; and the
delivery-to-legitimacy missing middle is explicit. The broader 14-theme program
remains active.
