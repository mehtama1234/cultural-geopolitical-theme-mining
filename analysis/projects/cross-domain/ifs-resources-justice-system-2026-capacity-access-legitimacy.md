# Justice is an access infrastructure whose performance depends on more than its budget

## Finding

The IFS Transforming Justice mid-grant report reframes justice as public infrastructure connecting state capacity to everyday social and economic life. Its central finding is that resources mean more than spending: buildings, staff, management, provider viability, case preparation, geography, demand, and coordination determine whether money becomes usable justice.

The pressure is visible in administrative delays. In early 2026, 330,000 cases were open in party-to-state tribunals; average resolution times were 61 weeks for asylum and immigration appeals and 35 weeks for social-security appeals. Civil legal-aid case volumes had fallen 57% by 2024 compared with pre-reform levels, while real expenditure had fallen nearly 15%. The report warns that lower completed legal-aid activity cannot be read as lower legal need because eligibility and provider capacity also shape what is observed.

This creates a legitimacy and household mechanism: when legal help, courts, tribunals, police contact, or preventive services become geographically distant, delayed, or unavailable, the formal right remains while the practical route to remedy weakens. Justice demand is also partly produced elsewhere—by welfare, housing, youth-service, early-years, and education policy—so one service can export pressure into another.

## Theme and subthemes

- **Capacity is composite:** budgets require buildings, staff, management, providers, and coordination.
- **Delay as household cost:** waiting for an appeal or remedy can prolong income, housing, immigration, or family insecurity.
- **Observed use versus unmet need:** fewer cases may indicate reduced access rather than reduced problems.
- **Geography of remedy:** court, police, and legal-aid location changes who can practically use the system.
- **Cross-service pressure transfer:** cuts or weak provision in youth, early-years, welfare, or housing systems can later alter justice demand.

## What the source supports

The report supports descriptive and analytical links between justice resources, tribunal backlogs, legal-aid contraction, geographic distribution, court/police infrastructure, and adjacent-service spillovers. It combines multiple IFS studies and clearly distinguishes administrative patterns from causal claims.

It does not provide a single causal estimate of justice spending, measure all unmet legal need, or prove that every delay or closure produces the same household or crime outcome.

## Next test

Follow named legal, tribunal, court, police, youth-service, or prevention episodes from resource allocation through geographic reach, waiting, representation, decision quality, remedy, household stability, and trust. Preserve the distinction between demand, completed cases, provider capacity, and unresolved need.
