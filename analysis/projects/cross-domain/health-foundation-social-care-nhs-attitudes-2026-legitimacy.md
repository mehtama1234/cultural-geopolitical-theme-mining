# The public wants more state responsibility for care but does not yet share a usable map of the system

## Finding

The Health Foundation’s September 2026 survey of 2,238 UK adults reveals a care-legitimacy paradox. The public is deeply pessimistic about social-care standards, prefers a larger state role and non-profit or public provision, and continues to strongly support the NHS’s universal, tax-funded principles. Yet many people misunderstand how social care is funded and organised, most are not financially preparing for future care costs, and there is no settled agreement on who should pay for reform.

Social care is therefore not only a service-capacity problem. It is a public-knowledge and consent problem. A majority can support “more state responsibility” in principle while disagreeing over taxes, cuts elsewhere, provider ownership, access, workforce pay, and the boundary between family and public obligation. The gap between actual provision and preferred provision creates a legitimacy pressure that may widen when people encounter care directly.

The NHS shows a related but different pattern: performance pessimism has eased, local service views are more positive than national views, and support for the founding model remains high. This separates institutional identity from operational confidence. Universal legitimacy can persist while access, waiting, staffing, and care continuity remain contested.

## Theme and subthemes

- **Care-system knowledge gap:** public support for reform is formed under widespread misunderstanding of entitlement and funding.
- **Ownership legitimacy gap:** people recognise the for-profit care market but largely do not prefer it.
- **Unresolved burden allocation:** greater state responsibility does not settle tax, family, provider, or service trade-offs.
- **Experience-conditioned legitimacy:** direct or family users are more negative about local social-care performance.
- **Universal identity versus operational confidence:** NHS principles remain durable while performance and government-policy judgments fluctuate.

## What the source supports

The briefing supports weighted survey estimates of social-care pessimism, policy disagreement, funding knowledge, financial preparation, provider preferences, NHS principles, and access priorities. It supports a public-consent and legitimacy mechanism, with explicit distinction between current and prior polling waves.

It does not measure actual care quality, unmet need, household savings adequacy, service outcomes, or the likely public response to a specific reform design.

## Next test

Follow a named social-care reform or local-care episode from public information and funding design through eligibility, provider capacity, family-care burden, actual access, cost exposure, workforce continuity, and durable legitimacy. Test whether clearer information changes preferences and whether preferred ownership models can deliver reachable care without exporting work and cost to households.

Source: [Health Foundation, We need to talk: Public attitudes to social care and the NHS](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/briefings/we-need-to-talk-public-attitudes-to-social-care-and-the-NHS)
