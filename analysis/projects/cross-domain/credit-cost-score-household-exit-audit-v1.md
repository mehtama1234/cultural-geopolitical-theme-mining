# Credit cost, score feedback, household room, and exit audit

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** borrowing costs, credit information, credit scores, revolving debt,
household buffers, repayment strain, dispute routes, and practical exit

## Question

When credit expands a household's choices, does better information and access
create durable room—or can pricing, score feedback, and repayment pressure turn
the same system into a narrowing path with few safe alternatives?

```text
need or purchase
  -> search, score, price, and approval
  -> borrowing, substitution, delay, or refusal
  -> payment, revolving balance, buffer use, or default
  -> score and future access
  -> dispute, renegotiation, hardship, switching, or exit
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Comparable evidence | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit costs can suppress a purchase | Philadelphia Fed reports 25.8% of surveyed adults considered borrowing for a large purchase but did not make it for financial reasons in the prior six months; 49.0% of those respondents named financing cost as the primary reason, equivalent to 12.6% of all respondents. Five percent chose a cheaper purchase mainly because of financing cost. | Consumer Finance Institute survey of just over 4,000 U.S. adults, July 20–23, 2026; descriptive tables and regression. | Self-reported decisions are not transaction data or a causal rate effect. Forgoing a purchase can be prudent, harmful, or unrelated to credit exclusion. |
| Flexibility differs beyond income | In the Philadelphia Fed regression, holding a personal loan was associated with a 23.2 percentage-point higher likelihood of forgoing financing and holding a credit card with an 8.7-point association; respondents with potentially higher-return investments were less likely to cite high borrowing costs. | Observational regression controlling for income, unemployment, age, education, race, family composition, homeownership, debt, and assets. | Associations do not establish that debt causes forgone purchases; unmeasured liquidity, credit score, urgency, and purchase size remain possible explanations. |
| Information can improve the price while increasing borrowing | An NBER randomized trial with 112,063 Chilean loan seekers found that eliciting beliefs led participants to search more and obtain lower rates. A price-comparison tool updated beliefs, increased negotiation, lowered rates without increasing search, and made participants more likely to take out a loan. | RCT with administrative data on the universe of consumer loans and a regulator-built comparison tool. | The abstract does not give effect sizes, product details, follow-up, repayment, fees, maturity, or household-security outcomes. Lower rates and higher take-up can be beneficial or risky. |
| A score change can become part of the default pathway | An NBER credit-record study finds an additional counted inquiry lowered scores by about five points without changing the rest of the credit report. Default did not respond for consumers with clean records, but rose 3.2 percentage points over two years among consumers with prior derogatory marks; at least 13% of the score-default relationship in that group was estimated to be self-fulfilling. | Regression discontinuity around the fourteen-day inquiry-counting rule, with two-year credit outcomes. | The study does not identify which lender changed an approval, rate, limit, or timing, nor later rent, insurance, work, recovery, or applications. |
| Household buffers are uneven | In the 2025 Federal Reserve SHED, 63% of adults said they could cover a hypothetical $400 emergency with cash or equivalent, 12% could not pay it, and 15% would use a card and carry the balance. Among linked SHED/credit-report respondents, average card balances rose $748 (11%) from 2023 to 2025; the increase was $2,530 (37%) for people who said it was difficult to get by and $59 (1%) for those living comfortably. | National survey; linked descriptive credit-record comparison for consenting respondents. | Emergency responses are hypothetical; linked respondents are not the full population. Balance growth does not reveal rates, spending, income shocks, repayment, or causality. |
| Repayment pressure is distributed | Among student-loan borrowers required to pay in the 2025 SHED, 73% paid the full required amount and 41% reported recent payment difficulty. Full-payment shares were 42% for required payers below $25,000 family income and 92% for those at $100,000 or more. | Conditional self-reported borrower groups. | Payment difficulty is not observed delinquency; nonpayment can reflect deferment, enrollment, forbearance, or income-driven rules. |
| Market cost and dispute exposure are large | CFPB reports more than $1.2 trillion in card balances, an approximate 25.2% average APR, $160 billion in interest assessed, and $31.3 billion in fees assessed in 2024. It records $9.8 billion in disputed charges, with 40% involving cancelled recurring transactions. | CFPB market report combining de-identified credit records, issuer data, and dispute measures. | Market totals and averages have different universes. Interest or fees assessed are not necessarily paid; disputes are not verified harm or successful refunds. |
| Remedy can improve credit access without restoring broad household security | Two randomized U.S. medical-debt relief experiments covered 83,401 people and $169 million in face-value debt. A credit-reporting subexperiment increased credit scores by 3.4 points and average credit limits by $340, while the hospital-debt follow-up found a 1.1-point increase in another unpaid bill sent to collections and no average effect on health-care use, mental health, physical health, or financial wellness. | Randomized debt-relief experiments with credit reports, collections, and survey follow-up, 2018–2020. | The portfolios and study arms are selected; credit access is not realized borrowing, housing, employment, insurance, or household recovery. Average nulls do not rule out heterogeneous effects. |
| Expectations can outrun income while credit access worsens | New York Fed August 2026 SCE respondents expected median one-year spending growth of 5.2% versus income growth of 3.0%; mean perceived probability of missing a minimum debt payment in the next three months was 13.2%, while perceived credit access deteriorated. | Rotating panel of approximately 1,300 U.S. household heads. | Expectations are not realized spending, income, or delinquency; spending growth may be prices or necessities rather than volume. |

## What we found

The evidence supports a **credit-room conversion gap**. Better information can
make a loan cheaper and improve negotiation. But it can also increase take-up
without showing whether the borrower can repay. At the other end, a small score
change can worsen later default for borrowers already near trouble. Aggregate
credit access therefore does not tell us whether a household gained a safe
option or entered a more fragile one.

Three mechanisms must remain separate:

1. **Price knowledge:** people understand rates and negotiate better.
2. **Credit availability:** a lender approves, expands, or restricts access.
3. **Household security:** the borrowing decision leaves enough room for food,
   housing, care, work, repayment, and a later shock.

The NBER Chile trial shows stage 1 can improve while take-up rises. The NBER
score study shows stage 2 can feed back into future default. The Fed and CFPB
records show the scale and unequal buffers around stage 3. None follows the same
borrower from information to loan terms, repayment, default, recovery, and exit.

The medical-debt experiments provide a useful counterexample to a simple
“credit repair equals welfare repair” story. Randomized relief changed credit
scores and limits, yet the follow-up did not produce average health, care-use,
or broad financial-wellness improvement. That does not make the credit effect
meaningless; it shows that a measurable access improvement can stop before
household recovery, and that a later collections outcome may move in an
unfavorable direction without proving moral hazard or intentional nonpayment.

## Curiosity checks

### Where did the pattern fail?

- Better loan comparison lowered rates without increasing search, showing that
  information can improve bargaining rather than merely add friction.
- A credit-score movement did not change default for people with clean records,
  so the feedback risk is concentrated rather than universal.
- Carrying a card balance is not automatically distress: some households use
  credit for timing or rewards, and a non-cash emergency response may be
  precautionary liquidity management.
- Forgoing a large purchase can protect a household from over-borrowing, even
  when the same measure looks like constrained consumption.
- Debt relief can improve a score or limit while leaving average health and
  financial-wellness outcomes unchanged; credit repair and household repair are
  separate endpoints.

### What else could explain the differences?

1. **Access mechanism:** credit and price information help people find cheaper
   terms, smooth a necessary expense, or avoid a worse lender.
2. **Fragility mechanism:** lower prices and easier approval encourage debt
   among people with limited buffers, making later shocks and score feedback
   more damaging.
3. **Selection mechanism:** borrowers with larger needs or weaker finances both
   seek credit and report more difficulty; observed associations need not be
   caused by the loan or score alone.

### What would change the answer?

A linked borrower-level panel showing loan purpose, rate, fees, maturity, total
repayment, income and assets, payment history, score changes, hardship actions,
disputes, and later borrowing would tell us whether information creates safer
choice or only cheaper entry into the same risk. The current sources do not
provide that full chain.
The strongest design would also retain randomized or quasi-random remedy timing,
so improved score/access can be separated from actual repayment, care, housing,
and financial-room outcomes.

| LinkedIn | An OECD consumer-finance relay links digital inclusion to scams, algorithmic opacity, branch closures, and weaker support routes. | Summary of comparative policy evidence; it does not measure individual losses or successful remedy. |
| LinkedIn | A medical-debt discussion connects credit visibility to renting, employment, treatment delay, and household budgets. | Meaning and pathway signal; combined claims require independent source checking. |

The discussion layer makes the social meaning visible: credit is experienced not
only as a price but as permission, stigma, future access, and the ability to keep
ordinary life moving. It does not establish prevalence or causal household harm.

## Stronger next test

Follow one borrower or household across the complete decision path:

```text
need and liquid buffer
  -> search, score, offer, and comparison information
  -> approval, rate, fees, maturity, and loan purpose
  -> purchase, substitution, delay, or refusal
  -> payment, hardship, delinquency, dispute, or refinancing
  -> score/access change, recovery, switching, or exit
```

Stratify by income, race, age, disability, housing status, debt type, and
financial-literacy or information access. Preserve cases where information
reduces cost and improves repayment, as well as cases where lower-cost credit
increases exposure or a score change becomes self-fulfilling.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** Credit information can improve search and price negotiation,
but the evidence also shows unequal buffers, high revolving costs, selective
score feedback, and a possible gap between credit access and safe household
room. The missing middle is a compatible borrower episode linking terms to later
repayment, hardship, correction, and exit.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call credit expansion empowering, harmful, or welfare-improving from
balances, rates, approval, information, score changes, or purchase delay alone.
Require a compatible borrower-level or account-level sequence with loan terms,
purpose, repayment, hardship or remedy, later score/access, and a credible
counterexample.
