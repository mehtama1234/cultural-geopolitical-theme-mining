# Ipsos Consumer Tracker Wave 148: price attribution, institutional trust and value literacy

## Finding

Wave 148 shows a consumer politics of attribution. Tariffs are no longer an abstract trade instrument: 81% say they will raise prices on goods they buy and 71% say they have already seen tariff-linked increases, while only 29% say those increases are worth it for national goals and 29% say it is fair for firms to pass the costs to shoppers. This is a legitimacy problem in which households can identify both the mechanism and the actor who appears to be passing through the cost.

The same wave shows institutional uncertainty being converted into household routines. Only 39% express at least some trust in government food-safety inspections, 54% say recent recalls reduced their trust, and respondents report washing produce, avoiding recalled foods, buying more locally sourced food and buying less fresh produce. The response is neither simple withdrawal nor passive trust; it is a practical redistribution of monitoring work from institutions to households.

In clothing, evaluative authority is moving away from brand prestige. 83% say expensive clothing is not necessarily better quality, 82% say brand names are becoming less important, 78% prioritize fabric over brand and 77% would pay more upfront for durability. This is value literacy rather than pure austerity: the consumer is willing to spend when longevity is legible, but is less willing to pay for symbolic brand authority alone.

The safety items add a legitimacy distinction. More police presence is viewed as making communities safer by 56%, compared with 31% for armed military/federal agents, 21% for armed teachers and 20% for generalized open carry. These are perceptions rather than crime effects, but they suggest that visible authority is judged through role and institutional fit, not simply through the quantity of weapons or agents.

## Mechanism

Cost shocks and institutional failures are being translated into attribution, household verification and selective willingness to pay. The emerging consumer does not simply consume less: they ask who caused the cost, what evidence makes quality trustworthy, and which forms of authority feel legitimate in context.

## Boundary

Ipsos used an online panel, partner-panel and river sampling, calibrated to U.S. population targets. The sample was 1,082 adults; the reported credibility interval was +/-3.6 percentage points, adjusted to +/-5.1 with the design effect. The findings are self-reported attitudes and behaviors, not transaction records, verified food-safety practices, crime outcomes or causal estimates.
