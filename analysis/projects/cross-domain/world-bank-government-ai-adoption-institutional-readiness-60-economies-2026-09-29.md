# Government AI use is broad, but institutionalization is shallow

## Finding

The World Bank's AI and Data for Better Governance Survey covers 60 economies and finds that government AI adoption is broad but shallow and uneven. Most governments report some AI use, but adoption is usually ad hoc and rarely institutionalized. A World Bank summary reports that 44% of governments' internal AI use consists of individual public servants using AI for tasks such as searching for information or summarizing documents.

The paper also finds that constraints vary with institutional readiness. Data and budget shortages are common everywhere; lower-income economies more often report missing policies, while higher-income economies more often emphasize privacy and ethical concerns. Many governments provide access to generative-AI tools before formal guidelines exist. The paper classifies governments into four archetypes, including “cowboys” whose use runs ahead of institutional scaffolding.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This is different from asking whether a government has an AI strategy or a pilot. The relevant distinction is **use versus institutionalization**:

`individual experimentation -> organizational learning or shadow workflow -> formal governance, data, skills, and accountability -> reliable public value`

The chain can stop at the first step. Individual use may save time, but it can also create uneven practice, privacy leakage, unreviewed outputs, vendor dependence, and invisible changes in how citizens are classified or served. Conversely, premature formalization can block useful low-risk learning. The public question is therefore not simply “does government use AI?” but “who is allowed to use it, for what, under which review and remedy conditions, and with what institutional memory?”

## Cultural and political significance

- AI adoption is becoming a status signal for governments, but visible use can outrun actual institutional capacity.
- Public workers become informal AI integrators when official systems lag; this shifts risk and judgment onto individual employees.
- Citizens may experience governance lag as inconsistent explanations, opaque decisions, or different treatment across agencies.
- Privacy, ethics, data quality, budgets, skills, and policy are not separate checklist items; they are complementary conditions of legitimate public automation.

## Boundary and next test

The survey measures reported use and readiness, not independently audited system performance or citizen outcomes. The next test is a named agency implementation that follows an AI use from pilot or individual experimentation through procurement, workflow redesign, human review, error correction, public explanation, and measurable service outcomes.
