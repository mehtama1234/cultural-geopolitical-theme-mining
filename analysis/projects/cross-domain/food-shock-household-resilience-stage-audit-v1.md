# Food-shock transmission and household resilience stage audit

**Status:** sixty-second active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** agricultural trade, conflict, weather, fertilizer, shipping,
international and domestic food prices, social protection, household budget
room, and resilience

## Purpose

This audit follows the food-shock chain from geopolitical and climate exposure
through trade and input markets into prices, household room, coping, and public
protection. It keeps forecasts, market indicators, country aggregates, and
household survey evidence separate. A well-supplied global market is not
treated as proof of affordable food for every household.

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Evidence with unit, date, and denominator | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade integration creates both buffers and exposure | FAO SOCO 2026 reports a fivefold increase in global food and agricultural trade between 2000 and 2024, reaching about $2 trillion. Countries with more trade partners are generally more resilient, and global trade often adjusts to shocks within months. | Diversified trade can move food across local shocks, while interconnected routes also transmit conflict, weather, finance, and logistics risk. | Trade value and network connectivity do not show household affordability, nutrition, distributional gains, or resilience in every commodity and country. |
| Policy can transmit a shock across borders | FAO SOCO reports export restrictions affected 8% of globally traded calories during the COVID period versus 16% during 2007–08; price spikes can persist without equally strong downward movements. | State trade decisions and asymmetric price adjustment are mechanisms between geopolitical shocks and food access. | The system-level comparison does not estimate household incidence or isolate restriction, inventories, transport, and market-power effects. |
| Inputs and weather create a lagged production risk | FAO Food Outlook June 2026 reports fertilizer trade volumes down 20–25% in January–April 2026 year over year, while 2026 global cereal output was forecast near 2,982 million tonnes, about 2% below the prior year but historically elevated. | Input-market stress can precede later yield and price effects even when current aggregate supply remains ample. | Trade volume is not farm application, output loss, or consumer pass-through; a forecast is not a realized harvest. |
| Prices can rise despite a large global supply frame | FAO FPMA Bulletin 7 reports the August 2026 Food Price Index at 133.3 points, up 1.9% month over month and 2.5% year over year; the Cereal Price Index rose 2.2%, wheat prices 2.6% month over month and 15% year over year. | Price pressure can coexist with a second-largest projected cereal harvest and a projected 31.6% stocks-to-use ratio. Distribution, logistics, input risk, and risk premiums matter alongside physical scarcity. | International quotations are not household food baskets; exchange rates, taxes, margins, transport, substitution, and safety nets mediate local impact. |
| Exposure is unequal across income groups | World Bank June 2026 food-security update reports food inflation above 5% in 45.0% of low-income countries, 36.7% of lower-middle-income, 29.8% of upper-middle-income, and 5.1% of high-income countries in the latest 169-country frame. | Country income and fiscal room condition the price-to-security pathway. | A country share is not a population or household share and does not measure diet change, transfers, or food insecurity within countries. |
| Household fragility turns shocks into reversals | PPRC Bangladesh State of the Real Economy 2026: nationally representative survey of 8,067 households; 27.9% were below the report's upper poverty line and 18.0% were classified as vulnerable non-poor. The report identifies essentials absorbing income, debt, food insecurity, chronic illness, weak savings, and governance friction as a cumulative risk stack. | Household resilience can coexist with near-poverty and limited capacity to absorb another price, health, or income shock. | This is cross-sectional household evidence and does not estimate causal shock transmission, duration, or a universal Bangladesh-to-global comparison. |
| Coping is an active but costly layer | The PPRC report describes work, migration, social networks, asset use, digital participation, and social protection as coping routes, while warning that coping cannot indefinitely substitute for reliable institutions. | Resilience is a household practice with potential depletion, not proof of secure welfare or institutional success. | The source does not quantify unpaid time, asset depletion, diet quality, later mobility, or which coping route protected which outcome. |

## Cross-source finding

The strongest supported pattern is **buffered but unequal transmission**:

```text
conflict, weather, energy, fertilizer, or trade-policy shock
  -> input, shipping, and commodity-market adjustment
  -> international and domestic food-price movement
  -> country fiscal and import capacity
  -> household budget room, diet, work, debt, and coping
  -> social protection, public legitimacy, and recovery or reversal
```

The market stages are well documented in aggregate. The household stage is
visible in Bangladesh and in broader food-security frames, but the full chain
is not observed in one household. This produces an important counterexample to
simple shortage narratives: cereal supply and stocks can remain historically
large while wheat prices rise and low-income countries face much higher food
inflation exposure. It also counters the opposite claim that any global price
increase means universal food insecurity: trade diversification, stocks, local
production, transfers, and substitution can buffer some places and groups.

## Bounded public discussion

Public discussion adds interpretation and legitimacy questions, not prevalence:

| Platform | Signal | Analytical use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | FAO frames food-market resilience as the way conflict, extreme weather, pandemics, financial crises, and trade disruption transmit into food security; replies emphasize moving from just-in-time toward shock-resilient supply chains. | Makes network design and shock transmission visible; institutional dissemination and replies are not independent household evidence. |
| LinkedIn | A CPI interpretation separates headline inflation from core inflation and highlights food, energy, shelter, and medical-care components. | Shows how aggregate statistics become competing household-cost narratives; it does not establish each household's basket or welfare. |
| LinkedIn | An energy-transition discussion links affordability, energy security, foreign dependence, and confidence in grid readiness. | Adds the energy-input and legitimacy connection; reported attitudes are not consumption or voting behavior. |
| LinkedIn | A CBO outlook announcement frames tariffs, immigration, fiscal policy, aging, and AI as interacting forces affecting prices, labor, and growth. | Extends the macro-policy framing; a forecast is conditional and not a distributional outcome. |

The discussion layer reinforces the next test: people and institutions ask not
only whether food exists, but whether a shock is passed through, who absorbs
it, and whether the public system provides a credible buffer.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not treat a $2 trillion trade system or fivefold trade growth as food
  security.
- Do not treat fertilizer trade decline as a realized yield or price effect.
- Do not treat the FAO index as a household cost-of-living measure.
- Do not treat low-income-country food inflation exposure as the share of
  people or households affected.
- Do not treat resilience, migration, or social networks as costless coping.
- Do not infer a causal effect from PPRC cross-sectional poverty or vulnerability
  status.
- Keep producer, trader, state, retailer, household, and humanitarian actors
  separate.
- Public posts are bounded interpretation, not representative public opinion.

## Stronger next test

Build a country-commodity-household episode ledger with:

1. dated shock, commodity, trade route, fertilizer/energy exposure, and policy;
2. farm, wholesale, retail, and household price or availability measures;
3. household food budget, diet, income, debt, work, transfers, and coping;
4. stock, import, transport, and market-power conditions;
5. food-assistance notice, receipt, adequacy, and public response; and
6. later diet, asset, health, migration, political trust, action, or recovery.

Begin with a place- and staple-specific public episode where domestic prices,
household survey measures, and a dated policy or shock overlap. Do not join a
global forecast to a household outcome by year alone.

## Deeper question

**When does a connected food system absorb a geopolitical shock, and when does
it convert that shock into unequal household loss despite adequate aggregate
supply?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is complete only when one country-commodity or same-household episode
links shock exposure to price/availability, coping or protection, and a later
food, health, work, political, or recovery outcome—or when the source owner
confirms the missing fields cannot be obtained. The broader 14-theme program
remains active.
