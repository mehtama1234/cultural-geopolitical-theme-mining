# Local firm formation, survival, place capacity, and practical access audit

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** business applications, employer-firm formation, small-firm support,
disaster liquidity, firm survival, local services, jobs, and place retention

## Question

When a place reports entrepreneurial activity or small-business growth, does it
create durable firms, jobs, services, and belonging—or only applications and
short-lived entry while costs, support gaps, and policy design push smaller
firms out?

```text
idea or need
  -> application and financing
  -> permits, customers, suppliers, workers, and employer transition
  -> revenue, wages, service availability, and survival
  -> shock, support, competition, or exit
  -> local capacity, mobility, belonging, and household choice
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Comparable evidence | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applications are not employer firms | NBER links the universe of U.S. business applications to later employer-firm outcomes and treats entry as two stages: pursuing an idea, then implementing it as an employer firm. High-startup locations show high application intensity, while low-startup locations show low transition rates; application and transition margins relate differently to local conditions. | Administrative universe of business applications linked to later employer-firm outcomes; local comparison. | The abstract does not follow household resources, financing, leases, permits, customers, service quality, belonging, or closure reason. Employer formation is not durable survival or good jobs. |
| Support rules can redistribute capacity | An NBER quasi-random timing design using Census administrative outcomes finds that expanding small-business support eligibility toward larger firms is followed by lower revenue among affected smaller firms, especially younger, more productive, and financially constrained firms; procurement shifts toward larger firms, while small-firm exits rise and wages and patenting fall. | Firm-level causal design with administrative Census outcomes. | Abstract-level evidence does not expose program identity, effect sizes, worker hours/benefits, customer prices, contract quality, or local service changes. Scale can bring quality or resilience; crowd-out is not automatically social harm. |
| Emergency liquidity can preserve firms after shocks | Revised NBER disaster-loan estimates find recovery loans reduce exit by 13.2 percentage points for all firms and 16.9 points for employer firms; employer employment rises 0.376 log points and revenue 0.719 log points. Delinquent debt falls 0.339, days late fall about 35, private debt rises $17,712 thousand per the table's reported unit, and private debt paid on time rises $16,369 thousand. | Instrumental-variable firm-level study using loan applications, Census outcomes, and business-credit records; post-disaster follow-up up to seven years. | Outcome panels and units differ; the retained source does not provide full loan terms, code, firm identifiers, worker pay, household spillovers, or whether all recovery gains are socially desirable. |
| Local business viability is relational, not only cost-based | Gallup finds 48% of business owners hold a positive view of their local area as a place to run a business, 42% say real-estate costs deter development, 43% say customer access is good, 34% say business-to-business support is helpful, and 31% report good professional-supplier access. | Gallup Pathways to Wealth survey, September 26–November 3, 2025; business owners recruited mainly from a probability-based panel. | Sample size, sector composition, and full weighting are not in the public article; ratings are perceptions, not actual costs, customer volume, or service quality. |
| Growth can increase mobility pressure | Gallup reports 11% of owners with a negative local-climate view are likely to leave within 12 months versus 5% among owners without a negative view. Among owners expecting revenue growth, 19% of dissatisfied owners are likely to relocate versus 5% of satisfied or neutral owners. | Cross-sectional subgroup comparisons. | Intention is not relocation or firm exit; firm performance may shape both satisfaction and mobility plans. Growth-oriented owners may simply have more options. |
| Firm size and household access can diverge | New York Fed Small Business Credit Survey evidence reports a 13-point profitability diffusion index for employer firms at the end of 2023 versus 46 in December 2019; the smallest firms drove lower revenue reports, while 2026 revenue expectations fell six points year over year. Small-firm employment still increased, and debt averaged about $70,000 per employee. | Weighted survey of employer firms; question-specific universes differ and recruitment is non-random. | Directional survey measures are not realized survival, wages, hours, customer access, or affordable credit. Small-firm employment growth can coexist with lower revenue or lower pay. |
| Place retention is also a life-stage bargain | Pew focus groups with 54 Philadelphia residents ages 18–29 identify pay, work-life boundaries, health benefits, advancement, transit, safety, mentorship, and hybrid/in-person learning as job-quality and place-retention conditions. Census context shows a net inflow of about 16,000 young adults per year in 2020–2024, followed by an outflow pattern after around age 30. | Ten facilitated 90-minute focus groups plus descriptive Census context. | Qualitative priorities are not representative or causal; migration does not reveal why a particular person moved, stayed, commuted, or returned. |

## What we found

The evidence supports a **formation-to-capacity gap**. Applications measure
intent or pursuit. Employer transition measures implementation. Survival and
employment measure a later firm stage. Local services, worker security, and
place retention are later still.

Policy can affect different links in opposite directions. A support rule may
increase scale while reducing smaller-firm revenue and entry. Emergency loans
may prevent disaster exit and unlock private credit, but only for firms reached
by the program and able to use debt. A place can attract young adults or
business applications while losing people or firms when customer access,
housing, safety, care, transit, mentorship, or growth pathways remain weak.

Do not treat these as one “business climate” measure:

1. **Pursuit:** an application or stated entrepreneurial intention.
2. **Implementation:** employer-firm formation, financing, permits, and workers.
3. **Durability:** revenue, wages, repayment, survival, and shock recovery.
4. **Local conversion:** services, jobs, customer choice, social infrastructure,
   and whether households can stay or leave on acceptable terms.

## Curiosity checks

### Where did the pattern fail?

- Low transition from application to employer firm may reflect deliberate
  non-employer activity, not failed entrepreneurship.
- Larger firms may deliver scale, quality, resilience, or lower prices even when
  support reallocates contracts away from smaller firms.
- Disaster loans can preserve firms and produce local entry spillovers, but debt
  can also create future exposure; survival is not automatically household
  welfare.
- Owners may rate a place negatively because of their own firm performance, not
  because local conditions caused the performance.
- Young-adult out-migration after age 30 may reflect family choice or regional
  mobility rather than local economic failure.

### What else could explain the differences?

1. **Capacity explanation:** finance, customers, suppliers, workers, and public
   services determine whether an idea becomes a durable local firm.
2. **Selection explanation:** stronger firms and more mobile owners choose places
   with better networks, so observed place associations are not causal.
3. **Allocation explanation:** public support changes who receives contracts and
   credit; aggregate activity can rise while smaller firms lose room.

### What would change the answer?

A place-linked firm panel that follows applications through employer transition,
capital, customers, wages, survival, public support, local service provision,
household exposure, and later exit or relocation would show whether firm growth
creates durable local capacity or merely rotates activity between firms and
places.

## Bounded public discussion

| Platform | Signal | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | Harvard Business School relays the disaster-loan research as a survival, employment, bankruptcy, and private-credit pathway. | Short research relay; firm recovery is not household welfare or proof of equal access to loans. |
| LinkedIn | A restaurant-industry discussion frames pressured households as trading replaceable occasions toward cheaper channels while preserving social or emotional occasions that feel worth the price. | Industry interpretation, not representative household behavior or firm survival evidence. |
| LinkedIn | A business account describes a large software renewal increase followed by an eventual switch, making switching costs and customer exit visible. | One unverified account and selected professional audience. |
| LinkedIn | A livable-cities discussion links resilience to residents, natural systems, built infrastructure, and local economies. | Institutional framing, not a city-level outcome evaluation or distributional cost study. |

These discussions add meaning to the measured stages: firms are not only
production units, and places are not only cost bundles. They are routes to
services, identity, social life, and future choice. The posts do not establish
prevalence or causal local outcomes.

## Stronger next test

Follow one local firm and its surrounding place across the complete route:

```text
application and owner resources
  -> financing, permits, customers, suppliers, workers
  -> employer transition, revenue, wages, service availability
  -> support, shock, competition, debt, or policy change
  -> survival, closure, relocation, worker/household effect, and local access
```

Compare new employer firms, non-employer firms, supported and unsupported firms,
small and large recipients, and places with different customer access, housing,
transport, care, and supplier capacity. Preserve cases where larger firms
improve service or resilience, and cases where small-firm support preserves
locally important but financially fragile capacity.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** Applications and firm formation are distinct stages; support
and emergency liquidity can alter survival, employment, and credit, but local
business capacity also depends on customers, suppliers, public services, worker
quality of life, and whether households can remain. The missing middle is a
place-linked firm episode connecting formation to durable service and social
capacity.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call a place entrepreneurial, business-friendly, or locally resilient
from applications, owner sentiment, employment direction, loan receipt, or
survival alone. Require a compatible firm/place sequence with implementation,
revenue or wages, service/customer conversion, shock response, and later
survival, relocation, or household access, plus a counterexample.
