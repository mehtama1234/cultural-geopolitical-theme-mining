# Drought becomes a firm and household-demand shock in Tunisia

## Finding

The World Bank’s new study links approximately two decades of Tunisia’s formal-firm registry data to delegation-level precipitation shocks. Across more than 1.4 million firm-year observations, a drought—defined as precipitation one standard deviation below the long-term average—reduces total revenue by about 12.4% and revenue per worker by 11.4%. The full sample shows no statistically significant change in worker counts.

The average hides uneven adjustment. Large firms are more exposed in revenue terms. Foreign-owned and exporting firms are more revenue-resilient, but foreign firms’ workforces fall more under drought, consistent with preserving output through capital-labor substitution. Exporting firms are more able to shield employment. Services and health show significant revenue-per-worker declines; wholesale and retail worker counts rise without a corresponding significant revenue gain.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a climate-to-firm-and-household mechanism:

`drought -> water and production shock -> firm revenue and productivity loss -> differentiated capital, export, and labor adjustment -> sectoral reallocation -> household income, health demand, and local economic security`

The key shift is from treating climate exposure as an aggregate agricultural problem to seeing it as a differentiated organizational and consumer shock. Firms with external markets, foreign resources, or export capability may protect revenue, while workers can still bear the adjustment. In health and tourism-linked services, household demand and place-based economic activity may transmit the shock beyond the directly water-dependent firm.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- “Firm resilience” can mean preserved revenue with fewer workers, so output stability is not equivalent to worker security.
- Export integration and foreign ownership may create adaptation capacity unavailable to domestic and non-exporting firms.
- Drought can reach households through health spending, tourism, local services, employment, and credit—not only food or farm income.
- Wholesale and retail worker reallocation may represent coping and sectoral displacement rather than improved opportunity.
- Formal climate adaptation policy must account for informal firms and workers, who are absent from the registry and may carry greater exposure.

## Boundary and next test

This is Tunisia-specific observational firm-panel evidence. It excludes informal firms and does not establish effects on wages, household consumption, health outcomes, water access, investment, credit, or political legitimacy. The next test is a named drought episode linking firm responses to worker earnings, informal activity, household health spending, food and water prices, public water provision, and local political attribution.
