# The social economy is becoming a market institution: inclusion depends on who controls scale, finance and impact

## Finding

The OECD's ASEAN paper identifies a regional shift from treating poverty, informality and service gaps only as problems for the state or charity toward building inclusive businesses and social enterprises as market institutions. The paper says Southeast Asia has approximately 244 million informal workers, only 33 percent of the population benefits from at least one social-protection scheme, and nearly one million social enterprises operate across the region.

The important finding is not that social enterprise is automatically inclusive. It is that inclusion is being designed as a value-chain role: low-income people may be suppliers, distributors, retailers, workers or customers. That creates a new political and cultural question about scale. Who controls the business, who defines impact, who gets affordable access, and whether commercial growth preserves solidarity rather than converting it into brand language?

## Cross-domain interpretation

`social need and exclusion -> mission-driven experiment -> recurring market role -> finance/accreditation/scale -> affordable access and work -> impact measurement and accountability`

The paper distinguishes inclusive businesses, which integrate low-income populations into core commercially viable value chains, from social enterprises, which pursue explicit social or environmental objectives and may pilot solutions that later scale. Examples include smallholder integration, sustainable food, affordable education, vocational training, circularity and employment for marginalised groups.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- The social economy turns values such as solidarity, people-over-capital and democratic participation into an organisational and consumer-market claim. That claim requires governance and measurement, not only mission statements.
- Consumers in underserved communities are not merely beneficiaries. They become customers whose affordability, quality and repeat access determine whether an inclusive model is genuinely useful.
- Scaling changes cultural meaning. A local social enterprise may retain community control, while a scaled inclusive business can reach more people but introduce investor priorities, standardisation, data extraction or mission drift.
- Finance is a time problem as well as a capital problem. Seed grants may support experimentation while the missing middle—often funding below USD 200,000 and later-stage growth finance—prevents a solution from becoming durable.
- Accreditation and impact measurement can make claims more credible, but they can also privilege organisations that can document outcomes and exclude informal, care-based or community forms of social economy.
- The emerging ASEAN framework is therefore a public-private legitimacy system: legal recognition, finance, skills, procurement, customer access, data and accountability determine whether “inclusive” becomes a lived market relationship.

## Boundary and next test

This is a regional policy and case synthesis, not causal evidence that inclusive businesses reduce poverty or improve welfare. The next test is a named ASEAN enterprise or programme followed through beneficiary prices, worker conditions, customer affordability, supplier bargaining power, public support, investor control, impact measurement and mission continuity after scale.
