# U.S. school finance, long-run output, and the local conversion gap

**Status:** provisional place/public-systems synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-10-19

## Bounded finding

State school-finance reforms can have a long-run economic effect, but the path
from public investment to lived opportunity is slow and unevenly measured. An
NBER study reports little predicted change in state earnings per worker during
the first 10–15 years after reform, followed by an estimated 4.7% increase in
earnings per worker and more than 5% higher GDP per capita after 30 years. A
60-year cohort-replacement result of 7.8% is simulated rather than observed.

The evidence supports this bounded chain:

```text
school-finance reform
  -> school resources and educational capacity
  -> student learning, completion, and workforce capability
  -> earnings and state output after a long lag
  -> [open] equal local opportunity, household room, voice, and legitimacy
```

## Evidence comparison without overclaiming

| Evidence surface | Result | Unit and boundary | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short and medium horizon | Little predicted earnings change during the first 10–15 years | State-level earnings path and model estimates | Student-level learning, local implementation, migration, and who bears early costs |
| Long-run earnings | About 4.7% higher state earnings per worker after 30 years | Staggered state reforms and event-study framework | Distribution across students, races, places, workers, and household types |
| Long-run output | More than 5% higher GDP per capita after 30 years | State-level aggregate output | Whether output becomes wages, public revenue, services, or household consumption |
| Very long horizon | 7.8% higher earnings per worker after 60 years | Cohort-replacement simulation | This is extrapolated, not an observed 60-year reform outcome |
| Distributional counterpoint | A separate reform study estimates that a 20% annual per-pupil spending increase across all 12 school years produced about 0.9 more completed schooling years, 25% higher earnings, and a 20-point lower annual adult-poverty incidence for children from poor families; it found no comparable effects for children from non-poor families | Earlier reform-induced spending variation linked to nationally representative child and adult outcomes | This is a distinct study and treatment contrast, not a decomposition of the newer macro estimate; it still does not show current local implementation or household voice |
| Mechanism boundary | Results are consistent with private earnings gains aggregating to output rather than only signaling or displacement | Study-level interpretation | Exact reform features, school quality, teacher capacity, migration, and general-equilibrium channels |

The measures are not interchangeable. State earnings are not student learning;
GDP is not household security; and a long-run average does not identify which
places received usable capacity or which families could remain, move, or contest
the system.

## What this adds to the atlas

Public investment can produce a genuine economic return while remaining nearly
invisible to the households living through the early period. The lag matters:
families and local institutions may experience costs, uneven school quality,
teacher shortages, or migration before aggregate gains appear. Even when the
state-level effect is real, it does not show that every student received an
adequate school, that every worker captured higher earnings, or that local
public capacity improved equally.

The key distinctions are:

```text
more school funding       != equal school quality
state earnings growth     != household room
GDP per capita             != student wellbeing
long-run return            != short-run political legitimacy
aggregate capacity        != usable local access
reform enactment           != durable institutional trust
```

This places education inside the broader program’s material and political
question: who can wait thirty years for a return, who receives the first-order
benefit, and whether families can exercise voice or exit when the local system
does not work for them.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Aggregate gains can coexist with unequal student, racial, household, and
  place outcomes.
- The distributional counterpoint indicates that the private return can be
  concentrated among children from poor families even when the newer macro
  result is reported as a state average; the studies should not be pooled as
  if they estimate the same treatment or outcome.
- Reforms may coincide with migration, industrial change, demographic shifts,
  and other education policies.
- The abstract does not disclose state and cohort counts, confidence intervals,
  exact reform features, or all event-study assumptions.
- The 60-year result is simulated and should not be reported as an observed
  reform effect.
- The evidence does not measure teacher conditions, student voice, school
  quality, local service access, household spending, or political response.

## Next decisive test

Use a place- and cohort-linked design that follows:

```text
reform exposure and local spending
  -> school staffing, facilities, course access, and student learning
  -> completion, migration, college/work entry, and early earnings
  -> household resources, housing, health, and civic participation
  -> local public capacity, trust, voice, and later political response
```

Include places with similar funding growth but different implementation,
students who remain versus move, and schools where aggregate resources rise
without usable access. Preserve the long lag and test whether early losses or
unequal distribution are hidden by the state average.
