# Preparedness is a global public good with a domestic budget problem

## Finding

The OECD's *Tracking spending on prevention, preparedness and response to global health threats* makes a political-economic problem visible: the capacities that prevent a crisis are globally shared, but their budgets are nationally contested. Prevention, surveillance, infection control, emergency response and research reduce cross-border risk, yet their benefits are delayed, diffuse and difficult to credit to a particular government.

The fiscal asymmetry is stark. In 2023, high-income countries spent about USD 75 per person on PPR and less than 2% of health expenditure; low-income countries spent less than USD 4 per person, but that represented about 10% of their health expenditure. Nearly three-quarters of low-income-country PPR spending with available data came from external sources, while OECD projects health ODA to fall 29–46% from 2024 to 2026.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`global risk -> delayed and diffuse prevention benefit -> weak domestic political credit -> underinvestment -> aid dependence in frontline states -> fragile collective resilience`

The publication's measurement proposal is therefore political infrastructure, not merely accounting. A Global Health and Resilience Tracker can make prevention legible across health, agriculture, animal health, development, research and technology budgets. Visibility may help governments and publics ask whether resources match exposure, but measurement alone does not guarantee capability, equity or trust.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and geopolitical significance

- Preparedness is an invisible social contract. People experience its value mainly when a system detects a threat early, keeps routine care functioning, protects workers, or prevents a crisis from becoming ordinary life.
- Aid dependence turns global solidarity into a sovereignty question. Countries on the frontline of shared risks may be asked to maintain collective protection through financing decisions made elsewhere.
- The burden is not only epidemiological. Thin preparedness budgets compete with immediate health needs, while the poorest countries face both lower per-capita capacity and higher relative budget pressure.
- The emerging theme is “resilience accounting”: societies need shared ways to count the infrastructures and institutions whose success is the absence of an event. This can support bargaining and accountability, but can also narrow the meaning of preparedness to what existing metrics can see.
- The risk portfolio is widening beyond pandemics to antimicrobial resistance, chemical, nuclear and radiological events, and biotechnology- and AI-enabled biological risks. Preparedness is consequently becoming a cross-sector governance problem rather than a narrow health-ministry function.
- For consumers and workers, the downstream question is not whether a country has a preparedness strategy but whether surveillance, laboratories, supply chains, infection controls, emergency services and public communication actually work when routine life is disrupted.

## Boundary and next test

This is an OECD policy brief and financing/measurement synthesis, not causal evidence that any specific aid cut will produce a particular outbreak or that a tracker will improve outcomes. The next test is a named country or regional health-security episode that follows financing through to laboratory capacity, worker protection, supply continuity, public communication, access, remedy and political trust.
