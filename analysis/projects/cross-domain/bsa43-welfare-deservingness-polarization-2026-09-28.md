# Welfare is a cultural boundary, not a single public attitude

**Checked:** 2026-09-28  
**Source:** National Centre for Social Research, *British Social Attitudes 43: Attitudes to welfare*  
**Record:** [machine-readable source record](../../records/bsa43-welfare-deservingness-polarization-2026.json)

## Core finding

The public does not hold one stable attitude called “welfare opinion.” In 2025,
49% supported more spending on benefits for disabled people who cannot work and
only 7% supported less; 63% put disability benefits among their top two extra-
spending priorities, above retirement pensions at 53%. Even among people who
opposed more welfare spending for the poor, only 11% supported less spending on
disabled people, compared with 67% supporting less spending on unemployed people.

The cultural mechanism is deservingness: public protection follows the imagined
claimant and the perceived moral boundary around need, blame, work, and group
membership. That boundary is increasingly partisan, but it is not unlimited:
even right-leaning voters remain broadly protective of disability benefits.

## Cultural/social interpretation

The important signal is not simply that support for disability benefits is high.
It is that a broad “welfare” label hides a sorting process. People can resist
more welfare spending while defending a claimant category they regard as needy,
blameless, or recognizably deserving. Political actors therefore do not face a
single public appetite for cuts; they face a map of protected and contestable
recipient prototypes.

This also explains why reform communication is unstable. A proposal can sound
like fiscal discipline in the abstract, then lose legitimacy when the public
learns which bodies, conditions, ages, or households will actually bear the
change. The 2025 data remain especially contextual: the report notes a wording
break in 2024 and the possibility that the failed PIP-cut episode temporarily
raised sympathy.

## Open middle

The next pass should join:

```text
fiscal pressure / political rhetoric / claimant prototype
  -> deservingness judgment and partisan sorting
  -> support or resistance to a specific reform
  -> household security, service access, and political legitimacy
```

The BSA chapter measures attitudes, not benefit adequacy, claimant outcomes,
media causality, or reform effects. The wording discontinuity means 2024 should
not be treated as a clean trend point.
