# Ghana’s “good” tariff access becomes a compliance-infrastructure problem

## Finding

The World Bank’s new analysis of Ghana’s export markets finds that preferential access can make tariffs near zero in major developed markets while leaving substantial non-tariff barriers in place. Across the paper’s partner-market comparison, 87% of Ghana’s trade value is subject to at least one non-tariff measure; agricultural product lines face approximately 100% coverage.

For cocoa, cocoa paste and butter, coconuts, and cashews, access depends on sanitary and phytosanitary rules, technical barriers, conformity assessment, quality standards, testing, labeling, and documentation. The paper warns that smaller firms may lack the capacity to absorb these costs.

The EU Deforestation Regulation makes the mechanism more concrete. About 60% of Ghana’s cocoa products go to the EU, and compliance requires detailed farm-to-port traceability. The paper describes Ghana’s digital farm mapping, farmer registration, and proposed sustainability premium as attempts to convert environmental regulation into export continuity.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a tariff-to-compliance-infrastructure mechanism:

`preferential tariff access -> SPS/TBT and environmental requirements -> testing, certification, data, and traceability costs -> selective firm/farmer participation -> export continuity, consumer assurance, and contested distribution of compliance burdens`

The key shift is from asking whether a country has market access to asking who can operationalize it. Open borders on paper can remain closed in practice for firms and farmers without testing capacity, digital identity, mapping, finance, or reliable certification.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Trade openness increasingly depends on invisible infrastructures of evidence, identity, quality, and environmental proof.
- Compliance can protect consumers and forests while also selecting against smaller firms and shifting fixed costs onto farmers.
- Digital traceability may improve farmer visibility and service access, but it raises questions about data rights, verification authority, and who receives premiums.
- Ghana’s cocoa relationship with the EU turns an external environmental rule into a domestic governance problem involving COCOBOD, exporters, farmers, households, and forest communities.
- Regional trade in light manufactured goods offers a possible diversification path if behind-the-border infrastructure and energy constraints are addressed.

## Boundary and next test

This is descriptive market-access and regulatory evidence. It does not establish that NTMs caused export exclusion, that Ghana’s mapping is complete, that farmers receive the proposed premium, or that digital compliance improves household welfare. The next test is a named cocoa or small-exporter episode tracking certification cost, firm entry/exit, farmer participation, premium receipt, data control, consumer prices, and environmental outcomes.
