# LSHTM health misinformation: information exposure becomes a health-capacity and trust problem

## Finding

The London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine's September 2026 survey of 2,110 UK adults adds a health-specific information-to-capacity mechanism. Nearly one in five adults reported low confidence judging whether a health claim was accurate, but confidence varied sharply by income and education: 56% of people earning over £40,000 were confident compared with 34% earning under £20,000; 55% with level 4 or higher qualifications were confident compared with 34% lacking level 1 qualifications.

Social media was the most common reported exposure route at 61%, followed by websites or blogs at 30%, family or friends at 27%, traditional news at 26%, colleagues at 20%, and AI chatbots at 19%. Younger adults reported higher stakes: 22% of 18–24 year-olds said they had made a health decision based on misinformation that they later regretted, and 38% said they knew someone who had suffered serious harm after such a decision.

The public's preferred response is not only more information. People favored penalties for those profiting from false or misleading health claims, public education, and platform warning labels. The broader theme is practical judgment: people need a trusted path from exposure to checking, care, and correction, and that path is unequally distributed by resources and education.

## Theme and subthemes

- **Health-information capability gap:** confidence in evaluating claims varies with income and educational attainment.
- **Platform-to-care pathway:** social and AI exposure can precede decisions about treatment, vaccines, supplements, reproductive health, and mental health.
- **Trust handoff:** people use NHS/government sites, clinicians, family, social media, and AI as overlapping checking routes.
- **Harm and legitimacy:** regret, known harm, and family worry generate demand for public action, platform responsibility, and trustworthy institutions.

## What the source supports

The source supports descriptive self-reported patterns from an online-panel survey commissioned and funded by LSHTM and conducted by Survation on 9–10 September 2026. It does not verify claim accuracy, clinically confirm harm, or estimate intervention effects.

## Next test

Connect a named NHS, clinician, pharmacist, school, platform, AI, media-literacy, labeling, or enforcement intervention to verified claim accuracy, timely care navigation, treatment continuity, equitable access to trusted information, and durable health-service trust. The key test is whether correction reaches people before harm and remains usable across income, education, age, and platform contexts.

## Boundary

Confidence is not accuracy, exposure is not proven misinformation, and reported regret is not a clinically verified outcome. The online-panel method and smaller regional samples limit generalization; the survey is a baseline, not an evaluation.

Source: [LSHTM, Health Misinformation: Public Perceptions of Threat to the UK](https://www.lshtm.ac.uk/research/research-action/features/health-misinformation-public-perceptions-threat-uk)
