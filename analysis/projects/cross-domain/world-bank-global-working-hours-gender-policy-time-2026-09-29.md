# Development redistributes working time rather than simply reducing it

## Finding

The World Bank’s new *Global Working Hours* paper builds a labor-force-survey microdatabase covering 160 countries and 97% of the world population, with time series spanning more than 20 years in 86 countries.

Hours worked per adult decline only slightly with GDP per capita. The more important pattern is redistribution across age and gender. Young people aged 15–19 and older adults aged 60+ work less as development rises, associated with greater school attendance and public pension coverage. Prime-age hours remain broadly stable, while falling male hours per worker are offset in many countries by rising female labor-force participation. Globally, women supply 35% of GDP-producing hours and men 65%.

## Cross-domain interpretation

This adds a development-to-time-allocation mechanism:

`development and institutions -> schooling, pensions, taxes, transfers, and working-time rules -> age/gender allocation of paid work -> household time, care, consumption, rest, and bargaining -> social meaning of security`

The cultural significance is that development does not simply make everyone work less. It changes who is expected or able to work, when paid work begins and ends, how long men and women work, and whether income security comes from wages, pensions, transfers, or unpaid household labor. “Shorter working hours” can therefore mean protection, exclusion, redistribution, or hidden care burdens depending on the group.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Schooling and pensions reorganize the life course by moving paid work away from the young and elderly.
- Gender equality in paid hours may reflect both more women entering employment and men working fewer hours; it does not by itself establish equal pay, job quality, autonomy, or care sharing.
- Labor taxes, social spending, and work-time regulation appear intertwined rather than separable as a simple “taxes reduce work” story.
- Time is a household resource: policy can alter capacity for care, rest, consumption, civic participation, and managing multiple jobs, but those downstream outcomes remain unmeasured here.
- Cross-country cultural differences matter: female hours are substantially lower in Muslim- and Hindu-majority countries and higher in former communist countries, though the paper does not identify the causal role of norms versus institutions, sector structure, or selection.

## Boundary and next test

This is cross-country survey and regression evidence. It excludes unpaid domestic and care work from its core hours measure and does not establish causal policy effects, job quality, household welfare, subjective time pressure, or political change. The next test is a named country-policy episode that follows working-time rules, pensions, taxes, and female employment into unpaid care, household consumption, health, rest, bargaining, and public legitimacy.
