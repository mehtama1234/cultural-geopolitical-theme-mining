# Trade facilitation is not gender-neutral when infrastructure, information and care determine who can reach the border

## Finding

The World Bank's India study shows that digitized and formally simplified trade procedures do not automatically produce inclusive market access. Women are less represented in trade-related services, and the barriers identified by the study are practical and cumulative: care time, movement and safety, fragmented or non-local-language information, limited consultation, inaccessible grievance routes, weak facilities and constrained finance.

The central finding is that a border is not only a legal or digital interface. It is a workplace and a time-use system. A trade-facilitation reform can be formally gender-neutral while remaining materially gendered if the people expected to use it cannot safely travel, wait, understand the rules, finance a transaction, obtain care coverage, or trust the complaint process.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`trade reform and digitization -> information and procedure access -> travel, waiting, safety and care burden -> firm participation and bargaining room -> complaint/remedy -> repeat market access`

The study interviewed 626 traders and 401 customs brokers and visited land ports, air cargo terminals, inland container depots and warehouses. Among the reported barriers, 54 percent of women traders found trade information incomplete, 50 percent found it unavailable in local languages, 64 percent of women customs brokers reported safety concerns during port visits, and close to one-third of women-led trading firms identified finance as their top expansion challenge.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Border infrastructure encodes social assumptions about who can travel, wait late, use sanitation and rest facilities, access transport, and work without care interruption.
- “Digital trade facilitation” can shift friction from the counter to the interface. If the interface is fragmented, English-dominant, hard to navigate or disconnected from human help, formal transparency may coexist with practical opacity.
- Consultation is a distributional mechanism. Large firms were more likely than micro firms to report being consulted on border-procedure changes, so the firms most able to absorb compliance costs can also have more voice in designing the system.
- Safety and remedy are economic variables. Fear of harassment or retaliation can reduce field participation, alter occupational choice, and prevent a formally available complaint mechanism from becoming usable protection.
- Care infrastructure, multilingual information, finance and safe facilities are not peripheral “women’s” additions to trade policy. They determine who can enter, remain, expand and exercise bargaining power in a supposedly open market.

## Boundary and next test

This is descriptive assessment evidence, not causal proof that any recommendation will close the participation gap. The next test is a named border or trade-facilitation episode that follows women-led firms through information access, travel and waiting time, safety, finance, clearance, complaint or appeal, transaction costs, repeat trading and firm survival.
