# Digital creators are becoming information intermediaries faster than their support systems can mature

## Finding

UNESCO’s South Africa research describes a creator ecosystem with growing influence over how information is produced, interpreted, and shared, but limited formal support for verification, media and information literacy, ethics, freedom of expression, AI disclosure, online safety, and platform governance.

The distinct finding is a legitimacy gap. Creators are increasingly treated as public-facing information actors, while the institutions around them still assume that content responsibility is an individual skill rather than a condition shaped by algorithms, monetisation, commercial partnerships, moderation, audience metrics, and exposure to online harm.

## Cross-domain interpretation

`creator visibility and platform distribution -> information interpretation and audience trust -> metric and commercial incentives -> verification, disclosure, and ethical pressure -> online harm and unequal exposure -> media literacy, peer support, platform voice, and public accountability`

In the survey of 48 South African creators, 43.8% relied on mainstream news as a primary information source, 36% did not systematically verify information before sharing, and 34% used likes and views as a key credibility signal. Only 10.4% had received Media and Information Literacy training; 33% consistently disclosed generative-AI use; and 59% were unaware of regulations relevant to digital content creation.

The risk environment is not abstract. The study reports that 75% had experienced or witnessed technology-facilitated gender-based violence, 72% had experienced or witnessed xenophobia online, and 43% reported identity-based hate speech or harassment. These are self-reported sample findings, not population prevalence or verified incident counts, but they show why creator ethics cannot be separated from safety, identity, platform power, and wellbeing.

## Cultural, social, consumer, and political significance

- Creator influence is becoming part of the information and civic infrastructure, not merely entertainment or personal branding.
- Likes, views, monetisation, and algorithmic amplification can become informal credibility systems.
- AI disclosure and media-literacy support are emerging as conditions of audience trust and cultural authenticity.
- Online harm is unequally distributed and can constrain who speaks, what is produced, and which identities remain visible.
- Creator participation in platform governance matters only if it changes moderation, safety, monetisation, and data practices rather than adding symbolic consultation.

## Boundary and next test

The research uses a small self-reported survey of 48 creators, 11 interviews, and UNESCO programme context. It does not establish causal effects on audiences, creator income, misinformation, wellbeing, or platform behavior. The next test is a named creator or creator cohort followed from content production and platform distribution through AI disclosure, audience trust, harassment, moderation, income, support, and actual influence on public discourse.
