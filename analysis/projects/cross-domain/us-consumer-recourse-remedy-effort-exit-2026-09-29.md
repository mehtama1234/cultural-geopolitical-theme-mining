# Consumer recourse: visibility is expanding, but remedy still leaves effort and residual loss with households

**Rotation date:** 2026-09-29  
**Scope:** consumer loss, complaint routes, institutional remedy, household effort, and exit  
**Status:** cross-source interpretation; no pooled estimate

## Question

When a consumer is harmed by fraud, a service failure, a billing error, or a
coverage decision, what happens between recognizing the problem and actually
recovering money, access, time, or trust?

## What the sources show

- Federal Reserve SHED 2024 estimates that 21% of adults experienced some
  financial fraud. Among adults experiencing non-credit-card fraud, 63% lost
  money, 32% recovered only some or none, and about 30% spent at least ten hours
  dealing with the event or trying to recover. Aggregate estimates imply a
  large residual loss, but the survey does not identify firm liability or
  case-level remedy.
- CFPB complaint records make consumer problems visible at massive scale and
  expose product-specific response routes, but complaint counts and response
  labels are not verified compensation, restored access, or durable consumer
  recovery. Explanation and non-monetary response can rise while monetary
  relief remains rare in the published aggregate surface.
- The Commonwealth Fund's 2025 privately insured working-age survey shows the
  missing time and health middle: denials can delay care, increase costs, worsen
  health, and generate debt; appeals can change outcomes but often take weeks
  or months, and many people do not appeal because they do not know how or do
  not expect it to matter.
- FTC AT&T and other redress records create payment or reissue routes, but
  public aggregate distributions do not show which named consumers received
  money, whether access was restored, what they sacrificed while waiting, or
  whether they switched or trusted the provider afterward.

## Interpretation

Consumer protection has two separate outputs: **problem visibility** and
**burden recovery**. The first is increasingly measurable through complaints,
surveys, orders, and response labels. The second remains harder to observe and
often depends on the consumer's own time, documentation, persistence, literacy,
cash buffer, and ability to leave the product.

Four stages should remain distinct:

1. **Recognition:** the person notices fraud, denial, throttling, a fee, or a
   service failure.
2. **Route:** they find a complaint, appeal, dispute, refund, or reissue path.
3. **Receipt and adequacy:** money, access, correction, or care is actually
   restored, and the remedy covers the residual loss.
4. **Afterlife:** the person can recover time and trust, switch providers, exit,
   or avoid recurrence.

The main consumer-power inequality is therefore not only exposure to harm. It
is the ability to convert a formal route into an adequate remedy without losing
more money, time, health, access, or dignity in the process.

## What this does not establish

These sources use surveys, complaint records, enforcement populations, and
aggregate redress reports. They do not form a common case panel and do not
establish complaint incidence, verified receipt, remedy adequacy, causal trust
change, switching, or exit for the same consumer.

## Next decisive tests

- Follow one named complaint, appeal, refund, or fraud case from exposure through
  effort, response, receipt, residual loss, and later switching or trust.
- Preserve timestamps and failed attempts, not only final disposition.
- Measure who receives remedy, who remains unrecovered, and whether the remedy
  restores access rather than only paying a nominal amount.
- Link remedy completion to household room, work time, care, health, and future
  product choice.

The key open arrow is **consumer harm → route effort → verified remedy and
adequacy → residual loss → trust, switching, or exit**.
