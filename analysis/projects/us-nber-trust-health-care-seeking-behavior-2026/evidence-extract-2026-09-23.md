# Official-source extract: trust and health-care seeking

**Captured:** 2026-09-23  
**Purpose:** preserve the smallest source-grounded extract used by the
machine-readable record. This is not a substitute for the linked NBER paper.

## Official source

- Michael E. Darden and Mario Macis, “Trust and Health Care-Seeking Behavior,”
  NBER Working Paper 32028, January 2024, revised April 2026, [official NBER
  page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32028), [revised paper PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32028/w32028.pdf).

## Extracted claims

- The authors develop a model in which care-seeking depends on beliefs about
  care usefulness, expected disutility, expected out-of-pocket costs, and claim
  denials, and they measure trust in multiple health-care entities rather than
  treating “health-care trust” as one relationship.
- A nationally representative U.S. adult survey measures overall and
  entity-specific trust, retrospective and prospective care-seeking, and the
  proposed mechanisms.
- Higher trust is strongly associated with greater preventive and elective
  care-seeking and lower care avoidance, with reported magnitudes comparable to
  education and income gradients.
- Structural estimates imply that trust across entities can substitute for one
  another overall, while trust components within a domain are more
  complementary, especially for individual providers. Counterfactual trust
  losses have different predicted effects, with trust in doctors largest in the
  reported simulations.
- The reported associations vary by age and political affiliation; the paper
  reports no differential relationship for Black respondents.

## Boundary preserved

The source is survey and model evidence. It does not observe an exogenous trust
shock, a named patient encounter, completed treatment, affordability, health
improvement, or durable trust change. Care avoidance can also reflect cost,
time, transportation, provider supply, health status, or an appropriate choice.
