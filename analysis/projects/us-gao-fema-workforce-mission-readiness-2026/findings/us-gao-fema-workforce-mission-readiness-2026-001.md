# Finding 001: Disaster assistance depends on workforce capacity before it becomes a household outcome

**Status:** bounded U.S. public-capacity and disaster-assistance finding ·
**Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

GAO's 2026 review of FEMA finds a federal workforce under pressure as disaster
frequency and concurrency increase. FEMA averaged about 25,134 employees in
fiscal year 2025, while more than 4,300 separated—55% more separations than in
the prior fiscal year. GAO reports that FEMA had rescinded its strategic plan
and had not based key workforce decisions on a workforce analysis. FEMA
officials attributed part of the problem to lost institutional knowledge.

The operational route is visible but not complete. FEMA deployed 13,500
employees for Hurricanes Helene and Milton; after those disasters, GAO reports
only 4% of the incident-management workforce was available to deploy in
November 2024. FEMA officials anticipated 240 surge-support personnel for the
2026 hurricane season, down from 600 in 2025. GAO also reports long waits and
unanswered calls for Texas flood survivors after reductions in surge and
call-center staffing.

The safe interpretation is:

> Disaster assistance is a capacity system: staffing, institutional memory,
> planning, and surge availability shape the route from federal money to a
> reachable answer, but these conditions do not by themselves prove an
> individual's denial, recovery, or loss.

## Evidence chain

```text
more frequent or concurrent disasters
  -> workforce reductions, planning gaps, and lost institutional knowledge
  -> thinner surge, call-center, and grant-administration capacity
  -> waits, unanswered calls, or harder access to assistance
  -> [open] receipt, housing continuity, income/health recovery, trust, and action
```

## What it adds to the broader atlas

This makes public capacity an intermediate mechanism rather than treating
assistance dollars as automatically delivered relief. It connects the recent
FEMA delivery review's communication and post-disaster housing problems to an
upstream staffing and planning condition, while preserving the distinction
between selected operational evidence and survivor-level outcomes. It also
adds a state-capacity counterweight to the flood firm-reallocation finding:
private and public productive capacity can be changing in different directions
at the same time.

## What the source does not show

- It does not provide a representative contact-failure or denial rate.
- It does not identify which individual survivor was affected by staffing or
  how long an access problem lasted.
- It does not establish that workforce reductions alone caused waits; demand,
  call systems, policy, and application complexity also matter.
- It does not measure assistance receipt, housing stability, income, health,
  trust, political action, or durable recovery.
- Temporary local hires, reservists, cross-agency surge, and future reforms may
  change capacity after the reviewed period.

## Next decisive test

Join event-level disaster demand, FEMA staffing and call-center capacity,
application attempts, notices, assistance receipt, processing time, housing and
income continuity, and later trust or action. Preserve concurrent disasters,
regions, survivor damage levels, and assistance types as separate units, and
test whether staffing changes predict realized access after accounting for
demand and communication-system load.
