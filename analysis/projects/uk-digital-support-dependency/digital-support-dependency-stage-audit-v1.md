# Digital support and dependency stage audit

**Status:** fifty-ninth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** digital access, informal and proxy support, disability barriers,
device and place gaps, essential-service navigation, privacy, time, and
practical independence

## Purpose

This audit asks whether “online access” is actually completed by the person
who needs the service. It separates household help, proxy action, device
availability, interface accessibility, task completion, and later independence.
The sources use different countries, populations, and methods; they are not
pooled into one digital-exclusion rate.

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Evidence with unit, date, and denominator | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social relationships are part of the access infrastructure | UK DSIT commissioned mixed-method study, published 2026: survey of 2,261 adults who had helped another adult use the internet, plus 24 expert interviews, 36 supported-person interviews, 36 supporter interviews, and a workshop. Thirty-five percent of UK adults reportedly provided informal digital help in the prior year; 53% of supporters provided proxy help. | Family, friends, neighbours, and colleagues can be an operating layer between a person and an online service. | The supporter survey is not a representative sample of all digitally excluded people, and support is not proof of exclusion or harm. |
| High-stakes tasks are inside the support layer | In the same 2,261-supporter survey, 65% helped with at least one purchase, banking, or bill-payment task; 44% helped with applications or public services; 27% helped with health appointments or prescriptions. Categories are non-exclusive. | Digital support reaches money, entitlement, and health—not only convenience or entertainment. | The data do not show unique recipients, successful completion, time spent, financial loss, or whether an official alternative existed. |
| Proxy access can create a security boundary | The UK study reports that 31% of supporters had used someone else’s login details to manage online banking or financial accounts; 57% reported repeatedly doing the same tasks even after demonstrating them. | Immediate access can coexist with credential insecurity and repeated dependence. | The measure does not show whether the login use was authorized, harmful, avoidable, or a safer choice for a high-stakes task. |
| Disability can turn nominal access into practical friction | Statistics Canada 2022 Canadian Survey on Disability: among about 8 million people aged 15+ with one or more disabilities, 17% reported Internet-use barriers at least sometimes; 13% had barriers accessing government information/services online, 11% booking appointments/services, and 9% banking or shopping online. Internet-use barriers were 28% for people with very severe disabilities versus 9% with mild disabilities. | Digital service availability and usable access are distinct, with a severity gradient. | These are self-reported cross-sectional barriers, not failed transactions, lost income, remedy outcomes, or causal effects of severity. |
| Interface conditions matter even when people are connected | Statistics Canada 2024 accessibility survey: 46% reported difficulty using a technological device in the preceding three months, 29% a cellphone-use barrier, 25% an online-communication barrier, 10% difficulty accessing support services through communication tools, and 9% a time-limit barrier. | The bottleneck can be device setup, settings, support, or time limits inside an otherwise available service. | The survey does not identify the provider, task result, alternate channel, cost, complaint, or later independent use. |
| Near-universal connectivity does not close device or place gaps | Malaysia DOSM 2025 ICT survey: household Internet access was 97.1% overall, 99.0% urban, and 90.7% rural; individual mobile use was 99.6%, Internet use 98.3%, and computer use 81.5%. | Access infrastructure can look nearly universal while rural and mobile/computer configurations remain different. | Access and use percentages do not measure speed, affordability, skills, task completion, shared-device privacy, or outcomes. |
| Automation may add a new intermediary, not remove the support need | Ofcom 2026 Technology Tracker and qualitative research: 53% of UK adult Internet users interacted with GenAI or an AI customer-service chatbot in the prior year; 8% used AI in relation to telecoms, 6% for an issue, 3% for comparison, and 2% for a complaint. Participants still valued human support for complex or sensitive problems. | Consumer AI is entering navigation and complaint routes while accountable human escalation remains important. | The source does not show better prices, resolution, completion, or welfare; qualitative participants are not representative. |

## Cross-source finding

The strongest supported pattern is **socially completed access**:

```text
service need
  -> digital barrier or interface friction
  -> another person, proxy credential, device, or AI intermediary
  -> immediate task completion or delay
  -> time, privacy, cost, and responsibility shift
  -> repeat dependence, independent use, formal remedy, or exit
```

The first three stages are visible. The last two are not consistently linked in
the same recipient or household. A person may complete a bank task through a
trusted relative, an appointment through a supporter, or a complaint through an
AI tool while still lacking independent access, safe credentials, knowledge of
the decision, or a route to challenge it.

The finding is not that informal help is bad. Teaching, doing-with, proxy help,
and advice can each be appropriate depending on urgency, disability, trust,
relationship, and task stakes. The finding is that national access statistics
can miss the people and time that make access work.

## Bounded public discussion

Public discussion supplies framing, not prevalence:

| Platform | Signal | Analytical use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | An ITU discussion frames universal, meaningful, and affordable connectivity as unfinished because infrastructure, skills, affordability, and sustainable participation must move together. | Supports the distinction between connection and meaningful use; policy commentary does not quantify the gaps or household burden. |
| LinkedIn | An Ofcom post frames AI as a telecoms customer-journey intermediary while preserving demand for human support and accountable access. | Tests whether automation is understood as convenience plus escalation; it summarizes a discussion paper, not consumer outcomes. |
| LinkedIn | An ITU resilience discussion connects affordability, regulatory alignment, cross-border dependency, and analogue fallback. | Adds infrastructure-failure and sovereignty questions; it does not document a specific failure or response. |
| LinkedIn | An UN Trade and Development post links digitally deliverable-services growth to infrastructure and unequal productive capacity. | Extends the access question to geopolitical distribution; it is an institutional aggregate summary, not worker or firm-level evidence. |

These signals reinforce a practical question: who supplies the missing layer
when a service is technically online but not independently usable? They do not
establish how often support succeeds or whether it is fairly distributed.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not treat 35% informal support as a digital-exclusion prevalence estimate.
- Do not treat proxy support as failure; it may be reciprocal, preferred, or
  safer for a sensitive or urgent task.
- Do not treat credential sharing as confirmed fraud or unauthorized access.
- Do not compare Canadian disability barriers with Malaysian Internet access as
  if they shared a denominator, year, instrument, or outcome.
- Do not treat AI navigation as a remedy, better bargain, or independent use.
- Do not treat public posts as representative public opinion.
- Preserve supporter burden and recipient autonomy as separate outcomes.
- The missing unit is one service episode with a named task, barrier, support
  mode, completion, time/cost/security consequence, provider response, and later
  independence or exit.

## Stronger next test

Build a privacy-safe, identifier-bearing episode ledger for one service type—
preferably a public benefit, health appointment, banking task, or telecoms
complaint—with:

1. person’s need, service, device, access condition, and date;
2. barrier type and whether a non-digital route was offered;
3. supporter identity category, mode (teaching, doing-with, proxy, advice),
   time spent, credential handling, and recipient consent;
4. task completion, delay, cost, privacy incident, abandonment, or remedy;
5. repeat dependence or independent completion later; and
6. provider response, trust, continued use, switching, non-use, or exit.

If the source cannot lawfully provide recipient-level follow-up, retain the
aggregate stage evidence and publish the missing fields rather than infer a
household outcome.

## Deeper question

**When does social help convert digital availability into genuine independence,
and when does it hide a transfer of time, privacy, and responsibility from an
institution to a household?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is complete only when one same-person service episode links a barrier
to support, completed or failed task, cost or security consequence, provider
response, and later independence or exit—or when the source owner confirms the
missing fields cannot be obtained. The broader 14-theme program remains active.
