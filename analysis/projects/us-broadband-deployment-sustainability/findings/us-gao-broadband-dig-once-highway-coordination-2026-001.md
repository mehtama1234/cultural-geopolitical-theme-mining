# Finding 002: “Dig once” can reduce the work of broadband construction, but coordination is not the same as service

**Status:** bounded infrastructure-governance finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

When roads are already being rebuilt, governments want broadband providers to
use the same corridor so they do not repeatedly dig up the road. The federal
“dig once” rule asks state transportation departments to name a broadband
coordinator, register providers, send electronic notices about planned highway
work, and coordinate with other plans.

GAO surveyed all 52 state contacts. Forty-six said their state had identified a
coordinator, and 46 said they coordinated broadly with other agencies. More than
half said provider-registration and notification processes were fully in place.
But some reported staff shortages, IT problems, and difficulty getting providers
to participate. States and stakeholders also said they did not yet know what
effect the rule had on actual broadband deployment.

The practical chain is:

```text
highway project
  -> coordinator and provider notice
  -> joint planning and right-of-way access
  -> fiber or other middle-mile construction
  -> operating network
  -> affordable, reliable household service [not measured here]
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a different layer from the existing broadband-funding finding. That
finding asks whether money, maps, completion data, and sustainability checks
are in place. This one asks whether agencies and firms can actually coordinate
the physical route.

The broader social point is:

> A public rule can create a cheaper shared route without proving that the
> route was built, used, or experienced as useful service.

Coordination capacity is therefore part of infrastructure access. Places with
fewer experienced staff or weaker provider participation may have the same
formal rule but less ability to turn it into a working network.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The report measures reported process implementation, not broadband coverage
  or household use.
- A missing outcome measure does not mean the rule had no benefit.
- The 46-of-52 counts are survey reports, not independent audits of every
  coordinator or project.
- The three selected states cannot represent every state or local setting.
- Reduced excavation, traffic, and cost were expected benefits; GAO did not
  estimate them in this review.

The next decisive artifact is a named highway corridor or project followed
through notice, provider participation, construction, activation, operating
cost, service price, reliability, and household or community use.
