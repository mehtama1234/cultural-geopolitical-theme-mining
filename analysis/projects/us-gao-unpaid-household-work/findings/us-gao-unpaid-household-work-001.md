# Finding 001: Unpaid household work is a multi-trillion-dollar social capacity that ordinary economic totals only partly show

**Status:** bounded cultural, care, and time-economy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO's 2026 estimate uses nationally representative American Time Use Survey
data from 2021–2024 and 2024 Current Population Survey wage data. It finds that
most people spend almost four hours a day on unpaid household work such as
cooking, cleaning, and caregiving, valued at around $70 per day. GAO estimates
the national 2024 value at least at $5.6–$6.0 trillion, equivalent to about
19.2–20.2% of GDP.

The distribution is not neutral. From 2021–2024, women were 54% of people doing
this work while making up 51% of the population. Women and married people also
spent more time on childcare than men and unmarried people.

The practical route is:

```text
unpaid domestic work and caregiving
  -> household functioning and social reproduction
  -> time and replacement value [estimated]
  -> gender, marital, work, and care differences [partly observed]
  -> [open] exhaustion, choice, pay, bargaining, wellbeing, and later security
```

The important insight is not that every hour should be converted into a wage.
It is that households are producing care and basic services at a scale large
enough to matter for the economy and for public policy, even when the activity
does not appear as a paid transaction.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This gives the time-and-care subtheme a national valuation layer. Earlier time
records show who spends time on care, work, social life, and civic activity;
GAO adds an estimate of what that work would cost at market wages. That makes
the hidden capacity visible, but it also clarifies the missing questions: who
has to supply the hours, who can shift them to paid services or other people,
who loses rest or paid work, and whether public support changes the burden.

It also prevents a narrow GDP story. A household can appear economically stable
because unpaid work absorbs care, food, cleaning, or transportation needs. The
same work can support resilience for one household and become an unequal time
constraint for another. This source does not identify which case applies to a
particular person.

## Limits and next test

- The dollar figures are replacement-cost estimates based on selected wage
  proxies, not wages actually paid to caregivers or a direct welfare measure.
- ATUS records time diaries and characteristics, but this report does not
  follow the same household from unpaid work to later income, health, job,
  relationship, or political outcomes.
- The gender and marital comparisons describe distributions; they do not
  establish why the differences exist or whether they reflect preference,
  constraint, bargaining, culture, or available alternatives.
- The national estimate does not show care quality, emotional meaning,
  exhaustion, conflict, safety, or whether the work was freely chosen.
- A higher replacement value does not by itself mean more social welfare or a
  policy failure; unpaid care can be valuable and burdensome at the same time.

The next decisive evidence would join time diaries to household resources,
employment schedules, paid-care access, health, stress, child or elder
outcomes, and later work or civic action. Until then, the safe claim is that
unpaid household work is a large and unevenly distributed social capacity that
is economically important but not fully visible in standard accounts.
