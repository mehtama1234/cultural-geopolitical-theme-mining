# Worker requests, granted changes, and durable control

**Status:** active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** worker requests, employer decisions, monitoring, notice, control,
schedule, safety, pay, health, retention, and exit

## What we found

BLS reports that 5.9 million U.S. wage and salary workers ages 16–75 requested
or made a change in their current job to help them do it better in July 2024.
Schedule changes were the largest category at 2.609 million, followed by
equipment at 1.477 million, tasks at 1.412 million, and telework at 802,000.
These categories can overlap and do not identify the same worker's condition,
employer, or later outcome.

Granting was uneven. BLS reports full granting for 70.1% of schedule requests,
69.2% of telework requests, 67.8% of equipment requests, 66.0% of task
requests, 61.0% of training requests, and 59.1% of accessibility requests.
Full grant is an administrative label, not proof that the change was timely,
usable, sufficient, or maintained.

The job structure around those requests also varies. Preliminary 2025 ORS
estimates cover 148.7 million civilian workers: 53.5% had jobs with the ability
to pause work, 18.5% had self-paced workloads, and 48.3% had employer-driven
week-to-week schedule variation. ORS is a job-level estimate, not a worker
request or accommodation outcome.

Gallup's September 2026 employee-data survey reports that only 20% had a great
deal of trust that leaders use employee data responsibly; 19% said someone at
work had discussed the data or metrics collected about their work. Comfort was
higher for team effectiveness (62%) than for performance reviews (53%). The
public summary does not disclose the sample or full method.

The 2026 ILO platform-work Convention requires information about automated
systems and human review for significant adverse decisions, including
suspension, deactivation, or termination. It creates a normative route, not
evidence of ratification, compliance, reversal, restored earnings, or durable
worker control.

## What this connects to

| Link | Evidence | What does not transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Worker need -> request | BLS counts schedule, equipment, task, telework, training, and accessibility requests. | A request is not proof of disability, unmet need, or a worker's bargaining position. |
| Request -> employer decision | Full, partial, and no-grant outcomes differ by request type. | A full grant is not adequacy, persistence, safety, pay protection, or retention. |
| Job structure -> practical control | ORS reports pause, pace, and schedule conditions. | Job-level structure is not the same worker's lived control or granted change. |
| Monitoring -> trust and voice | Gallup shows low great-deal trust and limited workplace discussion of data use. | Perceived collection is not an audit, and trust does not identify a remedy or performance effect. |
| Formal right -> contestability | ILO requires information, human involvement, and remedy access for platform-work decisions. | A standard is not implementation, worker awareness, or successful reversal. |

## The deeper question

**When does a worker-requested or formally reviewable change become durable
control over work, safety, pay, and schedule—and when does it become only a
recorded grant or paper right?**

### Why ask it

This question has high decision value for disability accommodation, workplace
monitoring, algorithmic management, schedule design, and labor enforcement. It
has high missing-link value because the current evidence stops at request,
grant, job structure, trust, or legal standard. It has high disconfirmation
value because a partial grant may be enough for some workers, and monitoring may
improve safety or coordination when purpose and voice are credible. Effort is
bounded to one protected worker or workplace episode; scope risk is controlled
by keeping BLS worker reports, ORS job estimates, Gallup perceptions, and ILO
normative rules separate.

Visible priority reasons: decision value **high**; missing-link value **high**;
connection value **high**; disconfirmation value **high**; effort **medium**;
scope risk **medium**.

## What we know so far

- Workers regularly seek changes to make work usable, but the type of request
  shapes the reported chance of a full grant.
- A full grant does not tell us whether the worker could use it without stigma,
  lost pay, extra workload, surveillance, or later retaliation.
- Pause control, self-paced workload, and schedule variability are distinct job
  structures, so flexibility cannot be reduced to one score.
- Employees report limited trust and limited discussion of workplace data use,
  especially where monitoring may affect evaluation or scheduling.
- A first-person LinkedIn account makes documentation and employer
  interpretation visible, but it is not evidence of prevalence or legal fact.

## What could change the answer

- A granted schedule or equipment change may materially improve safety, health,
  care capacity, productivity, and retention even without a formal appeal.
- A partial grant may be adequate for one worker and harmful for another.
- Monitoring may improve team safety or staffing when purpose, limits, and voice
  are clear, while the same data use may reduce autonomy when tied to ranking.
- Human review may correct automated decisions, but delay can still cost pay,
  work access, or household stability.
- Workers may leave rather than contest a decision; observed absence of a case
  is not proof that control was available.

## Challenge checks

- Activity was not treated as outcome: request, grant, control, safety, pay,
  health, trust, remedy, retention, and exit remain separate.
- Correlation was not treated as cause: BLS and Gallup are descriptive, ORS is
  job-level, and the ILO Convention is normative rather than an evaluation.
- Scope and time were checked: BLS uses July 2024 worker reports, ORS preliminary
  2025 jobs, Gallup a 2026 workplace survey, and ILO an adopted 2026 standard;
  these are not one panel.
- Repeated sources were not counted as replications; the LinkedIn account is a
  bounded lived example tied to BLS.
- Affected people were retained: workers with disabilities or care needs,
  managers, platform workers, employers, households, and coworkers affected by
  safety or scheduling decisions.
- No model memory was used as evidence; claims link to retained records.

## Current answer

**Partly answered.** The evidence shows a large request-and-grant surface, uneven
job-level control, low trust in employee-data use, and a new formal right to
human review in platform work. It does not yet show whether a specific worker
received an adequate, durable change that protected pay, health, safety,
household stability, or continued work.

## What to look for next

Follow one protected worker or workplace episode:

```text
work constraint, hazard, or automated decision
  -> notice, request, data collection, or adverse action
  -> employer/platform decision and reason
  -> full, partial, denied, or appealed change
  -> actual schedule, pay, safety, health, and household effect
  -> persistence, retaliation, retention, bargaining, or exit
```

Record the worker's requested outcome, decision time, actual use, pay and hours,
monitoring exposure, appeal route, remedy receipt, and later work. Stop when the
source ends at a grant, trust score, or legal text; report the durable-control
gap rather than calling the workplace flexible, safe, or fair.

## Stopping rule

Do not call a worker request, workplace monitoring policy, or formal platform
right effective until one compatible worker or workplace episode links need or
exposure to decision, actual use, pay/schedule/safety effect, remedy, and later
retention or exit, with a credible denied or non-reporting counterexample. If
those fields are unavailable, preserve the result as a durable-control gap and
rotate onward.
