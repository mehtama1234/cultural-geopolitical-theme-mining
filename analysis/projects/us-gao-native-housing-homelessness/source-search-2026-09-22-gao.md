# Source search: Native housing and homelessness

**Search date:** 2026-09-22  
**Geography:** American Indian, Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian communities;
Tribes and Tribal Designated Housing Entities  
**Status:** bounded pass complete; program reach and definition mismatch are
visible, while household-level housing security remains open

## Working question

Does current federal housing oversight show a gap between the formal housing
route and the way housing insecurity is experienced and described in Native
communities?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-GAO-NATIVE-HOUSING-2026 | [GAO-26-109173, Native American Issues: Preliminary Observations on Housing and Homelessness](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109173) | Identifies 30 federal programs, funding and construction constraints, Tribal and TDHE perspectives, and the mismatch between “doubling up” and some federal homelessness definitions. | Official preliminary oversight testimony | The work is preliminary and does not estimate the prevalence, duration, safety, or later outcome of doubling up across Native communities. |

## First pattern

```text
housing insecurity in a Tribal community
  -> doubled-up, sheltered, or unsheltered living
  -> federal definition and program eligibility
  -> funding, construction, maintenance, and service route
  -> [open] stable, safe, culturally appropriate housing and self-determined
     community outcomes
```

GAO found that 30 federal programs can provide housing support targeted to
American Indian, Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian populations. Nearly all
Tribes and TDHEs contacted said federal funding was insufficient to keep pace
with construction, rehabilitation, or maintenance needs. Tribes and Tribal
organizations also raised concerns that doubling up—temporarily staying with
others—may not fit federal definitions of homelessness, even when the setting
is uncomfortable or unstable.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Living with friends or relatives can be a culturally meaningful form of mutual
support and may prevent unsheltered homelessness. It can also involve crowding,
instability, or loss of privacy. The source does not justify coding every
doubled-up household as homeless or every shared arrangement as secure.

## Decision rule

Use this source for the gap between formal program legibility and lived housing
security in Native communities. Do not turn preliminary interviews into a
national prevalence estimate or infer that one federal program would solve the
housing problem.
