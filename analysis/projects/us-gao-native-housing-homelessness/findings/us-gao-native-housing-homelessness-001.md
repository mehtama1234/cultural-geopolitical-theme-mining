# Finding 001: Federal housing support can exist while Native housing insecurity remains hard to count and underfunded

**Status:** bounded Native housing, public-systems, and cultural-legibility finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO's September 2026 preliminary observations identify 30 federal programs
that can provide housing support targeted to American Indian, Alaska Native,
and Native Hawaiian communities. But nearly all Tribes and Tribal Designated
Housing Entities GAO contacted said federal funding was not sufficient to keep
pace with construction, rehabilitation, or maintenance needs.

The testimony also identifies a definition problem. Tribal communities can
experience sheltered and unsheltered homelessness, while “doubling up”—
temporarily staying with friends or family—is also widespread. Tribes and
Tribal organizations told GAO that this form of housing insecurity may not
align with federal homelessness definitions, which can make communities less
competitive for some funding.

The practical route is:

```text
housing need in a Native community
  -> doubled-up, sheltered, or unsheltered condition
  -> federal definition and application route
  -> eligibility, funding, construction, maintenance, and services
  -> [open] safe, stable, culturally appropriate housing and tribal control
```

This is not only a funding problem. It is also a legibility problem: a person
or family can be visibly housing-insecure to relatives, Tribal staff, or local
providers while remaining difficult to classify in a federal program.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

The finding sharpens the difference between a formal public option and a usable
one. The United States can have many programs on paper while funding lags
construction costs and the administrative category does not match the local
form of need.

It also preserves the cultural ambiguity instead of flattening it. Staying with
family can be protection, reciprocity, and belonging; it can also be crowded,
temporary, unsafe, or dependent on relationships that may not last. The source
does not measure which experience applies to which household.

Tribal governance matters too. Federal support may be used through Tribes and
Tribal housing entities, but the preliminary account does not establish how
program rules affect self-determination, local priority setting, or the balance
between federal requirements and community-defined housing needs.

## Limits and next test

- The report presents preliminary observations, not a national count of Native
  homelessness or doubled-up households.
- “Nearly all” is a qualitative summary of Tribes and TDHEs contacted; it is
  not a probability-weighted estimate of every Tribe or housing entity.
- The report does not measure the duration, crowding, safety, privacy, health,
  or stability of doubled-up arrangements.
- Thirty programs can provide support, but the count does not show usable
  funding, recipient coverage, application success, construction completion, or
  long-term housing security.
- The testimony does not establish that federal definitions alone caused the
  funding or access gaps.

The next decisive evidence would join Tribal or TDHE intake records to the
housing condition, duration, household composition, funding route, application
result, construction or repair status, and later stay/move outcome—using
Tribal data governance and consent. Until then, the safe claim is that Native
housing insecurity can be undercounted or poorly matched by federal routes
while funding remains below reported construction and maintenance needs.
