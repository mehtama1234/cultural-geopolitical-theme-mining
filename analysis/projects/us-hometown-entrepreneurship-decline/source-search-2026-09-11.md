# Source search: US hometown business and the loss of local ownership

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

Why are would-be business owners less likely to stay near where they were born, and what does that mean for the places they leave?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HBS-KERR-2026 | [HBS: Charting the Decline of the Hometown Entrepreneur](https://www2.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/charting-the-decline-of-the-hometown-entrepreneur) | Combining nearly half a century of US Census data, the authors report that by 2018 US-born self-employed White men were only 2 percentage points more likely than wage workers to stay in their birth state, down from an 8-point difference in 1970. The hometown advantage fell below zero for all Americans in the latest sample. | Research summary of AEA Papers and Proceedings article | The measure is about staying near birthplace, not local business survival, jobs, services or belonging. The subgroup result should not be generalized to every founder. |
| NBER-W21458 | [NBER: House Prices, Home Equity and Entrepreneurship](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21458) | US Census evidence finds that housing wealth can support longer-lived and more balanced entrepreneurship for a small group of homeowners, while much house-price-related entry is in construction and real estate and is small or short-lived. | Working paper with US Census data | It studies home equity and firm entry, not the newer decline in local birthplace ties. |
| NBER-W28606 | [NBER: Rugged Entrepreneurs](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28606) | County-level new business formation is strongly related to long-run geographic and cultural traits, including frontier history and birthplace diversity. | Working paper | Persistent county traits do not show why a particular person moved or whether local businesses served residents better. |

## First pattern to test

```text
capital, skills and local networks
  -> where a would-be owner chooses to live
  -> who owns firms and controls local services
  -> jobs, prices, place identity and local wealth
  -> housing, migration and political demands
```

The HBS result supports a decline in the old local tie. It does not show that places lost services or that mobile founders created worse outcomes for residents.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Moving can let a founder find customers, workers, capital or a better market. Local ownership is not always better, and a business can serve a place well without its owner being born there.

## Main gaps

- local business openings, closures, owners and workers together;
- effects by race, gender, age, industry and place;
- whether movers create more durable firms;
- local customer prices, service access and wages;
- the role of housing, credit, remote work and taxes;
- whether local ownership changes trust or political demands.

## Decision rule

Join migration, firm and place data before calling this a loss of local control. Do not treat birthplace as the same thing as community commitment.
