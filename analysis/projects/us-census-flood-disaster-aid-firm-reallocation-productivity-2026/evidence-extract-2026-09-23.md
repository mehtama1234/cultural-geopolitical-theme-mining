# Official-source extract: federal disaster finance and firm reallocation

**Captured:** 2026-09-23  
**Purpose:** preserve the smallest source-grounded extract used by the
machine-readable record. This is not a substitute for the linked Census
working paper.

## Official source

- Tarikua Erda, “Cleansing Floods? Creative Destruction and Government
  Spending After Disasters,” Census Bureau CES Working Paper CES-26-52, August
  2026, [official Census page](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-52.html).

## Extracted claims

- The paper uses confidential plant-level Census microdata and an event-study
  design around large federally declared floods.
- Plant exits are concentrated among the least productive plants; machines
  released by exiting plants are acquired by more productive entrants, and
  surviving plants upgrade machinery and productivity.
- Federal disaster spending expands financing access for nimble young and
  small firms. The abstract states that, without this support, financing
  constraints can impede the productivity-enhancing reallocation.
- The recorded paper estimates report a 3.6-percentage-point increase in the
  probability of plant shutdown within two years, more than 11% higher
  survivor productivity by year six, about 14% higher productivity among
  post-flood entrants, and about a 3.1% increase in bank loans after declared
  floods, especially in the smallest loan categories.
- The recorded comparison reports aggregate TFP falling about 0.73% after
  severe non-declared floods and county per-capita income falling about 0.9%,
  compared with a suggestive 0.7% increase after declared disasters.

## Boundary preserved

The study's cleanest evidence concerns manufacturing plants, credit, capital
reallocation, productivity, and county income. Plant exit and revenue-based
TFP do not directly measure worker welfare, displacement, health, housing,
customer/service continuity, resident control, or political trust. The fiscal
return claim is model-based. Selective exit, reconstruction, demand, migration,
and measurement choices remain relevant interpretations, and the results do
not establish that federal assistance always produces the same effects.
