# Source search: US news selection and inflation belief

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; news and expectation effects are measured, later action remains open

## Working question

How does the news people receive shape the economic future they expect?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-NEWS-SELECTION | [News Selection and Household Inflation Expectations](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33837) | A household-panel study finds that exposure to higher-price news raises inflation expectations by 0.4 percentage point, while exposure to lower-price news has no significant effect. The result fits a model in which households account imperfectly for the fact that high inflation is more newsworthy. | NBER working paper | It does not show each household's full media diet, price basket, spending or political response. |
| US-NBER-NEWS-CAUSAL | [The Causal Effect of News on Inflation Expectations](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34088) | Daily US television coverage raises household inflation expectations, with stronger responses to positive CPI surprises and effects that fade after about ten days. | NBER working paper | A short expectation response is not a lasting change in spending, trust or vote. |
| US-NBER-INFLATION-BELIEFS | [People's Understanding of Inflation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32497) | Provides a separate test of how party and news source shape perceived causes and policy preferences. | NBER working paper | It does not join the same household's news, price experience and later action. |

## First pattern to test

```text
price movement
  -> selected news and repetition
  -> inflation expectation and cause belief
  -> spending, saving, borrowing or political judgment
```

The sources support an effect on expectations. They do not establish the final household or political step.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Bad price news can be useful because it reports real harm. Lower-price news may be less common or less relevant to a household that still faces a high level of prices.

## Main gaps

- household price basket and income;
- complete media exposure and source trust;
- price news, cause belief and policy preference;
- spending, saving and borrowing records;
- turnout, voting and public demand;
- differences by party, income, age, race and place.

## Decision rule

Keep news exposure, expectation, cause belief, household action and political action separate. A headline can change what people expect without changing what they do.
