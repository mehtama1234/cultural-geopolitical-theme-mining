# Source search: US bank customers and the cost of staying put

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** first matched evidence pass completed; customer return and bank effects remain open

## Working question

Why do many depositors stay put when a better rate is available, and what does that do to both households and banks?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NBER-W34267 | [NBER: Dynamic Competition for Sleepy Deposits](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34267) | Using account openings and closures at more than 900 banks, the authors report that only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. They estimate that depositor “sleepiness” accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value, softens competition and reduced default probabilities during the 2023 banking turmoil in their model. | Working paper with bank-account data and model | The estimate of customer and bank effects depends on the model; it does not give the lost interest for each depositor or prove that switching is best for every account. |
| HBS-SLEEPY-2026 | [HBS: Why Banks Need “Sleepy” Customers](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/why-banks-need-sleepy-customers) | HBS reports that 94% of depositors leave funds in the same bank each year, that older and checking or savings customers switch less, and that online account holders may be less likely to move because changing automatic payments is costly in time. | Research summary | The article's figures and the NBER paper use related but not identical data and measures; the 94% figure should be reconciled with the NBER 5–15% opening rate. |

## First pattern to test

```text
bank account opens
  -> bills, direct deposit and habit settle around it
  -> switching takes time and attention
  -> customer accepts a lower rate or weak terms
  -> bank gains cheap, steady funding
  -> customer buffer and bank stability move together, but not equally
```

The evidence supports low switching and a bank value tied to that behavior. It does not show the net household cost after fees, convenience, safety, taxes and alternative rates.

## Matched evidence result

The first pass supports a two-sided tradeoff. Staying can cost interest and weaken competition, but it can also save setup effort and provide a stable banking relationship. The NBER model adds a system counterpoint: broad switching could shift deposits and raise risk for some banks. The next test is the customer's net return beside the bank's funding and service value.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Staying put can be rational. A familiar bank may offer useful bill tools, local access, trusted service or protection from a risky move. More switching can also make bank funding less stable during a crisis.

## Main gaps

- actual rate gap and interest lost by household;
- account fees, minimum balances and switching time;
- differences by age, income, race, place and digital access;
- whether customers know the available rate alternatives;
- the role of direct deposit and automatic bills;
- what happens to small banks if switching rises sharply.

## Decision rule

Pair deposit rates with account-level balances, fees and switching actions. Do not call every inactive depositor harmed, and do not treat bank stability as proof that the household received a fair return.
