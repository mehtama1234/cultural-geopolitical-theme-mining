# Finding 002: Water investment can improve pollution and health while household exposure stays uneven

**Status:** bounded drinking-water place-to-health finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER working paper uses 266 million drinking-water readings from 48 states,
covering 1,250 pollutants, and links pollution measures to Medicare outcomes
for older Americans. It reports that the share of readings above current health
standards fell by about half from 2003 to 2019, while low-income areas had
higher pollution. It also estimates that Safe Drinking Water Act loans reduced
pollution and mortality among older Americans.

The paper estimates that removing pollution above health standards would cost
about $46 per person per year at the average loan cost-effectiveness. That is a
cost-effectiveness estimate, not a bill paid by each household.

```text
public water loan
  -> water-system improvement
  -> lower measured pollution
  -> lower older-adult mortality [study estimate]
  -> household exposure, filters/bottled water, home value, trust, and local
     political response [not measured here]
```

## Why it matters

This adds a stronger consequence to the water lane. Public infrastructure can
change an environmental measure and a health outcome. But a national or
place-level improvement still does not tell us which household got cleaner
water, who paid for private protection, or whether residents felt more secure
and trusted the institution afterward.

The broader cultural and political question is therefore not only whether the
public system works on average. It is whether people experience the improvement
as fair, visible, dependable, and worth supporting.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is an NBER working paper, not a peer-reviewed final publication.
- The study uses readings, places, water systems, and linked Medicare outcomes;
  it is not a same-household journey from exposure to later health.
- The abstract does not show each household's tap exposure, water use,
  treatment, bottled-water or filter spending, or replacement receipt.
- A decline in readings above standards can coexist with local hotspots,
  unregulated pollutants, unequal monitoring, or unequal ability to avoid risk.
- The $46 figure is an estimated average cost-effectiveness, not proof that
  every community needs the same spending or that every household benefits
  equally.
- Lower older-adult mortality does not establish effects on younger people,
  housing value, trust, complaints, or political action.
