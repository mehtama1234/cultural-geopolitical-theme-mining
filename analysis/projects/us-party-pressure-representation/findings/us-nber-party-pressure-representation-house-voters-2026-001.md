# Finding 001: Representation passes through party pressure

**Status:** bounded political-institutional representation finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

What voters want and what their representative votes for are connected, but the
political party can come between them.

NBER Working Paper 34697 estimates voter ideology from survey answers and member
ideology from U.S. House roll-call votes in a shared framework. The paper reports
that party pressure creates a wedge between members' actual votes and the
preferences of their constituents. It also models party pressure as strategic:
parties balance winning legislation against protecting members' electoral
interests.

The practical chain is:

```text
constituent preferences
  -> member election incentives and party pressure
  -> roll-call vote and legislative coalition
  -> policy, constituent service, trust, and later participation [partly open]
```

The broader institutional insight is:

> A representative can be electorally connected to constituents while still
> voting through a party system that changes the final policy signal.

## What this adds to the atlas

This strengthens the distinction between voice and conversion. It links voter
beliefs, party organization, legislative behavior, and representation without
assuming that a vote alignment proves responsiveness or that divergence proves
betrayal.

## Limits and next test

- This is a working paper and its result depends on the model's assumptions
  about ideology, pressure, and issue positions.
- Survey ideology may not capture every constituent demand or knowledge level.
- Roll-call votes do not measure agenda setting, bargaining, constituent
  service, or all policy effects.
- Party pressure may coordinate a coalition and create coherent policy, not only
  distort representation.

The next decisive artifact is a protected member-district-year ledger joining
voter issue preferences, party pressure, member votes, amendments, agenda
control, constituent service, election risk, local conditions, trust, and later
participation.
