# BRIC pause and restart: from resilience funding to project delivery

**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Status:** bounded project-realization pass  
**Scope:** FEMA Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities (BRIC),
grant pause and restart, phased projects, communication, delivery, and future
community protection

## What we found

GAO's September 2026 review gives a more specific view of the grant chain than
aggregate termination counts alone. Across BRIC's first four grant cycles,
FEMA had awarded 1,245 community subgrants and allocated about $2.5 billion of
$4.8 billion available. By the data cutoff, FEMA had reimbursed $62 million,
and 37 subgrants had completed work and started closeout.

From April 2025 through March 2026, FEMA awarded no new subgrants and
obligated no funds after announcing that BRIC would end in April 2025. FEMA
announced a restart in March 2026. GAO reports that the communication gap
lasted about 11 months and that communities delayed or withdrew mitigation
work. For one community, a request to begin a second project phase submitted
in December 2024 had still not been approved as of July 2026, leaving the
community unable to complete that work.

This closes a real administrative-to-project middle: award, pause, delayed
phase, restart, and communication. It still does not prove that any specific
community became safer, faced a disaster during the delay, or received a
completed protection.

## The deeper question

When a resilience grant is paused and later restarted, does the community
recover the lost project capacity—or only regain an administrative promise after
delay has already changed cost, timing, and hazard exposure?

## Why this matters

The answer changes how resilience should be audited. “Awarded,” “allocated,”
“reimbursed,” “completed,” and “protected” are different endpoints. A program
can spend carefully and still fail to deliver on time; a pause can stop waste
and still create exposure if a needed phase cannot proceed.

Priority is **high** for decision value, missing-link value, and
disconfirmation value; connection value is high across public systems,
infrastructure, household security, and political legitimacy. Effort is medium
and scope risk is medium because a named project must be followed without
turning GAO's selected interviews into a representative sample.

## What we know so far

- BRIC had 1,245 awarded subgrants across four cycles, with about $2.5 billion
  allocated from $4.8 billion available as of the review's March 2025 data.
- Only $62 million had been reimbursed and 37 subgrants had completed work and
  begun closeout in that data frame.
- No subgrants were awarded and no funds obligated from April 2025 through
  March 2026 during the program interruption.
- FEMA restarted BRIC in March 2026, but the prior communication gap affected
  recipient planning and progress.
- At least one community could not proceed with a second phase while its
  request remained unapproved through July 2026.

## What could change the answer

The continuity claim would strengthen if a named project shows the phase
approval date, obligation and reimbursement, construction start and finish,
hazard exposure, completed mitigation, and a measurable change in expected or
realized protection.

It would weaken if delayed projects were replaced by faster funds, completed
through another route, or shown not to be needed. It would also weaken a harm
claim if the delay produced no material cost, schedule, or exposure change.

## Challenge checks

- Allocation was not treated as reimbursement, construction, completion, or
  protection.
- A communication gap was not treated as proof of physical harm.
- The one phased-project example was not generalized to every BRIC recipient.
- The March 2026 restart was not treated as recovery of the lost interval.
- GAO's selected six states and six communities were retained as a
  nongeneralizable interview sample.
- Affected parties remain distinct: applicants, state and local recipients,
  tribes, residents, contractors, FEMA regions, future disaster victims, and
  taxpayers.

## Current answer

**Partly answered.** BRIC shows that program continuity and communication can
interrupt the path from awarded resilience money to project delivery. The
public evidence does not yet show whether a named community lost measurable
protection, found replacement funding, completed the project after restart, or
experienced a later trust or participation change.

## What to look for next

```text
named BRIC award and intended hazard reduction
  -> pause, phase request, notice, and restart status
  -> obligation, reimbursement, replacement funding, and construction
  -> completed mitigation and hazard exposure
  -> household/community protection and cost or schedule effect
  -> resident attribution, trust, future applications, or exit
```

Start with GAO's named project examples, FEMA award and phase records, state
subrecipient documents, procurement and construction records, and local hazard
plans. Keep the program-level counts as context rather than a project outcome.

## Stopping rule

Stop this pass when one named BRIC project has a dated phase decision,
reimbursement or substitute funding, completed work, and a measured protection
or exposure outcome. If the public record stops at award, pause, restart, or
communication, publish the delivery gap and do not call the program protective,
failed, or legitimate on that basis alone.
