# Finding 001: Foreign defense technology sharing depends on a permission system whose speed changes with the request

**Status:** bounded geopolitical, technology-control, and alliance-interface finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The United States provides defense articles and services to more than 100
foreign governments and international organizations. When a request involves
sensitive technology or information, the Department of Defense and other
agencies use technology-release and foreign-disclosure processes to decide what
can be shared.

DOD officials estimated that about 7,000 foreign military sales cases undergo
this review each year. More than 70% were estimated to continue an existing
sale or precedent and usually receive approval under existing policy. For the
discovery stage, officials estimated that the time needed to determine whether
an applicable policy or precedent exists ranges from a few days to a year, with
delays affecting less than 10% of requests at that stage.

The system is not one office making one decision. Military departments and
interagency stakeholders hold different authorities. GAO reported that this
specialization can make coordination difficult and that limited visibility into
the cause of a delay can frustrate foreign partners waiting for an answer.

In an urgent Ukraine example, reviews that normally took a year were completed
within weeks, and some within hours. Officials said that speed required heavy
overtime and pushed other work down the queue.

The route is:

```text
foreign partner request
  -> policy, security, technology, and interagency review
  -> approval, redesign, delay, or denial
  -> [open] transfer and operational use
  -> [open] partner trust, dependence, cooperation, or strategic effect
```

As of May 2026, DOD officials reported completing 26 of 33 reform action items
and expected to complete the current set by November 2026.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a permission-and-trust version of a recurring geopolitical pattern:

> A capability can be wanted by a partner and supported by U.S. policy while
> the administrative route for granting access remains specialized, slow,
> selectively accelerated, and difficult for the partner to see from outside.

The finding is not that the process is simply broken. Deliberation protects
security and technological advantage, and most cases reportedly move through
existing channels. The narrower point is that alliance capacity depends partly
on a hidden permission layer: who can decide, how long it takes, what gets
prioritized, and whether the partner understands the delay.

This also connects geopolitical relations to domestic institutional capacity.
Urgency can produce a real capability quickly, but by using overtime and
deprioritizing ordinary requests. Speed, security, fairness across partners,
and staff sustainability can therefore pull in different directions.

## Limits and next test

- The public report does not provide a complete case-level request file or a
  partner-by-partner approval rate.
- The reported 7,000 cases, 70% continuation estimate, timing range, and less-
  than-10% delay estimate come from DOD officials and were not independently
  validated as a public administrative dataset.
- Reform completion does not prove faster review, transfer, delivery, or use.
- The report does not identify a named partner's final request outcome or later
  operational effect.
- Urgent prioritization may be appropriate in a crisis; it does not by itself
  show unfairness or failure.

The next decisive artifact is a lawful named-request ledger joining request
date, technology category, responsible authorities, review stages, decision,
redesign or delay, delivery, partner use, sustainment, and later partner or
external response. Until then, the safe claim is that technology sharing has a
real permission stage whose timing and visibility shape the room for alliance
capability, but whose downstream effect remains open.
