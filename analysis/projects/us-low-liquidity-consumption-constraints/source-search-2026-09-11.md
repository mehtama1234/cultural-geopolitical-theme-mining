# Source search: US low-liquidity consumption constraints

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; spending differences and likely sources are measured, the link to later household outcomes remains open

## Working question

When people buy less or more than their income seems to allow, is that a preference, a cash limit, or a cost of changing course?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-CONSUMPTION-WEDGES | [Consumption Wedges: Measuring and Diagnosing Distortions](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34891) | Using transaction records linked to survey expectations for middle-income US consumers with little liquid wealth, the authors find large and varied gaps between observed spending and a no-friction benchmark. The median gap is 40% of that benchmark in absolute value; 49% of consumers spend below it and 51% above it. Borrowing limits alone cannot explain both directions. The patterns fit a mix of cash limits, trouble focusing on future needs, and costs of changing consumption. | NBER working paper | The benchmark is a model measure, not a bill or welfare score. The study does not show which purchase was missed, whether spending helped or harmed the household, or what happened later. |

## First pattern to test

```text
income and liquid cash
  -> ability to wait, borrow or change a plan
  -> observed spending
  -> food, care, housing or debt choice
  -> later household security
```

The source supports large differences in spending and points to several possible causes. It does not establish which cause applies to each person.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Spending above or below a model benchmark is not automatically a mistake. People may have real needs, useful plans, or a reason to value today more than later. The benchmark cannot decide that by itself.

## Main gaps

- the item and need behind each spending change;
- liquid cash and credit limits at the time;
- price, health, family and work shocks;
- later debt, missed care or food security;
- whether a product or policy lowers the constraint;
- differences by income, age, race and place.

## Decision rule

Keep observed spending, cash constraint, preference, debt and welfare as separate claims until the same consumer can be followed through the choice.
