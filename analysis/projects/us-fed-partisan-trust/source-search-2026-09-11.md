# Source search: US trust in the Federal Reserve

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** first detailed finding promoted; trust and belief effects are measured, later action remains open

## Working question

When does an economic message become a test of who people think is on their side?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-FED-PERCEIVED-BIAS | [Perceived Political Bias of the Federal Reserve](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33071) | A survey experiment with 5,205 US consumers finds that people who see the Fed as aligned with their party report more trust, are more likely to receive or pay for Fed communications, and give those messages more weight when updating inflation expectations. | NBER working paper | It does not show actual spending, borrowing, voting or lasting communication effects. |
| US-NBER-FED-PARTISAN-TRUST | [Partisan Trust in the Federal Reserve](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33684) | Trust in the Fed is higher among respondents of the same party as the president in much of the historical data, but the partisan trust and inflation-belief patterns do not always move together. | NBER working paper | Trust, inflation beliefs and political identity are separate measures; the path into household action remains open. |

## First pattern to test

```text
price experience and political identity
  -> perceived institutional alignment
  -> trust and message receipt
  -> inflation belief
  -> spending, saving, borrowing or political judgment
```

The sources support the trust and belief links. They do not establish the final action.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

People may distrust a message because their own bills conflict with it, not because of party identity. A message can also be useful even when the speaker is not trusted.

## Main gaps

- actual household prices and income;
- message source, media and repetition;
- trust before and after a policy change;
- spending, saving and borrowing records;
- voting, turnout and policy demand;
- differences by party, age, race, income, place and education.

## Decision rule

Keep perceived alignment, trust, belief, behavior and political judgment separate. A household saying it trusts an institution is not proof that it changed its budget.
