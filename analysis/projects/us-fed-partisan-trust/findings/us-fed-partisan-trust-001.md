# The same Federal Reserve message can arrive as information or partisan threat

**Status:** provisional cross-source finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-13

## The bounded finding

Perceived institutional alignment is a measurable gate between a public
economic message and what people do with it. In NBER Working Paper 33071, a
politically representative online sample of 5,205 US adults was more likely to
describe the Federal Reserve as an out-group than an in-group, and respondents
who saw the Fed as an in-group reported higher trust and different inflation
expectations. The experiment further finds that in-group respondents demanded
more Fed information and weighted it more heavily when updating expectations.

This supports an information-and-legitimacy mechanism: institutional messages
are filtered through identity before they become economic beliefs. It does not
establish that the Fed is actually partisan, that trust caused the expectation
gap, or that the measured updating became spending, voting, or collective
action.

## What the source measures

| Measurement | Reported result | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| Survey sample | 5,205 Prolific participants, Apr. 21–May 2, 2024 | Online survey designed to be politically representative; not a probability sample |
| Perceived Fed status | 63% out-group; 37% in-group | A respondent classification, not an audit of Fed conduct |
| Partisan alignment perception | 66% of Democrat-leaning respondents saw the Fed as favoring Republicans; 60% of Republican-leaning respondents saw it as favoring Democrats | Perceived bias may reflect prior identity, media, or economic beliefs |
| Trust in Fed management | Mean 4.2 among in-group respondents vs. 3.1 among out-group respondents, on a 1–7 scale | Cross-sectional subgroup difference; perceived alignment was not randomly assigned |
| Inflation expectations | About a 2.0 percentage-point in-group/out-group gap; about 2.1 points between highest- and lowest-trust groups | The paper does not identify trust as the sole cause |
| Information processing | In-group respondents were more willing to receive/pay for Fed communication and gave it more weight in updating | Experimental demand/updating setting, not long-run media use or policy compliance |
| Hypothetical Trump-election scenario | 84% viewed the Fed as Republican-aligned; aggregate trust was relatively stable while subgroup trust moved in opposite directions | Counterfactual scenario, not an observed election or forecast |

## The mechanism under test

```text
Economic announcement or institutional communication
  -> perceived messenger identity and alignment
  -> attention, information demand, and source weighting
  -> inflation/financial expectations
  -> spending, saving, borrowing, portfolio, or work decisions
  -> judgment about competence, fairness, and responsibility
  -> trust, political demand, vote, or collective action
```

W33071 directly measures the middle of this chain: perceived alignment, trust,
information demand, and expectation updating. The later behavioral and
political arrows remain research questions.

## Cross-source connection

The BIS household monetary-policy study measures a separate US household
experiment in which hypothetical rate increases produced small reductions in
reported spending and larger durable-spending responses, with inflation
expectations an important channel. Read alongside W33071, it yields a precise
next hypothesis: the same rate communication may produce different expectation
and spending responses depending on whether households treat the Fed as a
credible in-group messenger. The samples, dates, treatments, and outcome units
are different, so this is a hypothesis bridge—not a pooled estimate.

The Federal Reserve SHED price-adaptation layer supplies a separate population
distribution of substitution, delayed purchases, reduced saving, borrowing,
and extra work. It can describe the available adaptation menu, but cannot tell
whether a household attributes that adaptation to the Fed or changes political
behavior because of it.

## What remains open

- Does perceived Fed alignment predict actual receipt, attention, and recall of
  a dated policy communication outside an online experiment?
- Do trust and message interpretation vary by debt type, savings, housing
  tenure, income volatility, race, age, local labor market, and media source?
- Does expectation updating predict actual purchases, refinancing, saving,
  portfolio changes, work hours, or missed bills?
- When economic pressure is attributed to the Fed, does it alter institutional
  trust, partisan evaluation, voting, protest, or demands for policy change?
- Can a panel or field experiment separate messenger credibility from the
  realized contract, price, and income exposures that follow?

**Evidence status:** a detailed survey-experimental source finding with a
bounded bridge to separate household monetary-policy and price-adaptation
layers. No same-household trust-to-behavior-to-politics chain is claimed.
