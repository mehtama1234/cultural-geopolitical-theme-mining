# Institutional trust, information processing, material expectations, and action bridge

**Status:** thirty-first active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** Federal Reserve trust, partisan interpretation, economic information, media intermediaries, household expectations, and civic action

## Question

When institutions communicate about prices, rates, or public conditions, does
trust change whether people receive and use the information—and when does that
interpretation become material behavior, accountability, or political action?

```text
institutional event or message
  -> source exposure and perceived alignment
  -> trust, attention, and interpretation
  -> expectation or attribution
  -> spending, saving, borrowing, compliance, or civic action
  -> institutional response and durable legitimacy
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage or link | Comparable observation | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trust is politically sorted before a message arrives | In a 2024 NBER survey experiment of 5,205 U.S. consumers, 63% viewed the Federal Reserve as an out-group and 37% as an in-group; 66% of Democratic-leaning respondents saw the Fed as favoring Republicans, while 60% of Republican-leaning respondents saw it as favoring Democrats. | Online survey experiment with political-affiliation, perceived-alignment, information-demand, and expectation stages. | Prolific is not a probability sample; perceived alignment may reflect prior identity, media, and economic beliefs rather than actual institutional conduct. |
| Alignment and trust move together | Mean trust in Fed management was 4.2 on a 1–7 scale among respondents viewing the Fed as an in-group versus 3.1 among out-group respondents; the inflation-expectation gap between those groups was 2.0 percentage points. | Pre-treatment subgroup comparison and regression controls. | Cross-sectional trust differences do not show that trust caused expectations or that the Fed caused the perceived alignment. |
| Trust affects information demand and weighting in an experiment | In the same study, in-group respondents were more willing to pay for and receive Federal Reserve communications and gave them more weight when updating inflation expectations. | Randomized information-demand budget and information-processing treatment. | Experimental demand and updating are not long-run news use, actual policy compliance, spending, voting, or trust change. |
| Message design changes expectation estimates | Around the September 2025 rate-cut announcement, a 25-basis-point hypothetical vignette reduced one-year inflation expectations by 2.043 percentage points, versus 0.833 points in the full randomized information treatment and 0.815 points in the repeated-cross-section event study. | NBER comparison of hypothetical, randomized-information, and naturally occurring announcement designs. | These estimate different reduced-form objects; the event study includes media, press-conference, and concurrent-news effects and none measures realized behavior. |
| Prior knowledge is a major boundary | In the same randomized treatment, the inflation-expectation estimate was −1.937 points among 557 previously unaware respondents but 0.026 among 751 previously aware respondents. | Randomized information treatment split by prior knowledge. | Awareness is selected and can proxy for financial literacy, media exposure, interest, or prior beliefs; it is not a universal treatment effect. |
| Institutional trust is fragmented rather than uniformly absent | The Weidenbaum Center's Waves 6–8 report describes low but stable federal-government trust sorted by partisanship, geography, and personal economic security. In Wave 8, high trust in the presidency was 75% among Republicans versus 9% among Democrats; news-media trust was 55% versus 23%, and military trust 87% versus 54%. | Repeated YouGov survey waves; institution-specific subgroup shares. | Associations do not establish individual change, institutional performance, democratic stability, or action. |
| Place differences are institution-specific | Wave 8 high trust in the military was 73% in rural areas versus 64% in urban areas, while news-media trust was 42% urban versus 34% rural; comparable gaps were not found for Congress and local government. | Urban-rural cross-sectional comparisons. | Place may stand for party, media environment, race, economic structure, or institutional contact; it is not a causal place effect. |
| Attention and credibility can diverge | Reuters Institute discussion describes social and video platforms as widely used news sources while trust remains higher for news than for social media and AI chatbots. ## Cross-source finding

The central pattern is an **interpretation bottleneck**. An institutional message
does not reach households as a neutral signal. Perceived alignment determines
whether people seek the source, how much weight they give it, and which meaning
they attach to the same announcement. Awareness and source credibility can
matter more than message presence.

The chain must keep four outcomes separate:

1. **Receipt:** did the person encounter or understand the message?
2. **Interpretation:** did it signal competence, bias, threat, or useful
   information?
3. **Expectation:** did inflation, employment, or other beliefs change?
4. **Action:** did spending, saving, borrowing, compliance, protest, voting, or
   institutional accountability change?

The strongest counter-signal is that a large expectation response in a
hypothetical vignette shrinks when information is randomized in a real policy
context, especially among people who already knew the decision. Conversely,
institution-specific trust sorting can coexist with continued trust in some
institutions, showing differentiation rather than simple universal distrust.

## Bounded public discussion

An Ofcom relay describes fragmented news discovery through social platforms,
creators, influencers, and AI while noting continued trust in established news.
A Reuters relay highlights the gap between platform attention and credibility.
A Reddit discussion of low institutional confidence contains competing blame
stories—leaders, money, party strategy, institutional failure—and also comments
defending trust in particular institutions. A dignity-focused LinkedIn post
frames respectful disagreement and fair participation as proposed routes to
trust.

These discussions expose attribution and messenger questions. They are selected
relays or self-selected reactions, not representative public opinion or proof
that trust-building proposals changed behavior.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- Low trust in one institution can coexist with high trust in another; trust is
  not one general personality trait or a single national score.
- A person may reject a messenger but still update from prices, employers,
  family, or another trusted source.
- A rate announcement can change expectations because it signals private
  information about the economy, not because it changes borrowing incentives.
- A large hypothetical response may reflect imagination or demand effects rather
  than a real behavioral response.
- High platform attention can coexist with low credibility, and established
  provider trust can coexist with low reach among some groups.
- Partisan interpretation may represent accountability or differentiated
  performance judgments rather than irrational polarization.

## Stronger next test

Build a respondent-level communication episode that records:

1. prior institutional trust, party identity, place, economic security, and
   financial knowledge;
2. message source, platform, exposure, comprehension, perceived alignment, and
   competing information;
3. expectation revision and attribution immediately after the event;
4. linked observed spending, saving, borrowing, benefit use, compliance, or
   portfolio behavior; and
5. later complaint, civic action, vote, protest, switching of information
   source, trust, and institutional response.

Compare a randomized message with a real announcement, and preserve people who
receive the message but do not believe it, people who act without trusting the
institution, and people whose trust improves after a correction or result.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The corpus shows that perceived alignment and prior
knowledge shape information demand and expectation updating, while institutional
trust is sorted by party, place, and economic security. It does not yet follow
the same person from message exposure through verified household behavior or
civic action and back to institutional response.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call a message trusted, behavior-changing, or legitimacy-building from
belief updates, platform reach, or survey trust alone. Require a matched or
longitudinal episode with exposure, interpretation, a real material or civic
action, and a later institutional response, including a counterexample.
