# Finding 001: Eligibility can expand while the application route still loses people

**Status:** bounded higher-education access finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO reports that 9.9 million students were eligible for a Pell Grant in school
year 2024–25 after the simplified FAFSA and changed eligibility formula. That
was about 570,000 more eligible students, or 6% more than the prior year, and
about 1.9 million more students were eligible for the maximum $7,395 award, a
31% increase. The increase occurred even though fewer students completed the
FAFSA, after delays and rollout barriers affected the new application.

The change was uneven. Much of the overall eligibility increase was among
students in households earning $60,001–$125,000. Among students with another
family member in college, eligibility rose from 55% to 60%, while maximum-award
eligibility rose from 48% to 77%. More than 90% of students in vulnerable
populations such as homeless or foster-care students were eligible, most also
for the maximum award.

GAO also identifies a counterexample to a simple expansion story: a student in
a family of four with another person in college and household income of
$95,000 could have been eligible under the old rules but not the new ones.

The practical route is:

```text
FAFSA rule and application
  -> application completion
  -> eligibility calculation
  -> award and payment
  -> enrollment and persistence [not observed here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a higher-education version of a recurring subtheme:

> A simpler or broader formal route can improve eligibility without proving
> that the intended benefit became usable.

The source also shows why aggregate expansion can hide redistribution: more
students qualified overall, but some household configurations lost eligibility,
and fewer students completed the application during the rollout.

## Limits and next test

- Eligibility is not an award, payment, enrollment, persistence, graduation,
  or reduced student debt.
- The comparison is between two school years and does not isolate every effect
  of the formula from rollout delays, applicant composition, or other changes.
- GAO analyzes aggregated Education data; it does not follow the same student
  from application through payment and college outcome.
- The vulnerable-population result describes eligibility, not actual access to
  college or aid use.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected student route joining FAFSA
submission, verification or correction, award, disbursement, enrollment,
work/care constraints, persistence, and exit. Until then, the safe claim is
about formal eligibility and application friction, not college access success.
