# Finding 001: A place can offer income mobility without equal wealth mobility

**Status:** bounded Census CES wealth-and-place mobility finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

Census CES Working Paper 26-30 builds county-level measures from roughly 3.4
million parent-child pairs. It compares five outcomes using the same linked
families: total income, labor income, homeownership, housing wealth, and total
wealth.

Income and wealth mobility are related but not interchangeable. Wealth measures
vary more across counties, and their correlations with income mobility are below
0.5. Many cities and suburbs look less mobile by housing wealth than by income.
The Great Recession was associated with lower later income mobility across the
parental distribution, while its negative wealth association was concentrated
among children from poorer families.

```text
family resources and childhood place
  -> housing, schools, work, social conditions, and recession exposure
  -> adult income and labor income
  -> ownership, housing wealth, and total wealth
  -> [open] debt, transfers, wellbeing, belonging, and political meaning
```

## What this adds to the atlas

The usual “land of opportunity” map can be incomplete. A person may climb the
income ladder without gaining the home or assets that provide room, security,
and influence across generations. Conversely, a place can show high average
upward mobility while preserving a strong advantage for families who already
own valuable property.

This makes housing wealth a cultural and political resource, not only a balance
sheet. Ownership can affect who can stay, borrow, help children, absorb shocks,
and feel that a place belongs to them. The paper measures the mobility pattern,
not those later meanings or mechanisms.

## Limits and next test

- The paper is a working paper, not a Census Bureau publication endorsement.
- County estimates are associations, not randomized evidence that a place or recession caused the outcome.
- Gross wealth is estimated from property records and capitalized financial income; it is not net wealth or spendable cash.
- The cohorts were born 1978–1986 and adult outcomes are measured mainly in 2019–2021, so this is not a current estimate for all generations.
- The study does not identify the roles of schools, segregation, housing supply, labor markets, debt, migration, inheritance, or family transfers separately.
- Higher absolute mobility and lower relative mobility can coexist; neither is a complete measure of wellbeing.

The next decisive test is a protected person-place panel joining moves, housing
cost and debt, ownership timing, transfers or inheritance, schools, work,
recession exposure, later household security, and civic or political meaning.
