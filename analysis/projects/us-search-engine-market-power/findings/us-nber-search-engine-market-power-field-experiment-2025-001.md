# Finding 001: Active choice alone barely moves search use, but exposure can change beliefs and persistence

**Status:** bounded U.S. digital-platform field-experiment finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 33410 studies approximately 2,300 U.S. desktop Google users
whose default search engine was Google. Requiring an active choice increased
Bing's market share by only 1.1 percentage points. Users paid to try Bing for
two weeks updated positively about its relative quality, and 33% preferred to
continue using Bing. The paper's model estimates that removing demand frictions
and incorrect quality beliefs would increase Bing's market share by 15
percentage points and consumer surplus by $6 per consumer-year, but those are
counterfactual model results rather than observed universal effects.

```text
incumbent default
  -> active-choice prompt or temporary alternative exposure
  -> observed use and quality-belief update
  -> continued preference for some users
  -> [open] durable switching, privacy, security, welfare, and exit
```

## What this adds to the atlas

The experiment separates two mechanisms that are often collapsed into
“choice”: being asked to choose and being given enough exposure to learn about
an alternative. Active choice alone had little effect, while temporary use
changed beliefs and later preference for a subset of users. This makes
attention, learning, and defaults part of practical consumer power.

## Limits and next test

- The unit is a bounded U.S. desktop-user experiment, not the entire search
  market or all devices.
- Continued preference after a paid trial is not verified durable switching,
  privacy improvement, security improvement, or political action.
- The modeled market-share and surplus counterfactuals depend on assumptions
  about switching costs, inattention, quality beliefs, and demand.
- The paper's disclosed relationships with platform firms and data providers
  remain part of the source context and should be preserved when interpreting
  the result.

The next decisive test is a user-level panel linking default exposure, trial or
choice-screen treatment, comprehension, accessibility, retention, privacy and
data-sharing settings, support or complaint route, and later switching or
non-use.
