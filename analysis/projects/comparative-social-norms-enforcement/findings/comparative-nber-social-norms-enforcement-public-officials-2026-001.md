# Finding 001: Good norms may need continued enforcement

**Status:** bounded comparative institutional-culture and enforcement finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study examines Singapore, a low-corruption setting, using housing
transactions for public officials and relatives around planned Mass Rapid
Transit stations.

Civil servants disproportionately bought homes near stations that were planned
but not yet publicly announced. The effect was strongest one to two years before
public announcements, concentrated among mid-level officials and agencies
connected to rail planning, and also appeared among relatives.

The study reports that stronger formal enforcement substantially reduced both
direct and indirect misconduct. Survey measures of perceived corruption, public
trust, and pro-integrity norms are consistent with norms that persist but are
fragile.

The route is:

```text
integrity norm and access to information
  -> official behavior and private gain
  -> enforcement or non-enforcement
  -> [open] durable norms, trust, and public legitimacy
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This gives the cultural-political side of a recurring pattern:

> Informal trust and good expectations can help a system work, but they may
> weaken when enforcement becomes less credible.

It also shows why formal rules and culture should not be treated as substitutes
automatically. Enforcement may deter conduct directly while also telling people
that the norm is real and monitored.

## Limits and next test

- The evidence concerns Singapore, not the United States.
- The public abstract does not disclose the exact transaction count, full
  survey frame, or all enforcement dates and controls.
- The transaction result does not measure every type of corruption or every
  official's beliefs.
- Lower observed misconduct after enforcement does not prove that public trust
  or norms recovered.

The next decisive U.S. test is a protected public-integrity ledger joining
official access, information timing, transaction or contracting behavior,
enforcement, reporting, public trust, and later conduct. Until then, the safe
claim is that institutional integrity may require both social expectations and
credible formal enforcement.

- [NBER Working Paper 35756: Do Social Norms Substitute for Enforcement?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35756)
