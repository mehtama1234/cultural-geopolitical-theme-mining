# AI implementation, worker control, and security audit

**Status:** active depth artifact; cross-source stage audit, not a workforce-wide causal estimate  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** AI adoption and output -> task redesign -> worker voice, autonomy, training, bargaining power, and durable security

## What we found

AI evidence is becoming more precise about activity and output, but the worker
conversion remains open. More commits, projects, or firm adoption do not tell
us who controls the pace, who receives the time or earnings gain, whether a
worker can challenge the system, or whether a household becomes more secure.

```text
AI access or organizational adoption
  -> task activity, time, or output
  -> workflow and monitoring change
  -> worker voice, autonomy, training, or outside option
  -> pay, hours, job quality, and household security
  -> continued work, bargaining, switching, or exit
```

## What this connects to

The evidence supports a **conversion gap**, with three distinct bottlenecks.

First, activity is not output. The coding study finds large increases in
commits and projects but smaller release growth and no marketplace-use growth.
This is a counterexample to treating tool activity as realized economic value.

The Census/NBER diffusion study adds a second industrial counterexample. Firm
adoption, functional deployment, and worker task use do not move as one thing:
worker task use was reported by 23% of firms while 66% of users described AI as
augmentation only, and AI-related employment decreases were reported by 2% of
firms. The association with employment decreases was tied to functional
deployment and operational investment, not worker-task use after controls. This
does not prove that AI is safe; it shows why the unit and layer of adoption
matter before interpreting a workforce result.

Second, output is not control. Firm examples can show faster service or fewer
labor inputs while lacking measures of job quality, redeployment, workload,
voice, or who controls released time. ILO's psychosocial review identifies the
possible cost as intensified monitoring or pace, not merely job loss.

Third, training is not security. The NBER retraining evidence suggests earnings
gains often arrive through movement into less exposed occupations. That may be
valuable, but it is different from a worker gaining bargaining power or keeping
control over the original job. The outside-option experiment supplies a useful
counterexample: competition can improve leverage even when no training or
formal representation is observed.

Fourth, exposure is not displacement. The NBER CPS study of recent college
graduates finds no statistically significant relative unemployment increase in
summer 2026, including under an expanded measure that captures people who want
a job but are not counted as unemployed. This does not disprove future or
occupation-specific disruption; it prevents a current exposure signal or public
fear from being treated as a realized employment effect.

Firm performance is a separate clock. The latest NBER featured-paper summary
associates growth in AI-skilled employment share with 6.2 percentage points
greater sales-per-worker growth in 2018–2024, but not in the prior eight years.
That result is a useful industrial-stage signal and a reason to test timing,
firm selection, management capability, and workforce composition. It cannot be
read as causal productivity, wage growth, worker control, or household benefit.

## Bounded public discussion

Public discussion adds interpretation and contested expectations, not
population estimates:

| Record | Discussion signal | Use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| [ILO work discussion](../../social-discussions/records/ai-work-control-005.json) | AI is discussed as a productivity, inequality, autonomy, and job-quality issue rather than only a displacement count | Keeps output and control separate; selected professional discussion is not worker prevalence |
| [AI hiring discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-acca-ai-hiring-linkedin-001.json) | Skills-based hiring, hybrid flexibility, human judgment, and distrust of AI screening are treated as linked worker concerns | Identifies a control and legitimacy hypothesis; report commentary is not a hiring-system audit |
| [Automation and meaning discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0064-automation-meaning-linkedin-001.json) | Discussion treats automation as a question of purpose, dignity, and what work is for | Adds cultural meaning; replies do not measure job redesign or wellbeing |
| [Career uncertainty discussion](../../social-discussions/records/ai-work-control-003.json) | Workers debate mass unemployment, new work, and the credibility of alternatives | Makes perceived outside options visible; self-selected Reddit comments are not employment data |
| [AI workplace surveillance discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0055-surveillance-linkedin-001.json) | Surveillance and performance targets are discussed as possible safety tools or anxiety/injury risks | Preserves the counterexample that the same monitoring can protect or intensify work; no worker-level outcome |

The discussion layer reinforces the central finding: workers ask who decides,
who verifies, who benefits, and what alternative exists. It does not answer
those questions for any measured firm or worker.

## U.S. industrial and consumer boundary for the next pass

The U.S. evidence now gives a more specific industrial/consumer test without
claiming one pooled effect:

| U.S. stage | Evidence | What it permits | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workplace use and time | Census HTOPS reports that, among recent workplace-AI users, 31% reported saving 1–2 hours, 15% 3–4 hours, and 15% more than 4 hours; 10% reported no time saved and 4% extra time | AI can create a measurable time-saving or time-cost signal | Whether time becomes rest, care, learning, more output, monitoring, or a higher pace |
| Industrial adaptation capacity | NBER exposure/adaptive-capacity work identifies 37.1 million workers in highly exposed occupations, including 6.1 million in the high-exposure/low-capacity overlap in its January version; its later chapter revision reports a smaller covered estimate, so the versions are not pooled | Exposure and ability to adapt are separate gates, with clerical and administrative work a key risk concentration | Person-level displacement, retraining completion, cash buffer, care burden, local demand, and later earnings |
| Management and security | Gallup's four-wave U.S. workforce evidence finds frequent AI users report more than twice the displacement concern of infrequent users; perceived manager respect and wellbeing care narrow the association by 6.8 and 11.1 percentage points respectively | Organizational treatment is a plausible buffer between exposure and worker insecurity | The public evidence does not show causal management effects, actual job loss, pay, schedule, bargaining, or retention |
| Consumer-facing firm capability | The U.S. firm-capacity record reports formal training at 29.1%, 59.1%, and 78.9% for small, medium, and large establishments, while NBER task evidence finds fewer than half adopt AI within most occupations/tasks | Firm size and implementation capacity may shape whether consumer-facing AI is usable and contestable | The establishment data do not identify AI training, customer error, human fallback, complaint completion, or worker outcomes |
| Bargaining and exit | The NBER outside-option experiment finds access to a competing platform nearly doubled drivers' labor-supply elasticity to Uber and lowered the implied markdown from about 68% to 50% | Competition can create leverage even without formal representation | Net earnings, safety, hours stability, benefits, durability, and household security |
| Public accountability | NYC's October 1, 2026 joint enforcement policy says AI reliance does not remove responsibility under city law; Int. No. 161-2026 was heard and laid over on October 5 while proposing reporting on displacement, salary changes, and new training | A public institution can make accountability and measurement part of the implementation chain | No published tool inventory, employee count, investigation, correction, compensation, training completion, or worker outcome is yet established |
| Complaint and consumer oversight | NYC Int. 919 proposes a DCWP AI oversight office and complaint portal; Int. 1101 proposes investigation of AI complaints and a complainant share of recovered proceeds; both remain proposals | The consumer route is being designed around intake, referral, investigation, and possible recovery rather than AI disclosure alone | No enacted office, operating portal, complaint volume, investigation, recovery, or claimant receipt is established |
| AI consumer refund route | The FTC announced that starting October 27, 2026 it would send 2,124,796 PayPal payments totaling more than $15.8 million to eligible consumers who paid Cleo AI for cash advances; recipients were told to accept payments within 30 days | A named AI-finance consumer case has a public distribution count, amount, payment channel, eligibility communication, and acceptance deadline | The announcement precedes the payment date; it does not yet show delivered, accepted, returned, unresolved, or account-corrected payments, or whether the remedy prevented repeat exposure or restored trust |

The U.S. industrial question is therefore not simply whether firms adopt AI. It
is whether implementation changes the worker's feasible option set: usable
training, control over pace and review, credible alternatives, and a remedy
before income or service quality is lost. The consumer question is the paired
surface: whether AI-enabled customer service or allocation produces a usable
human fallback, correction, and completed remedy. A reported time saving or
faster service is not enough to answer either question.

This scope is intentionally U.S.-bounded for the next acquisition cycle. The
comparative cases remain useful as mechanism and counterexample records, but
they are not substituted for U.S. industrial or consumer outcome evidence.

## Named U.S. implementation and remedy episodes

Three U.S. cases make the open middle more concrete:

| Episode | Dated implementation/remedy evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meal-delivery customer service, randomized field experiment, December 2020–November 2021 | 138 agents and 256,934 chats; AI response suggestions reduced response time 22% and increased measured sentiment 0.45 points on a five-point scale; effects were larger for less-experienced agents, but repeat complaints and prior chatbot failures remained weak points | A firm can deploy AI assistance and measure worker-facing speed plus customer interaction outcomes; systemic complaints can remain outside the tool's capability | Customer identity across later contacts, refund/correction, retention, switching, agent pay, workload, authority, or household welfare |
| OpenAI/Statsig U.S. PERM recruitment settlement, June 2023–August 2026 | DOJ identified five OpenAI and one Statsig recruitment episodes involving a mail-only application burden for U.S. workers; the settlement requires $1.2m to Treasury, $2m reserved for potential back pay, electronic/external posting and tracking controls, semiannual reports, and three years of IER oversight | A named AI-sector recruitment channel can be linked to an official remedy design with access restoration and monitoring obligations | Number of affected or qualified workers, individual back-pay receipt, restored opportunity, implementation compliance, later hiring, trust, or exit; the agreement is not an admission of liability and does not prove AI caused the channel design |
| Cleo AI cash-advance refund distribution, October 2026 | FTC announced 2,124,796 PayPal payments totaling more than $15.8m, with eligibility emails before October 27 and a 30-day acceptance window; the underlying case alleged promised cash advances were often not delivered as advertised and cancellation was difficult | The consumer remedy has a named administrator, payment count, total value, channel, and acceptance procedure | The payment plan is not observed receipt; later acceptance, return, account correction, repeat borrowing, financial recovery, and trust remain open |

These episodes sharpen the conversion gap. In the customer case, AI improved
the interaction while the underlying service problem could persist. In the
recruitment case, a formal remedy specifies the channel and oversight needed
to restore access, but public records do not yet show the worker-level result.
In the Cleo case, even a precisely counted refund distribution still has to be
accepted by the consumer and connected to account correction or later financial
outcomes.
The evidence therefore supports a practical rule for future acquisitions:
record the system's immediate metric, the affected person's usable remedy, and
the later outcome as separate fields.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not join worker-task adoption to firm expectations by occupation alone.
- Do not treat commits, tasks, or throughput as earnings, job quality, or
  worker control.
- Do not treat a reported reduction in labor input as worker displacement or
  household harm without a defined denominator and counterfactual.
- Do not treat training returns as proof of accessible, preferred, or durable
  transition.
- Do not treat outside-option elasticity as realized household security.
- Do not treat AI exposure, replacement concern, or a single vulnerable cohort
  as realized displacement without a dated comparison and employment outcome.
- Keep worker, employer, platform, customer, and household units separate.
- Preserve the positive counterexample: AI can reduce routine work or improve
  safety when workers have review, participation, and a credible alternative.
- For named episodes, distinguish system performance, access restoration,
  payment eligibility, payment receipt, and durable worker or customer outcome.

## Stronger next test

The next high-value record is one same-worker or same-workplace episode with:

1. baseline role, task, pay, hours, and system exposure date;
2. employer decision, notice, training, monitoring, and worker voice;
3. task, pace, schedule, pay, health, and household-time changes;
4. review, correction, remedy, or an accessible human fallback;
5. outside alternatives, bargaining, continued work, or switching; and
6. later earnings, job quality, recurrence, trust, and exit.

The highest-value acquisition now includes three follow-up routes: customer
complaint -> agent/system response -> correction or refund -> repeat contact or
exit; recruitment barrier -> claimant identification -> back-pay decision ->
receipt -> later employment; or Cleo eligibility -> notification -> PayPal
delivery -> acceptance/return -> account correction -> later borrowing,
cancellation, repeat exposure, or trust. If a same-person record cannot be
lawfully obtained, preserve the stage comparison and seek a bounded workplace,
platform, or consumer-remedy episode rather than infer durable control from
aggregate adoption or announced distributions.

## Deeper question

**When does AI-enabled output become worker security and bargaining power, and
when does it become a private adaptation whose time, risk, and meaning are
assigned by the firm?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is sufficient when adoption, task activity, output, organizational
design, worker control, training, outside options, earnings, and exit have been
separated by unit, date, method, and limits; the social discussion is bounded;
and the same-workplace missing middle is explicit. The broader 14-theme program
remains active.
