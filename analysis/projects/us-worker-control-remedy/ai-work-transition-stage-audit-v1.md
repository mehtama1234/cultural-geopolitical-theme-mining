# AI work transition stage audit

**Status:** fifty-eighth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** generative-AI adoption, time, task redesign, firm expectations,
worker displacement concern, management support, retraining, public trust,
and durable work control

## Purpose

This audit tests whether the current AI-at-work evidence reaches beyond
adoption or productivity to a usable worker transition. It compares unlike
units explicitly: randomized workers, worker-task observations, executives,
workforce-panel observations, training spells, and public discussion. None is
treated as a substitute for a same-worker or same-workplace episode.

## Evidence ledger

| Stage | Evidence with unit, date, and denominator | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access can change time before task redesign | NBER W33795 randomized access experiment: 7,137 knowledge workers across 66 firms, six months. Treated users reduced email time by about 2 hours per week in the current abstract; the revised public version reports 3.6 fewer hours among frequent users and 1.3 fewer hours by assignment, with moderately faster document completion and no significant meeting-time change. | Individual access can alter a measurable time pattern. | Saved time could become rest, care, more work, or higher expectations; no pay, authority, household, or durable job result is observed. |
| Exposure is not adoption | NBER W35677: 13,920 employed RPS respondents ages 18–64 across four 2025–26 waves and 55,634 worker-task observations. Fewer than half adopt within most occupation/task cells; exposure scores explain 5%–53% of occupation and 7%–44% of task adoption variation. | Similar work does not imply the same use; employer access, training, task fit, privacy, and worker choice remain live mechanisms. | The descriptive survey does not identify which mechanism causes the residual variation or whether low adoption protects control. |
| Firm expectations move faster than measured retrospective effects | NBER W34836: nearly 6,000 senior executives in the US, UK, Germany, and Australia. Sixty-nine percent of firms report active AI use; 90% report no employment or productivity impact over the prior three years, while expected next-three-year productivity is +1.4%, output +0.8%, and employment expectations differ by respondent perspective (employers −0.7%, employees +0.5%). | Adoption, retrospective impact, and forward expectation are separate clocks and perspectives. | Expectations are not realized output, job loss, pay, worker voice, or distribution of gains; the sample is not a US-only administrative panel. |
| Worker threat is visible alongside use | Gallup workforce-panel analysis: nearly 30,000 unique worker observations across four waves from 2023 through Q1 2026, with about 30% observed more than once. Nineteen percent said their job was somewhat or very likely to be eliminated by AI; frequent users showed more than twice the acute concern of infrequent users in most waves. | Users may see task substitution earlier, or may work in more exposed settings; threat interpretation is part of the transition. | Observational panel evidence cannot say that use caused fear or that fear predicts actual job elimination. |
| Management can be a buffer, but not yet a proven intervention | In the same Gallup analysis, the frequent-use/displacement association was 6.8 percentage points smaller at the highest respect rating and 11.1 points smaller when workers felt the organization cared about wellbeing. Concern was also associated with −0.20 SD engagement, +0.18 SD burnout, −0.19 SD satisfaction, and +4.1 percentage points actively looking for another job. | Perceived respect, care, and worker-value communication are plausible organizational mechanisms connecting exposure to control and exit. | These are associations; supportive management may mark safer firms rather than cause the buffer, and job-search or wellbeing measures are not durable outcomes. |
| Training can create an earnings route, often through occupational movement | NBER W34174 compares WIOA occupational training spells with matched job-search-only service users. Estimated quarterly earnings returns for high-exposure trainees were about $1,000 in 2012–19 and about $3,000 in 2022–24; gains were mainly associated with moving into less AI-exposed work, with a smaller AI-complementary route. | Training is a possible transition bridge, not only a skill-consumption story. | The public abstract does not disclose the full sample; selection, completion, access, hours, benefits, stability, household costs, and worker preference remain open. |
| Formal preparation lags informal adaptation | SCSP–Gallup, June 2–23 2026: 3,152 US adults. Fifty-one percent used AI at least sometimes, versus 39% in the comparable 2025 measure; 57% asked AI for help using AI, 23% asked a human, 26% completed online training, and 16% received employer training. Only 20% expected most or all displaced workers to learn new skills. | Individuals are adapting and self-teaching while confidence in an institutional transition bridge remains weak. | These are self-reports and expectations, not training completion quality, competence, job placement, or benefit adequacy. |

## Cross-source finding

The strongest supported pattern is a **transition gap**:

```text
AI access or exposure
  -> measurable time or task change
  -> worker interpretation of threat, value, and control
  -> training, explanation, and review
  -> pay, schedule, job quality, and household security
  -> continued work, voice, switching, or exit
```

The first two arrows have increasingly concrete evidence. The middle arrows
have worker-panel associations and a promising training-return comparison. The
last arrows remain unlinked in the same person or workplace. A firm can report
productivity expectations while a worker experiences threat; a worker can save
time while receiving no control over what happens to it; training can raise
quarterly earnings while requiring a move into a different occupation.

This is not evidence that AI necessarily harms workers. It is evidence that
“adoption,” “productivity,” “trust,” “training,” and “security” are different
currencies and should not be collapsed into one AI-work outcome.

## Bounded public discussion

Existing public records add frames and questions, not prevalence:

| Platform | Observed discussion signal | Analytical use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | An ILO relay emphasizes uneven productivity, limited large-scale displacement so far, and risks to inequality, younger workers, autonomy, and job quality. | Tests whether public interpretation preserves the distinction between speed, output, employment, and control; selected professional audience and secondary relay. |
| LinkedIn | A GAO surveillance relay describes safety and health uses alongside anxiety and injury risk when targets accelerate work. | Adds oversight and pace as mechanisms; it summarizes a report rather than sampling workers. |
| LinkedIn | A hiring discussion links AI talent to skills-based hiring, hybrid flexibility, autonomy, human judgment, and distrust of AI screening. | Suggests professional trust and alternative work conditions may shape adoption; author-reported claims are not independently validated. |
| Reddit | A career thread disputes whether AI means mass unemployment, new work, or durable human-facing roles, using self-reported exposure examples. | Makes perceived alternatives and uncertainty visible; it is self-selected and not employment data. |
| X | A trending summary frames AI-linked layoffs through efficiency and profit versus lost income, consumer demand, and doubts about attribution. | Connects firm decisions to household demand and legitimacy; underlying posts are not individually retained. |
| YouTube | Career videos frame the issue as disappearing jobs, career survival, and skills for students and workers. | Shows cultural meaning of exposure as career threat; search descriptions are not labor evidence or transcript audits. |

The discussion layer reinforces the next measurement need: people are asking
whether there is a credible alternative, who controls the transition, and who
captures the time or productivity gain. It does not answer those questions.

## Missing links and challenge checks

- Do not join NBER worker-task adoption to Gallup fear by occupation alone.
- Do not treat the 1.3–3.6 weekly hours saved as leisure, care, or worker power.
- Do not treat executive expectations as realized firm outcomes.
- Do not treat the Gallup respect/wellbeing buffers as causal management effects.
- Do not treat the WIOA earnings return as universal access or adequate recovery.
- Do not treat public discussion as representative worker opinion.
- The counterexample remains live: the same tool may reduce email time, improve
  service speed, increase monitoring, or intensify expectations in different
  workplaces.
- The key missing denominator is the protected worker or workplace episode in
  which exposure, notice, task/pay/schedule change, training, review, remedy,
  receipt, later earnings and exit are observed together.

## Stronger next test

Select one publicly documented workplace or platform episode with lawful access
to the underlying record and build a dated ledger containing:

1. system, task, exposure date, employer, decision owner, and baseline role;
2. notice, explanation, training, monitoring, schedule, pay, and worker voice;
3. review, correction, remedy, payment or access restoration, with receipt;
4. later hours, earnings, job quality, health, care, and household costs; and
5. continued use, alternative work, collective action, switching, recurrence,
   trust, or exit.

If worker-level follow-up is not lawfully available, publish the exact missing
fields and keep the episode as an open chain. Do not infer household recovery
from aggregate earnings, survey confidence, or an employer announcement.

## Deeper question

**When does AI-enabled time or productivity become durable worker security and
control, rather than a private adaptation whose gains or risks are assigned by
the employer?**

## Stopping rule

This pass is complete only when one same-worker or same-workplace episode links
exposure to a later pay, schedule, household, voice, or exit outcome, or when
the source owner confirms that the missing fields cannot be obtained. The
broader 14-theme program remains ongoing after this pass.
