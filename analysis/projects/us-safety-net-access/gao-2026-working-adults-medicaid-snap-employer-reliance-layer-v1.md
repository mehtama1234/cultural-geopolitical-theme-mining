# GAO 2026 working adults, Medicaid/SNAP, and employer reliance layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-108703
**Reference year:** 2024 worker and program participation; selected employer data as of September 2025; report reissued with revisions August 24, 2026
**Status:** labor–safety-net distribution and employer-concentration finding; not a causal wage, benefit, or policy-effect estimate

## Why this update matters

Public-benefit debates often treat work and safety-net participation as
opposite states. GAO's update shows a more consequential arrangement: millions
of wage-earning adults worked while enrolled in Medicaid or living in a SNAP-
receiving household. The safety net is therefore not only an unemployment
buffer. It is also part of the operating environment for low-wage labor,
health coverage, food security, household scheduling, and employers whose labor
model may coexist with public support.

This matters for the broad trend map because it adds an institutional transfer
between household security and firm labor demand. It also makes “employment” a
weak proxy for material independence: hours, weeks worked, wage level, health
costs, food needs, household composition, eligibility rules, and benefit
continuity can all intervene between having a job and having adequate security.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Medicaid worker scale | About **13.8 million** adults enrolled in Medicaid worked at some point in 2024, out of about 28 million adults ages 19–64 in Medicaid | Census-based estimate; “worked at some point” is not stable employment, adequate earnings, or continuous coverage |
| SNAP worker scale | About **10.6 million** adults living in SNAP-receiving households worked at some point in 2024, out of about 20 million adults ages 19–64 in those households | SNAP is measured at household level; the worker need not be the benefit recipient or household decision-maker |
| Full-time threshold | About two-thirds of working adults in each program worked 35 or more hours per week | Weekly full-time hours do not establish 50-week employment, schedule predictability, wage adequacy, or benefits |
| Annual attachment | Compared with wage earners outside either program, working Medicaid enrollees and SNAP recipients were less likely to work full-time hours for 50 or more weeks per year | The comparison is descriptive and does not identify why the difference exists |
| Employer sector | **88%** of these wage-earning adults in both programs worked in the private sector | Private-sector employment does not mean the employer caused eligibility or can control household benefit status |
| Occupational concentration | Workers were especially represented in transportation, restaurants and food preparation, and retail sales | Occupation shares do not by themselves measure pay, turnover, scheduling, working conditions, or worker choice |
| Employer concentration | In selected data from 11 states, 46 companies—about one-third of the companies across states—were among the top 25 employers of Medicaid enrollees or SNAP beneficiaries in at least two states | These are selected state administrative data and top-employer counts, not a national employer ranking or proof of deliberate benefit shifting |
| Large firms | 17 of those 46 cross-state companies were among the 50 largest Fortune 500 companies by employee count | Firm size and beneficiary presence do not establish per-worker incidence, corporate policy, or legal responsibility |
| State and data scope | Employer analysis used data from 15 state agencies across 11 states as of September 2025 and focused on nondisabled, nonelderly adults for that component | State reporting, definitions, and coverage differ; the employer component is not the same universe as the national Census estimates |
| Revision status | GAO reissued the report on August 24, 2026 to correct Rhode Island figures in two tables | The correction is a reminder that administrative joins and denominators require version control |

## The central mechanism

```text
low or unstable labor-market income
  -> Medicaid/SNAP eligibility and enrollment or household receipt
  -> health coverage and food-budget protection
  -> ability to remain in work, but also benefit cliffs, paperwork, and churn risk
  -> employer scheduling, pay, benefits, retention, and public-policy response
  -> household security, mobility, trust, and political interpretation
```

The key unit is not “working” alone. A worker may be attached to a job while
working too few weeks, facing variable hours, lacking employer health coverage,
or supporting a household whose food needs exceed earnings. Medicaid can
protect against medical-cost exposure; SNAP can protect the food budget. Those
protections may make labor-market participation possible, but eligibility
recertification, reporting requirements, administrative error, or earnings
changes can also create uncertainty around the same work relationship.

The employer concentration finding creates a second mechanism. If large or
repeated employers appear among the top employers of benefit-connected workers,
public resources and firm labor systems are interacting at scale. That is a
useful policy and consumer signal, not yet a conclusion that firms intentionally
underpay, that benefits are being shifted to taxpayers, or that workers would
prefer a different job. To establish those claims requires wages, schedules,
employer benefits, household composition, eligibility transitions, and worker
choice in the same unit.

## What this changes in the broader trend map

This layer joins three previously separated surfaces: household material
security, public-program administration, and firm labor demand. It strengthens
the trend that institutions are increasingly interdependent: employers provide
jobs, households depend on public health and food protection, and government
programs absorb risks that wages or employer benefits do not fully cover.

It also changes the meaning of “labor-force resilience.” A high employment
rate can coexist with short annual attachment, public-benefit reliance, food
pressure, medical-cost exposure, and limited mobility. Conversely, program use
does not imply nonwork or lack of contribution. The atlas should track work
intensity, benefit receipt, employer benefit availability, household need, and
later security as separate currencies.

The layer is complementary to the existing SNAP transition and health-cost
choice records. Those records address transitions, payments, and household
adaptation; this one adds occupational and employer concentration. They should
not be pooled into a single “dependency” index or used to infer employer
intent.

## What remains unproven

GAO does not estimate wages, hours volatility, turnover, benefit take-up,
benefit cliffs, employer health-plan coverage, food insufficiency, medical
debt, or the causal effect of Medicaid or SNAP on employment. It does not show
whether workers selected jobs because of flexibility, public benefits, or lack
of alternatives. It also does not show whether any employer’s staffing model
caused public-benefit participation.

The national estimates and the selected-state employer analysis have different
universes. The employer component excludes disabled and elderly workers in the
described analysis and depends on state administrative data. “Top employer” is
a concentration measure, not a per-worker burden or a comparison of firms'
compensation policies. Program enrollment, household receipt, and individual
benefit use must remain distinct.

## Next test

Build a state-year worker–household–employer ledger linking, for the same
person where lawful and feasible:

- job start, end, hours, weeks, occupation, wage, schedule predictability, and
  multiple-job status;
- employer health coverage, leave, premiums, and eligibility rules;
- Medicaid enrollment and churn, SNAP application/receipt, recertification,
  benefit amount, and administrative interruption;
- household size, disability, children, rent, food sufficiency, medical costs,
  and informal support; and
- later job retention, earnings, health continuity, food security, mobility,
  complaint, and political or institutional response.

Use employer concentration only as a sampling and accountability signal. Test
whether similar firms differ in wages, schedules, benefits, turnover, and
benefit-connected workforce share, while controlling for occupation and place.
The strong result is not fewer benefit users; it is durable household security
with work that is adequately paid, predictable, and not dependent on hidden
public subsidy to remain viable.
