## What the distribution does and does not show

The range is wide enough that a national participation rate cannot stand in for
state experience. But the chart does not identify which part of the spread is
need, eligibility, labor-market conditions, household composition, state
administration, office/channel access, or take-up. It is therefore not a state
performance ranking and not a measure of food-security success.

The state rate also uses a population denominator, while the SIPP transition
records use identified person-month pairs and the USDA household composition
figures use SNAP households. Those units must not be pooled into one rate.

## Next matched test

Match these state rates to a compatible ACS or official state context containing
poverty, income, household composition, disability, language, geography, and
employment, then add state administrative measures such as processing,
procedural denial, renewal, office/channel availability, or outreach. Keep
participation, eligible nonparticipation, procedural loss, food security, and
benefit adequacy as separate outcomes. A high-rate state can still have poor
route access, and a low-rate state can combine low need with low take-up.
