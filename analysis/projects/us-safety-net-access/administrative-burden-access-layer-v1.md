# Safety-net access, administrative burden, and household security layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Sources:** NBER linked administrative and place-based studies, USDA SNAP research, and the existing matched-evidence record  
**Unit:** program participant, parent, census tract, office, household, and policy setting  
**Status:** causal participation/access evidence; same-household downstream outcomes remain open

## Why this layer belongs in the broad program

Public help is not only an eligibility rule or a benefit amount. It also has a
route: notice, documents, travel, internet, language, childcare, waiting,
renewal, and the possibility of an interruption. That route consumes the same
time and money that households need for work, food, care, and housing.

```text
food or income need
  -> eligibility, work rule, notice, office distance, and renewal task
  -> application, receipt, interruption, or exit
  -> food, work, debt, health, time, and household security
  -> trust, complaint, policy demand, and political response
```

## What the evidence supports

- A study of a SNAP work requirement found a 23-percentage-point increase in
  exits among incumbent participants after 18 months, with no employment
  effect in that setting. Exit is therefore not interchangeable with work.
- A study of parents found no work increase but substantially lower SNAP
  receipt, identifying administrative burden as the main mechanism in that
  policy setting.
- A place-based study found SNAP participation in a census tract fell 7–9% over
  two years after an enrollment office closed, with suggestive increases after
  openings. Physical access is part of program access even when eligibility is
  unchanged.
- USDA summarizes research finding that SNAP improves food security and helps
  smooth food access across the month. Participation, however, is not the same
  outcome as food security.

These results support a bounded societal pattern: a rule or access point can
change who receives help without producing the work or security improvement
that the rule is intended to create. The magnitude and direction depend on the
policy, place, population, and available alternatives.

## What this adds to the 14-theme map

- **Public systems and policy feedback:** administration is part of the real
  benefit; caseload changes must be separated into eligibility, non-application,
  and loss after enrollment.
- **Household room and consumption:** the route to aid can consume time, money,
  and attention before any benefit arrives.
- **Time, work, and care:** a renewal task can compete with a shift, commute,
  childcare, or unpaid care.
- **Unequal exposure and status:** disability, language, internet access,
  geography, family structure, and work schedules change the cost of complying.
- **Trust and political meaning:** a failed route may be experienced as a
  broken public promise, but distrust must be measured rather than inferred.
- **Political judgment and collective action:** participation and caseloads can
  shape public interpretation while leaving the household’s downstream result
  unobserved.

## Limits and next test

The cited studies are valuable causal evidence, but each belongs to a defined
state, period, rule, office network, or population. They establish changes in
participation or employment, not the same household’s food, debt, health, time,
trust, or later political action.

The next event-ledger test should record notice date, deadline, documents,
attempts, travel, internet, language, disability, childcare, work hours lost,
decision date, benefit amount, missed purchases, borrowing, health, and return
to the program. Compare online, phone, mail, and in-person routes; smooth
phase-outs with sharp cutoffs; and office-open with office-closed places. Keep
eligibility, application, receipt, interruption, food security, employment,
debt, and trust as separate outcomes.
