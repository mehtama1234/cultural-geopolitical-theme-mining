# Finding 001: Work and public-benefit reliance can coexist in the same labor system

**Status:** bounded work, safety-net, and employer-dependence finding · **Checked: 2026-09-22**

## The bounded finding

GAO's analysis of 2024 Census data estimated that about 28 million adults ages
19–64 were enrolled in Medicaid, and about 13.8 million of them worked at some
point during the year. It also estimated that about 20 million adults lived in
SNAP-receiving households, and about 10.6 million of them worked at some point.
Among the working adults in both groups, about two-thirds worked 35 or more
hours per week, and 88% of wage earners worked in the private sector.

The work was not necessarily steady: compared with nonprogram wage earners,
these groups were less likely to work full-time hours for at least 50 weeks.
In selected administrative data from 15 agencies across 11 states, 46 companies
appeared among the top 25 employers of Medicaid enrollees or SNAP beneficiaries
in at least two states; 17 were among the 50 largest Fortune 500 employers by
employee count. The selected-state employer result is a concentration signal,
not a national ranking or proof of employer intent.

The practical route is:

```text
job, hours, wages, health, and household needs
  -> work alongside Medicaid enrollment or household SNAP receipt
  -> employer, benefit, and public-support interaction
  -> [open] schedule control, adequacy, continuity, bargaining, trust, and exit
```

The broader insight is that “working” and “using a public benefit” are not
opposite social categories. A worker can be attached to the labor market while
the household still needs food support or medical coverage. That does not show
that the job is inadequate, that the employer shifted costs to government, or
that the benefit caused or prevented work; it shows that wages, health costs,
hours, household needs, and public support do not line up into one independence
measure.

## Limits and next test

- “Worked at some point” is not stable employment, adequate pay, predictable
  scheduling, or continuous coverage.
- SNAP is measured at the household level; the worker need not be the recipient
  or decision-maker.
- Medicaid enrollment also does not show who used care, why they qualified, or
  whether coverage supported work.
- The 15-agency employer analysis is selected-state administrative data, not a
  national employer ranking.
- The source does not join wages, hours, employer coverage, turnover, benefit
  churn, schedule control, worker alternatives, or political action.

The next decisive evidence would link the same worker and household to wages,
hours, employer coverage, benefit entry and exit, medical or food needs,
schedule control, and later job stability or bargaining.
