# SNAP restriction, P-EBT expansion, and the food-security boundary

**Checked:** 2026-10-19  
**Status:** bounded comparison of policy direction and purchase response; no nutrition or welfare claim

Two US safety-net studies show that changing the food-benefit rule changes
purchases, but in different directions and with different unresolved burdens.

| Policy surface | Evidence | Observed purchase response | Still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restriction | First statewide SNAP restrictions on sweetened beverages and candy | Restricted spending fell 11%; purchased calories and sugar fell 5–8%; substitution appeared in fruit drinks and snacks; shelf prices barely moved; losing the SNAP tax exemption raised the tax-inclusive price 1.9% | Actual consumption, diet, health, food security, stigma burden, cash substitution, and whether modeled $1.1B annual gains occur |
| P-EBT expansion | State-timed pandemic P-EBT disbursements among eligible households with school-aged children | Sugary-item spending rose 16%, more than overall food spending; no comparable change appeared in placebo households or placebo years | Receipt accuracy, consumption, nutrition, food adequacy, household room, and persistence after payments ended |

## Bounded synthesis

The two studies identify policy-sensitive purchase behavior, not a universal
direction of household welfare. Restriction changes what can be purchased with
SNAP and may alter stigma, substitution, taxes, and store or cash behavior;
P-EBT changes available resources and may produce stocking, sharing, or
purchase-composition responses. Lower or higher spending is not itself a
measure of diet, adequacy, autonomy, or health.

The shared route is policy design → benefit or purchase constraint → product
choice and substitution → food consumption and household room → health,
security, stigma, and political meaning. The existing studies observe the
purchase stage and some causal timing, but not the same household's full food
security or later outcome.

## Next bounded test

The next useful evidence is a household panel joining benefit receipt, food
 purchases, consumption or dietary measures, food-security status, prices,
 cash substitution, and later health. Keep the restriction and P-EBT estimates
as distinct policy estimands.
