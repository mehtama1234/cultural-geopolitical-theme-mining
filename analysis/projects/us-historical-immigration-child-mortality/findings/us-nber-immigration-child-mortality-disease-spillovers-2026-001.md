# Finding: historical immigration exposure could become a shared place-level health risk

**Status:** bounded historical Massachusetts public-health finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35743 builds a Massachusetts town-level panel from 1860 to
1915, when early vital registration makes mortality measurable before national
registration was widespread. Using a shift-share instrumental-variable design,
the paper estimates how local exposure to immigration affected infant and child
mortality.

Increases in the foreign-born share significantly raised infant and child
mortality. The infant effect was concentrated in the post-neonatal period.
Changes in the composition of births by parent nativity explain only part of
the estimated effect, which is consistent with increased exposure to
communicable disease and spillovers to children of native-born parents.

The place-and-health insight is:

> An exposure associated with migration can become a shared environmental
> outcome rather than an effect confined to the exposed group. Housing,
> crowding, sanitation, and public-health capacity mediate how demographic
> change becomes health risk.

## Limits and next test

- This is Massachusetts evidence from 1860–1915, not a contemporary national
  immigration estimate.
- The exact town and birth denominators are not disclosed in the abstract.
- Immigration share does not identify a pathogen, household behavior, housing
  condition, or public-health response by itself.
- The result does not establish individual immigrant mortality or present-day
  political meaning.

The next decisive artifact is a town–household–birth ledger joining immigration
exposure, crowding, sanitation, disease, public-health infrastructure, parent
nativity, mortality, and local political or institutional response.
