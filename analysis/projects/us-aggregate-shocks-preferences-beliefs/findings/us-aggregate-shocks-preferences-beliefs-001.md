# Finding 001: Big shocks can become part of how people see politics and institutions

**Status:** bounded review finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

Giuliano and Spilimbergo review evidence on recessions, inflation, trade,
migration, wars, terrorism, pandemics, and natural disasters. Across the
reviewed literature, aggregate shocks can affect political preferences, risk
attitudes, and trust in institutions. Shocks experienced during young adulthood
often have stronger and longer-lasting effects. Negative economic shocks tend
to move preferences rightward in the reviewed evidence, while non-economic
shocks are more mixed and context-dependent.

In simple terms:

```text
large shared shock
  -> experience is interpreted through age, place, and institutions
  -> preferences, risk beliefs, or trust may change
  -> later political action and permanence remain separate questions
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This supplies a bridge between material conditions and cultural-political
meaning. It helps explain why the same economic or geopolitical event may leave
different political traces across generations and places. It also prevents a
simple “shock causes ideology” story: the review emphasizes different methods,
different contexts, and different persistence.

## Limits and next test

- This is a review of heterogeneous studies, not one pooled U.S. estimate.
- The findings do not establish that every shock permanently changes beliefs.
- Exposure, attribution, media, migration, institutions, and cohort change may
  be entangled.
- Changed preferences are not automatically votes, protest, policy, or durable
  trust.

The next test would follow a defined shock across cohorts or places with
repeated measures of exposure, attribution, trust, identity, political action,
and later persistence.
