# Defense spending, procurement, readiness, and geopolitical leverage bridge

**Status:** thirtieth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** military spending, arms transfers, procurement, industrial capacity, readiness, alliance coordination, and household or social-program incidence

## Question

When military spending and arms transfers rise, how much becomes delivered,
usable, and politically durable capability—and what costs or dependencies are
shifted onto workers, households, allies, and social programs?

```text
security pressure / political commitment
  -> budget and procurement authorization
  -> supplier capacity, review, production, and delivery
  -> acceptance, maintenance, interoperability, and readiness
  -> partner use and public attribution
  -> deterrence, alliance behavior, household incidence, or social-program trade-off
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage or link | Comparable observation | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Military resource allocation has risen | SIPRI estimates 2025 world military spending at $2.887 trillion, up 2.9% in real terms from 2024 and 41% from 2016, equal to 2.5% of global GDP and $352 per person. | Open-source national and regional estimates in current and constant dollars. | Estimated spending is an input, not capability, readiness, welfare, intent, or household incidence. |
| Spending is concentrated but directions differ | In 2025 the top five spenders represented 58% of world spending; U.S. spending was $954 billion and down 7.5% in real terms, while estimated Chinese spending was $336 billion and up 7.4%. | Country estimates and rankings; exchange-rate and accounting comparisons. | China is estimated; monetary ratios do not capture purchasing power, classified spending, aid accounting, or output. |
| Alliance spending creates options, not automatic joint capability | NATO spending was estimated at $1.581 trillion, 55% of world spending; 23 members met at least 2% of GDP under SIPRI's calculation, while Europe rose 14% in real terms. | Alliance and regional aggregation from national estimates. | The 2% threshold does not measure interoperability, readiness, political durability, or burden-sharing quality. |
| Arms-transfer concentration can create dependence | U.S. suppliers accounted for 42% of global major-arms exports in 2021–25, up from 36% in 2016–20; 99 states received U.S. major arms, and the U.S. supplied 58% of European NATO imports. | SIPRI five-year TIV-based transfer-volume comparison. | Transfer volume is not financial value, delivery, maintenance, autonomy, consent, or political control. |

## Cross-source finding

The central pattern is a **resource-to-leverage conversion gap**. Spending and
transfer concentration create material options and bargaining possibilities, but
those options only become usable capability through procurement execution,
industrial capacity, review speed, delivery, acceptance, sustainment,
interoperability, and political authorization.

The chain has at least six separate stages:

1. **Allocation:** budget, GDP burden, aid, and fiscal financing.
2. **Contracting:** authorization, vehicle, obligation, vendor access, and
   review.
3. **Production:** workforce, components, facilities, surge capacity, and
   delivery schedule.
4. **Readiness:** acceptance, maintenance, personnel, training, data, and
   mission-capable status.
5. **Alliance conversion:** partner consent, interoperability, use, repair,
   dependence, and replaceability.
6. **Consequence:** deterrence, external behavior, public legitimacy, worker or
   household incidence, and social-program trade-off.

The key counter-signal is that high spending and large transfer shares can
coexist with readiness gaps, review friction, unfilled recommendations, and
unknown delivered quantities. Conversely, a lower annual U.S. spending figure
may reflect accounting or assistance timing rather than broad capability loss.

## Bounded public discussion

SIPRI's LinkedIn relay links the 2025 spending increase to NATO, security, peace,
and transparency concerns. A second relay places it in a longer U.S., Chinese,
Russian, European, and Ukrainian spending trajectory. SIPRI's arms-transfer
discussion highlights U.S. supplier concentration and recipient dependence while
stopping short of claiming political control. An Al Jazeera expert discussion
connects military competition with critical technology, energy security, trade,
and shipping disruption.

These discussions help identify attribution, transparency, dependence, and
economic-security questions. They are research relays or curated analysis, not
public-opinion samples or evidence of changed state behavior.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- Higher spending can purchase readiness, but can also be absorbed by inflation,
  legacy sustainment, personnel costs, or delayed projects.
- A large contract ceiling can coexist with zero obligation or no delivered
  inventory; a test milestone is not fielding.
- Arms-transfer concentration can reflect emergency aid, replacement cycles,
  or a few large systems rather than durable political dependence.
- A partner can import systems while retaining political autonomy through
  diversification, domestic production, or alternative suppliers.
- Review friction can protect legal and technical quality in some cases; speed
  alone is not capability.
- Defense borrowing can preserve social spending in one episode and displace it
  in another; historical averages do not identify household incidence.

## Stronger next test

Build an actor-and-contract ledger that follows one named capability from:

1. strategic requirement and appropriation;
2. solicitation, review, award, obligation, supplier, workforce, and facility;
3. production lot, delivery, acceptance, training, maintenance, and mission-
   capable status;
4. partner transfer, local co-production, use, repair, replacement options, and
   public or legislative attribution; and
5. fiscal financing, worker conditions, household or social-program incidence,
   alliance response, and later strategic behavior.

Compare a delivered and sustained system, a delayed or cancelled system, and a
partner that diversified away from a dominant supplier. Preserve separate
denominators for money, units, contracts, facilities, readiness, and public
opinion.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The corpus shows why spending and supplier concentration
matter but cannot substitute for procurement realization, readiness, partner
use, public legitimacy, or household incidence. It does not yet follow one
capability from appropriation through accepted delivery, sustained use, and an
attributed geopolitical or social consequence.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call spending strategic leverage, alliance cohesion, or deterrence until a
named capability has verified obligation, delivery, acceptance, use or
readiness, sustainment, a relevant partner or external response, and a tested
counterexample. Do not claim social displacement from aggregate defense growth
without a country- or household-level fiscal and welfare ledger.
