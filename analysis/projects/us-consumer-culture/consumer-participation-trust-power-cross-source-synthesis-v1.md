# Consumer participation, trust, and practical power: cross-source synthesis v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-16  
**Status:** bounded cross-source synthesis; no pooled consumer estimate  
**Scope:** US consumer, household, platform, payment, public-help, and
institutional-response layers already present in the atlas

## The proposition

Consumer participation is not a single measure of welfare or confidence. It is
the visible result of a set of options that may be unequal:

```text
need or exposure
  -> information, price, privacy, time, stigma, safety, and alternative routes
  -> buy, use, share, report, seek help, review, switch, stay, or do not appear
  -> firm/platform/institutional learning and response
  -> recovery, trust, status, dependence, public demand, or exit
```

The current evidence supports the first three stages as separate empirical
surfaces. It does not yet follow one customer through the complete sequence.
The broad societal theme is therefore **participation under unequal
optionality**: people can appear to choose while the feasible cost of refusing,
contesting, or leaving differs sharply by resources, identity, information,
payment route, transport, time, and institutional authority.

## Evidence matrix

| Surface | What the local record measures | Safe interpretation | Missing arrow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer confidence and prices | Monthly US aggregate sentiment, CPI, and unemployment | Felt economic confidence can diverge from aggregate price and labor indicators | Household basket, buffer, attribution, purchase change, and later action |
| Payment systems | Merchant settlement data and modeled fee/reward incidence | Payment design can redistribute value across cash/debit and credit users; the dollar incidence is model-based | Same household's payment choice, prices paid, liquidity, welfare, and switching |
| News and civic platforms | News-source use/trust and descriptive participation types | Attention, trust, and civic action are distinct participation styles | Platform exposure, prior identity, belief change, action cause, and durable engagement |
| Charitable food | Receipt, unmet need, access barriers, safety, treatment, and reasons for nonreceipt | Help can be available yet socially, physically, temporally, or informationally unusable | Dated need, provider alternative, actual food/security outcome, dignity, and later trust |
| Digital fraud and recourse | Reported loss, recovery time, unrecovered money, complaint routing, and response labels | Exposure and institutional visibility do not establish recovery, remedy, or exit | Same customer, originating contact, correction adequacy, repeat effort, trust, and switching |

These records deliberately retain different units, denominators, clocks, and
uncertainty. Their convergence justifies a research priority; it does not
justify adding their percentages or calling the result a consumer welfare
index.

## What the combined evidence changes

### 1. Participation can be costly even when the money price is low

A service, payment route, or public-help route can shift cost into time,
transport, attention, privacy, safety, stigma, or recovery effort. A posted
price and a recorded use event therefore understate the feasible cost of
participation for people with fewer alternatives.

### 2. Trust can increase exposure as well as reduce friction

Trust may make a person more willing to share data, use a payment route, rely
on a platform, or seek help. That can be beneficial, but it can also increase
the information or dependence held by the firm or institution. Trust is not
itself informed consent, fair treatment, remedy, or durable welfare.

### 3. Non-use and non-reporting are substantive observations

Failure to report fraud, seek food help, complain, switch, or appear in a
platform record can mean satisfaction—but it can also mean stigma, futility,
lack of awareness, transport, time, safety, or inability to leave. The missing
record is part of the power relationship and should not be silently coded as
no harm or no demand.

### 4. Status changes the practical meaning of a route

The same help, product, or complaint channel can signal independence to one
person and dependency, poverty, age, weak control, or risk to another. The
consumer-cultural question is therefore not simply whether a route is
available, but who can use it without losing dignity, privacy, or bargaining
power.

### 5. Institutional visibility is not consumer power

Reports, reviews, complaint labels, and response categories make a problem
legible to an institution. They do not establish that the institution corrected
the problem, returned money, protected access, prevented recurrence, or gave
the customer a credible alternative. Visibility can improve accountability or
simply produce a better record of unresolved dependence.

## End-to-end status

| Arrow | Status | Current conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Need/exposure → participation or non-use | Compared/reported | Resources, information, routes, time, and social meaning shape what can be attempted. |
| Participation → cost, data, attention, or status consequence | Reported/estimated | Payment, fraud, food-help, and platform records expose different currencies of participation. |
| Participation/report → institutional learning | Observed/inferred | Transactions, complaints, reviews, and non-use can change what institutions see, but the feedback effect is not jointly measured. |
| Institutional response → verified remedy/recovery | Open/partly observed | Response labels and route visibility are not sufficient evidence of correction or restored security. |
| Experience → trust, identity, civic action, or exit | Open/partly measured | Separate surveys measure trust and action, but no current record joins them to the same consumer episode and later behavior. |

## The next bounded test

Use one customer or household episode from an existing fraud, payment, public-
help, or complaint route and require these fields before promotion:

1. dated need, exposure, or first contact;
2. information shown, privacy choice, price, time, transport, and social-signaling context;
3. attempted use, alternative route, non-use, report, or switch;
4. money, time, data, safety, dignity, and access consequence;
5. responsible firm or institution and the customer's attributed cause;
6. response authority, repeat effort, correction, and verified remedy;
7. later recovery, continued use, dependence, abandonment, switching, trust,
   identity, or political/public demand.

Compare customers with a real alternative against those without one, and
report non-reporters and non-users as a separate selection problem. If no
single source supplies all fields, the correct output is an acquisition gap or
stage-limited comparison—not a synthetic same-customer story.

## Counterexamples that would change the theme

The unequal-optionality interpretation would weaken if:

- practical alternatives were equally usable across resource and status groups;
- non-use tracked lack of need rather than awareness, stigma, time, or futility;
- visible institutional responses reliably produced adequate, verified remedies;
- trust increased participation without increasing extraction or dependence; or
- continued use consistently reflected satisfaction after accounting for exit costs.

## Relationship to the broad program

This synthesis is the consumer-facing expression of the program's governing
chain: condition → feasible options → adaptation → institutional response →
meaning/action → redistribution of money, time, risk, data, and power. It links
household room, platforms and attention, unequal status, public systems, firm
power, trust, and political judgment while preserving the fact that none of
those links is currently a single closed causal chain.
