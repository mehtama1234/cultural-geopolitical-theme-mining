# Critical-minerals application pathway audit

Status: fifty-fifth active depth artifact; checked 2026-10-18.

## Purpose

The critical-minerals route asks when domestic investment and substitution
actually reduce material dependence and create usable strategic capacity. The
correct unit is not a mineral alone; it is the mineral, application, purity,
process, supplier, inventory, qualification requirement, and time available to
switch. This audit compares the battery and semiconductor pathways without
pooling them.

## What we found

| Application pathway | Current evidence | Practical implication | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Battery materials | GAO says battery recycling technologies are mature, but U.S. recycler capacity is limited and feedstock is often landfilled or exported | Some import reliance could fall in roughly 2–3 years if collection, processing, and feedstock systems are built | A potential horizon is not measured recycling output, yield, price, qualification, or import displacement |
| Battery chemistry substitution | GAO identifies lithium-iron-phosphate as a route that can reduce cobalt, manganese, and nickel reliance for stationary grid storage but performs less well in some long-haul vehicle uses | Substitution is application-specific; a technology can reduce dependence in one market while preserving it in another | No firm-level chemistry switch, shipment, price, vehicle deployment, or consumer outcome is linked here |
| Semiconductor gallium/indium | GAO assesses short-term substitution and recycling as unlikely to reduce import reliance; substitutes must perform at the same level in the intended application | Qualification and performance make semiconductor dependence slower to change than a nominal domestic project count suggests | No substitute qualification, inventory, production interruption, or customer re-design record is observed |
| Domestic project route | DOE selected five organizations for award negotiations with cost, purity, and quantity targets; one company separately reports a $3.2 million DOE grant and a Phase 2 target of at least one metric ton of 4N gallium per year | Selection, grant, prototype, and production target create a capacity hypothesis | Selection is not an award or funding commitment; company target is not operating output, customer qualification, or import displacement |
| Refining concentration | IEA reports the average top refined supplier share across energy minerals rose from 68% in 2020 to 70% in 2025; Indonesia and China supplied more than 75% of refined-supply growth from 2023–25 across key markets | New domestic projects can coexist with continuing concentration and external bottlenecks | Aggregate concentration does not identify a particular firm's exposure, inventory, or switching cost |
| Export-control exposure | IEA reports strategic minor-mineral prices more than doubled through 2025 and early 2026, while Chinese-controlled mineral tariff codes subject to export controls tripled since 2023; modeled downstream production at risk includes $6.5T for rare earths and $300B for battery-grade graphite | Policy and price signals can transmit before a physical shortage reaches a product | Modeled downstream exposure is not realized loss, shipment interruption, or geopolitical concession |

The central result is a difference in conversion speed. Battery substitution and
recycling have a plausible route through feedstock and capacity, while
semiconductor materials face tighter performance and qualification constraints.
Domestic capability therefore reduces dependence only when it supplies the
right purity and quantity, is accepted by the application, reaches production,
and can replace an external route in time.

## Evidence ledger

| Arrow | Observed evidence | Missing decisive field |
|---|---|---|
| External concentration → exposure | IEA refining shares and export-control/price context | Named supplier, contract, inventory, shipment, and customer exposure |
| Mineral → application | GAO separates battery chemistry, recycling, gallium, and indium constraints | Purity, performance, qualification, and application-specific substitution record |
| Federal support → capability | DOE selection/grant and company target | Executed award, plant commissioning, output, yield, cost, and customer qualification |
| Disruption/control → firm outcome | Price and modeled downstream-risk signals | Actual shipment delay, price pass-through, production change, redesign, or stockout |
| Domestic project → strategic leverage | Diversification and policy routes are discussed | Supplier switching, alliance procurement change, export-control response, or foreign concession |
| Material route → social incidence | Technology, defense, grid, and consumer applications are named | Worker, community, household, environmental, and downstream product burden |

## What this connects to

- **Infrastructure and technology dependence:** chips, batteries, grids, data
  centers, and defense systems inherit material-specific bottlenecks.
- **Firm power:** a mine, refiner, recycler, component maker, or qualifying
  customer may control the practical switching gate.
- **Geopolitical state consequences:** export controls become leverage only if
  the affected actor cannot substitute, draw inventory, or redirect supply in
  time.
- **Consumer and household room:** a mineral shock may appear as a delayed
  product, higher price, reduced performance, or changed service rather than a
  visible national shortage.
- **Public legitimacy:** domestic projects can symbolize sovereignty while
  leaving foreign processing, technology, or customer dependence intact.

## Deeper question

**When does a domestic mineral project become a qualified, scalable, and
replaceable supply route rather than a capability signal?**

This is high learning value because the battery and semiconductor paths provide
a built-in counterexample to one “critical mineral” story. It also identifies a
bounded next test: one battery material and one semiconductor material, each
followed through an actual control or disruption.

## Bounded public discussion

The existing strategic-autonomy LinkedIn discussion frames sovereignty as
defense, industrial, engineering, and supply-chain capacity within an alliance.
The X resource-security discussion links rare-earth dependence to extraction
cost, cooperation, sovereignty, and strategic response. These discussions help
test how dependence is interpreted publicly, but they do not verify supply,
price, substitution, or leverage.

## Challenge checks

- Import reliance was not treated as a shortage, coercion, or realized harm.
- Battery recycling and LFP substitution were not generalized to gallium or
  indium semiconductors.
- DOE selection, grant, prototype, and production targets were not treated as
  operating output or import displacement.
- IEA modeled downstream exposure was not treated as realized firm loss.
- Aggregate refining concentration was not treated as one firm's dependence.
- Public discussion was used for meaning and counterexamples only.

## Next test

Build two separate application ledgers:

```text
material/application | control or disruption date | named supplier/process |
shipment/price/inventory | substitute candidate | qualification/performance |
recycling output/yield | firm production or redesign | downstream price/availability |
worker/community/environmental incidence | supplier switch | alliance/foreign response
```

Start with one battery material where a shipment or export-control event has a
dated public record, and one semiconductor material with a named substitute or
qualification effort. Require an application-specific unit, a dated supply or
price observation, and a later firm or external response before claiming reduced
dependence or geopolitical leverage.

## Stopping rule

Do not call a material route diversified, sovereign, replaceable, or
geopolitically effective from import reliance, a project selection, a grant, a
recycling horizon, a production target, a price model, or an export-control
announcement alone. Close this subtest only when one application-specific route
links disruption/control to shipment or price, inventory/substitute
qualification, firm output, and an observed supplier or external response—or
when the source owner confirms the missing fields are unavailable.
