# Firm capacity, market power, practical choice, and political voice

**Status:** fifteenth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** firm capacity, AI adoption, labor-market power, search defaults, switching costs, customer dependence, and employer political influence

## The question

When a firm is large, technologically capable, or locally dominant, what kind
of power does it have? Can it raise prices, shape work, influence politics,
control information, or make exit difficult? And what evidence would show that
people can actually respond?

The stages are:

```text
firm scale, capital, data, or local concentration
  -> product, workplace, default, wage, or political channel
  -> customer/worker exposure and dependence
  -> choice, bargaining, complaint, vote, or exit
  -> firm response and remedy
  -> changed market structure, welfare, work control, or political behavior
```

The current evidence shows several power channels, but they must not be pooled
into a single firm-power index.

## What the evidence shows together

### 1. Power is channel-specific

A large firm may have training capacity but also outage exposure. A dominant
employer may influence political expectations without changing every vote. A
search default may reduce active choice while an alternative trial changes
beliefs. A software vendor may raise renewal prices because migration is
costly.

These are related forms of dependence, not one outcome. Firm scale, labor
markdowns, information defaults, political influence, and customer exit need
separate units and tests.

### 2. Capacity can increase options for the firm without increasing options for others

Firms may have capital, data, infrastructure, and legal resources to adapt to
AI or market shocks. Workers and customers may face time, skill, contract,
identity, or network costs when they try to respond. The result can be formal
choice with expensive practical exit.

This is why the next observation must include the cost and success of response,
not simply the availability of an alternative.

### 3. Expectations can move before outcomes

The executive survey shows broad adoption but limited reported past impact and
large forward expectations. Expectations can shape hiring, investment, training,
pricing, and political claims before realized productivity or employment is
visible. A firm’s expectation is an actor belief, not an outcome.

### 4. Political influence may come through dependence

The NBER employer-power study adds a political route: concentrated local
employment can shape communication and turnout. This is important because firm
power may be cultural and political even when a direct price or wage effect is
hard to observe.

The design still does not show coercion or the same worker’s private belief.
Employer communication, worker dependence, vote, and welfare remain distinct.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- Large firms may provide training, wages, or useful infrastructure that expands
  worker options rather than narrowing them.
- A concentrated employer may be politically influential because it supplies
  useful local economic information or community coordination, not because
  workers are coerced.
- A default may improve consumer choice by reducing search costs for a good
  option, while the same default can entrench an inferior one.
- A customer may accept a higher renewal price because the product is valuable,
  not because exit is impossible.
- AI expectations may fail to materialize because implementation, data,
  regulation, or worker resistance limits adoption.
- Worker sorting can reflect amenities, care constraints, discrimination,
  information, or firm quality rather than employer monopsony alone.

## Stronger next test

Choose one firm channel and build an event ledger:

```text
firm and market/place
  -> concentration, default, contract, AI deployment, or employer dependence
  -> customer/worker exposure
  -> response cost and available alternative
  -> actual switch, bargain, complaint, vote, or exit
  -> firm response, remedy, or retention tactic
  -> later price, wage, task, service, market-share, or political outcome
```

For worker cases, join the same workplace or local market to exposure,
communication, dependence, vote or voice, and later mobility. For customer
cases, record contract term, migration time/cost, alternative quality, and
post-switch result. For AI adoption, join firm expectation to implementation,
task, worker control, output, and employment—not only executive opinion.

## Current answer

**Answer state: partly answered.** The repo now shows several distinct channels
through which firms can shape options: labor dependence, defaults, switching
costs, AI expectations, and political communication. It does not justify a
single claim that large firms always harm consumers or workers. The next
decisive evidence is a named firm or local market with a measured response cost,
actual exit or bargaining, and a later outcome.
