# Local business formation and place layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Sources:** Census Business Formation Statistics, HBS/AER entrepreneurship research, NBER linked administrative-data research  
**Unit:** entrepreneurs, applications, employer-firm transitions, counties, and local industries  
**Status:** bounded firm/place layer; local service, belonging, and political effects remain open

## Why this layer belongs in the broad program

A business is not only a company record. It can be an employer, a service, a
meeting place, a local owner, a tax base, or a source of identity. Those roles
must be measured separately. A rise in applications does not automatically mean
more lasting firms, jobs, services, or community power.

```text
capital, credit, labor, customer, or industry change
  -> owner location, application, start, closure, or move
  -> employer firm, jobs, services, prices, and local meeting places
  -> household access, local identity, trust, and political voice
  -> public, lender, worker, and resident response
```

## What the current evidence shows

HBS’s account of Census-based research reports that the local advantage of
entrepreneurship has weakened. Among US-born White men, self-employed people
were 8 percentage points more likely than wage workers to remain in their
birthplace state in 1970, but only 2 points more likely in 2018. For the full
sample, the local advantage falls below zero in the most recent census used.
This is a change in the relationship between entrepreneurship and place, not a
measure of which stores, services, or social institutions disappeared.

The same research reports a shift away from high-capital self-employment sectors
such as agriculture, retail trade, and utilities toward lower-capital sectors
including personal services, construction, and real estate. That offers a
possible mechanism for why local financing and networks may matter differently,
but it does not establish the cause or the community result.

Census publishes annual county Business Formation Statistics for 2005–2025.
The release distinguishes business applications from projected formations and
employer firms, and the 2026 release documents disclosure-avoidance noise.
Applications are therefore an early signal of intended entry, not proof of a
lasting business, local job, service, or place-based relationship.

Linked administrative-data research likewise treats entry as stages: applying
for an employer identification number and becoming an employer firm are
different transitions, and places can differ in the strength of each stage.
This makes a useful societal question visible: is a place losing interest in
starting businesses, or losing the capacity to turn interest into employers?

## What this adds to the 14-theme map

- **Firm, sector, and market power:** separate applications, employer firms,
  ownership, jobs, and local service control.
- **Housing, place, and mobility:** owner movement and business location are
  place processes, not just labor-market outcomes.
- **Household room and consumption:** closures or moves may alter access,
  prices, travel, and choice, but those effects require local records.
- **Trust, identity, and cultural meaning:** a local business can be a social
  institution, but belonging cannot be inferred from turnover counts.
- **Political judgment and collective action:** local tax base, services, and
  meeting places may shape public voice; the political arrow is currently
  unmeasured.
- **Geopolitical/state consequences:** industry and ownership shifts can alter
  local and national productive capacity, but this layer does not yet trace
  them to state dependence.

## Limits and next test

The evidence is strongest for entrepreneur location, business-entry stages, and
the distinction between applications and firms. It is not yet a county-level
finding about closures, prices, jobs, essential services, social cohesion, or
voting.

Select matched counties or commuting areas and align applications, projected
formations, employer establishments, payroll jobs, ownership/location, rents,
credit, local prices, essential-service access, and local political measures.
Include a place where applications rise but employer formation or services do
not, as a counterexample. Keep business survival, worker outcomes, customer
access, identity, and political participation as separate endpoints.
