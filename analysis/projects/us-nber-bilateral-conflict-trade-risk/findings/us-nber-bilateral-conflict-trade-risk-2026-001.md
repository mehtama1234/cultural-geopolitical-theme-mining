# Finding 001: Economic coercion can replace military confrontation while retaliation compounds trade losses

**Status:** bounded comparative geopolitical-to-trade finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35077 constructs a monthly bilateral hostility measure from
GDELT events, calibrates it against human-curated ground truth, and separates
kinetic fighting, military posture, sanctions-context hostility, and routine
diplomacy. The paper reports that, over the past decade, the trade-weighted
share of hostility channelled through sanctions contexts nearly doubled.

In a gravity-trade analysis, kinetic conflict and sanctions-context hostility
are associated with large trade reductions, while routine diplomacy has no
detected aggregate trade effect. A directed structure identifies retaliation
that compounds trade losses over several months. Relative to the paper's
pre-escalation baseline, it estimates roughly $334 billion in bilateral trade
at risk, with the United States–China pair accounting for about half.

The safe interpretation is:

> Economic coercion is not merely a rhetorical substitute for military conflict;
> it can be a measurable trade-disrupting interaction whose effects persist
> through retaliation. The estimate does not show who pays or whether the
> coercing government achieves its political objective.

## Evidence chain

```text
geopolitical hostility and sanctions-context pressure
  -> government or counterpart response
  -> bilateral trade disruption
  -> lagged retaliation and compounding loss
  -> [open] firm substitution, household incidence, alliance behavior,
          public legitimacy, and strategic success
```

## What it adds to the broader atlas

This adds a measured comparative bridge between external state behavior and
economic exposure. It is different from the CRS tariff timeline, which records
the sequence of 2025 U.S. pressure, foreign countermeasures, pauses, and
negotiations. The NBER paper supplies a cross-pair pattern and an estimated
retaliation mechanism; it does not replace an episode-level account.

It also sharpens the meaning of “geopolitical consequence.” A government can
use economic instruments without a military exchange, but the resulting cost
may travel through exporters, importers, suppliers, workers, consumers, and
third countries before any political objective is observable.

## What the source does not show

- The panel is global and bilateral; it is not a U.S.-only estimate.
- The event indicator and gravity estimates are authored measurement and model
  outputs, not a direct count of household or firm losses.
- The $334 billion figure is a counterfactual comparison with a
  pre-escalation baseline, not observed lost revenue or a forecast.
- Trade reduction does not establish coercive success, policy concession, or
  alliance breakdown.
- The source does not identify product-level substitution, prices, employment,
  public opinion, or incidence by income or ownership.

## Next decisive test

Follow one U.S.-linked coercion episode through the named demand, target,
retaliatory action, affected products and firms, price or quantity change,
supplier substitution, domestic political pressure, negotiation terms, and an
attributed policy change or durable reversal. Keep government response, firm
compliance, household incidence, alliance behavior, and strategic outcome as
separate evidence stages.
