# Claims ledger: US local prices and the geography of consumption

**Check date:** 2026-09-11  
**Scope:** United States; local prices, income group, consumption, place, and mobility

| ID | Claim | Evidence type | What supports it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | Prices differ across US places, and housing is a major part of that difference. | BEA regional accounts | BEA's 2024 RPPs range from 110.7 in California to 86.9 in Arkansas; housing-rent RPPs range from 154.3 in California to 54.2 in West Virginia. | State averages hide neighborhoods, quality, and household choices. |
| C-002 | Local price differences are wider for low-income households. | Consumption and price-index study | NBER Working Paper 29533 builds price indexes for more than 140 products and finds nearly twice the spatial spread for low-income as high-income households. | The index is not a full measure of services, jobs, or place quality. |
| C-003 | Expensive places can offer lower real consumption to less-educated households. | Consumption-data estimate | NBER finds a significant negative relationship between local prices and real consumption for high-school graduates and dropouts; the estimated difference between the most and least affordable commuting zones is large. | It does not show why households stay or move. |
| C-004 | High local prices do not affect all income groups in the same way. | Group comparison | NBER finds little relationship between local prices and real consumption for college graduates with similar observable characteristics. | This result does not mean high-income households face no cost or constraint. |
| C-005 | Place changes real buying power, but price alone does not tell us whether a place is better. | Our inference | C-001 through C-004 connect local prices, income group, housing, consumption, and place choice. | Jobs, services, safety, family ties, moving costs, and product quality remain separate. |

## Mechanism under test

```text
place and income
  -> local price, housing, service, and job mix
  -> real buying power
  -> product, housing, work, or moving choice
  -> household security and local political judgment
```

## What would weaken the finding

- Low-income households receive enough wage, service, housing, or family benefits in high-price places to offset the price gap.
- Local prices do not change actual consumption after quality and product choice are counted.
- Moving costs and job access explain the result better than price exposure.
- A cheaper place has such poor access or quality that its lower price does not create more real room.

## Next test

Join the same household's income, prices, purchases, housing, transport, health, job access, and moves over time. Compare similar families across places and keep price, service quality, and the value of staying separate.
