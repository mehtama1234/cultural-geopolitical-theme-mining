# Finding 001: Security cooperation commitments depend on a workforce still being built and staffed

**Status:** bounded geopolitical workforce and institutional-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The Department of Defense uses a security-cooperation workforce in the United
States and overseas to plan, monitor, and carry out activities such as training
partners, selling defense articles, and checking how those articles are used.
GAO identified 66 workforce provisions in the FY2024 defense authorization law.
As of May 2026, GAO found work complete on 46 of them. Within the 36-part Human
Capital Initiative, 23 provisions were complete, 11 were in progress, and 2 had
not started. DSCA estimated that the remaining work would be finished by the
end of FY2028.

DSCA had identified 19,379 workforce positions across nine functional areas as
of October 2025. But the report also describes capacity gaps: about five of 15
positions at the Foreign Military Sales Center of Excellence were unfilled,
which officials said limited curriculum, course, and research capacity. A
January 2025 hiring freeze also paused efforts to expand some Defense Security
Cooperation Service staffing, leaving that new organization behind its hiring
targets.

The route is:

```text
U.S. security objective
  -> trained and staffed security-cooperation workforce
  -> planning, transfer, monitoring, and partner contact
  -> [open] partner capability and sustained use
  -> [open] trust, alliance behavior, and strategic effect
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a workforce and continuity version of a recurring geopolitical
pattern:

> A country can make formal commitments to build partner capacity while the
> people who must plan, train, monitor, and sustain those commitments are still
> being counted, certified, hired, and organized.

The finding does not say that security cooperation is failing. Professional
certification, workforce coding, research grants, and a formal service can make
the system stronger. The narrower point is that institutional capacity has its
own pipeline: a legal requirement becomes a team, a filled position, a trained
person, a monitored activity, and only then a possible partner capability.

It also gives the atlas a cultural-relational angle. The workforce standards
include country and regional awareness, and overseas staff work with foreign
governments. Staffing, training, and continuity therefore affect how a U.S.
commitment is experienced by a partner, even though this report does not
measure partner trust or judgment.

## Limits and next test

- Position counts are not the same as filled jobs, experienced staff, or time
  available for a particular partner.
- “Complete” provisions describe implementation status, not operational
  effectiveness.
- The report does not link a staffing gap to a named partner's training,
  delivery, monitoring, readiness, or later behavior.
- Workforce counts use different scopes; the 19,379 positions, 736 functional
  area positions at security-cooperation organizations, and 1,320 SCO personnel
  are not interchangeable denominators.
- The planned FY2028 completion date is not proof that the reforms will be
  completed or durable.

The next decisive artifact is a lawful partner- and activity-level ledger
joining assigned personnel, qualifications, turnover, contact and monitoring
frequency, equipment or training delivery, partner use, sustainment, and later
cooperation or refusal. Until then, the safe claim is that the workforce is a
real conversion stage between U.S. security policy and partner capability, with
formal progress and remaining staffing capacity risks visible at once.
