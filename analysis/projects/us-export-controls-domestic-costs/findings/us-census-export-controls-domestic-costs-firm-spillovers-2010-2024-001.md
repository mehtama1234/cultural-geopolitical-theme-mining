# Finding 001: A targeted geopolitical restriction can spread through the firm

**Status:** bounded export-control, firm, labor, and geopolitical-cost finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

The Census CES paper studies U.S. export controls using transaction data and the
product classifications used to administer the controls. It reports that the
share of U.S. exports subject to controls doubled between 2010 and 2024.

Using the 2014 U.S. restriction on exports to Russia as a natural experiment, the
paper finds that affected firms became less likely to export the targeted goods
to Russia. The effects did not stop there: affected firms also reduced exports
of other products and to other destinations, and their average payroll fell.

The practical chain is:

```text
national-security export control
  -> targeted product/destination restriction
  -> compliance, demand, supply-chain, or strategic adjustment
  -> other exports, destinations, payroll, and firm capacity
  -> worker, consumer, diplomatic, and security consequences [open]
```

The simple geopolitical insight is that a state can impose a narrow rule with a
wider domestic footprint. The wider footprint may be a cost, an adaptation, or
part of the intended pressure—but the firm data alone cannot decide which.

## What this adds to the atlas

This creates a clear domestic-to-geopolitical bridge: external policy can change
firm scope, market access, and labor resources at home. It also strengthens the
program’s rule that strategic capacity and domestic burden must be measured
separately. A restriction can work on the target while producing spillovers in
places the headline policy did not name.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is a Census CES working paper, not a Census Bureau endorsement.
- The 2014 Russia restriction is one policy episode; effects may differ across
  controls, countries, products, and firms.
- Export and payroll changes do not measure national-security success or failure.
- Lower average payroll is not identical to layoffs, lower wages, or household
  harm.
- Spillovers may reflect demand, sanctions, compliance, substitution, macro
  conditions, or strategic firm response.

The next decisive artifact is a firm-product-destination-worker panel linking
control notice, license, shipment, substitution, prices, payroll, employment,
investment, new markets, consumer incidence, and later security or diplomatic
outcomes.
