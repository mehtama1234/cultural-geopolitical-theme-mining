# A free platform still has a time price

**Status:** provisional platform-consumption finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

NBER Working Paper 34743 uses two field experiments on Facebook and Instagram
to study demand for time-intensive digital goods. The paper treats ad load as
the primary user cost in the setting, reports low user response to ad load, and
finds that a person's time share and time cost influence which online or
offline activities can substitute for the platform. It also models the
possible competition implications of separating Facebook and Instagram.

```text
free or low-money-price platform
  -> ad load + time and attention cost
  -> staying, substitution, or offline activity
  -> consumer benefit and platform competition
  -> [open] work, care, sleep, well-being, switching, and power
```

The finding adds a distinct consumer trend to the atlas: attention is not only
an outcome of platform design; it is an input into demand and competition. It
does not make time spent equivalent to harm, and it does not turn a modeled
de-merger into an observed policy effect.

## What the study contributes

| Layer | Evidence | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| User cost | Two Facebook/Instagram field experiments vary or measure ad load as the main user cost in the setting | Public abstract does not provide all treatment cells, logged minutes, content value, or subgroup outcomes |
| Substitution | Time share and time cost influence diversion across online and offline activities | Diversion is not observed household welfare, care displacement, or successful switching |
| Demand | Users show low elasticity to ad load in the reported setting | Low elasticity can mean value, habit, limited substitutes, or tolerable ads |
| Competition | The paper explores a potential Facebook/Instagram separation | A model is not an actual structural change or realized consumer outcome |

## Why this matters for the broad goal

Money-price measures miss a central part of digital consumption. A person may
pay with minutes, attention, interruption, or foregone alternatives while the
app remains nominally free. The cost can be positive when the time is valued as
leisure or connection, and negative when it crowds out sleep, care, work, or a
preferred activity. The same number of minutes can therefore represent very
different household and cultural outcomes.

This also changes how platform power should be read. A user may not leave when
ads increase because the platform is socially valuable, because close online
and offline substitutes are unavailable, or because the user has adapted to
the ad burden. Continued use is not proof of satisfaction, dependence, or
free choice. Conversely, low ad-load response is not proof of exploitation.

## Next test

The next high-value design is a small consented time-use panel, not a large
platform scrape. Record platform, version, ad load, content purpose, active
minutes, interruptions, offline substitute, work/care/sleep context,
accessibility, switching attempt, and later perceived benefit or burden. Keep
time-use, content value, well-being, and platform market power as separate
outcomes. A useful counterexample is a user with similar exposure who reports
valuable connection and no displaced activity; another is a user who reduces
platform use but loses social or informational access.
