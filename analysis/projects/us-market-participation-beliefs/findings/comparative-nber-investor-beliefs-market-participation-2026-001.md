# Finding 001: Fear of crashes can shape who stays in financial markets

**Status:** bounded comparative market-participation and belief finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study uses more than two decades of respondent-level investor surveys
and compares people’s beliefs with option-implied market benchmarks.

Investors who step out of the market are generally more pessimistic about future
returns and see greater crash risk than the pricing population. Subjective crash
beliefs remain an important predictor of participation even after expected
returns are considered.

The study also reports that stocks more sensitive to the beliefs of these
non-marginal investors earn lower returns, especially when disagreement is
high. The authors describe this as a composition effect: belief-driven exit can
leave risk concentrated among fewer investors.

The route is:

```text
belief about crash risk
  -> market participation or exit
  -> who remains to price and hold risk
  -> [open] realized wealth, retirement security, and later trust
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a financial-culture version of a recurring pattern:

> Exit is not only a response to material conditions; it can also reflect a
> person’s interpretation of risk, and that exit can change the system left
> behind.

That is different from saying that pessimistic people are irrational or that
participation always improves welfare. Beliefs may encode liquidity needs,
limited risk capacity, advice, age, or prior losses.

## Limits and next test

- The public abstract does not give the full sample, geographic frame, or
  socioeconomic coverage.
- A participation proxy is not a complete record of every account or asset.
- Beliefs may reflect financial constraints rather than cause market exit.
- The source does not measure household wealth, retirement adequacy, realized
  gains or losses, or later participation for the same people.

The next decisive test is a protected household-finance panel joining stated
crash beliefs, account participation, liquidity needs, advice, fees, holdings,
realized returns, and later financial security. Until then, the safe claim is
that beliefs help sort who participates and that this sorting can affect market
risk composition.

## Source

- [NBER Working Paper 35708: “Non-Marginal” Investor Beliefs](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35708)

**Evidence status:** respondent-level survey and market evidence supports a
belief-to-participation composition pathway; household welfare and geographic
transfer remain open.
