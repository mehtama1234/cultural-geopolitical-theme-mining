# Finding 001: Tracking restrictions can reduce a measured fraud signal

**Status:** bounded U.S. privacy and consumer-fraud finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 31692 uses Apple's App Tracking Transparency policy as a
natural experiment. The study reports that restricting data tracking and
sharing reduced consumer fraud complaints, with larger effects for complaints
involving personal-information misuse and in areas dominated by firms with
riskier data practices. The paper also reports a decline in dark-web discussion
and higher prices for sensitive data, connecting the consumer complaint result
to a data-resale mechanism.

```text
mobile tracking and data sharing
  -> data availability and resale exposure
  -> fraud incidence or reporting signal
  -> [open] loss, reimbursement, account restoration, trust, and exit
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This closes a consequence stage that the browser-default study does not: data
governance can change a measured consumer-harm signal through an intermediary
data market. It also makes privacy a practical-risk issue rather than only a
preference or formal-control question. The source still does not provide a
same-consumer loss-to-recovery path, so complaint decline must not be narrated
as universal safety or restored household room.

## Limits and next test

- Fraud complaints are a reported/observed signal, not all fraud incidence or
  all unreported loss.
- The different complaint, geographic, and data-market outcomes have distinct
  units and denominators.
- Tracking restrictions may shift fraud channels or create other costs.
- The result does not establish reimbursement, account restoration, trust, or
  durable switching/non-use.

The next decisive test is a protected consumer or account panel joining device
and tracking exposure, data sharing, fraud attempt, realized loss, reporting,
investigation, reimbursement, account restoration, time burden, trust, and
switching or non-use.
