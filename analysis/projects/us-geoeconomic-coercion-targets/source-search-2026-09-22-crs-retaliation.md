# Source search: coercion, retaliation, and outside response

**Search date:** 2026-09-22  
**Geography:** United States and trading partners  
**Status:** bounded pass complete; foreign retaliation is visible, but later strategic success and public incidence remain open

## Working question

When the United States uses tariffs as leverage, do foreign governments and firms merely absorb the pressure, or do they answer, negotiate, and later change course?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-CRS-TARIFF-RETALIATION-2025 | [Presidential 2025 Tariff Actions: Timeline and Status](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48549/R48549.11.pdf) | CRS records dated foreign countermeasures, including Chinese tariffs, export restrictions, company designations, and later temporary reductions; Canadian and EU responses also include tariffs, pauses, exemptions, and negotiations. | Congressional Research Service, based on official government documents | It records announced and implemented government actions, not firm-level pass-through, household prices, alliance durability, or whether U.S. goals were achieved. |

## First pattern

```text
U.S. tariff pressure
  -> foreign government retaliation or negotiation
  -> tariffs, export controls, company designations, pauses, or exemptions
  -> [open] firm pass-through, public cost, coalition durability, and strategic result
```

This adds the missing external-response stage to the geoeconomic lane. A foreign government can answer with measures aimed at U.S. goods and companies, then later pause, reduce, or remove some measures while leaving others in place. A response is therefore not the same thing as either surrender or durable escalation.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

The timeline is a government-action record compiled from official documents. It does not show whether a measure changed the other government's underlying policy, whether firms complied, or who paid. Temporary tariff reductions and negotiated frameworks can reflect bargaining, but they do not by themselves prove strategic success.

## Decision rule

Keep U.S. pressure, foreign government response, firm response, public incidence, negotiation, alliance behavior, and strategic outcome as separate stages. Reopen this lane only with a case that adds firm or household incidence, or an attributed policy change after the response.
