# Finding 002: Tariff pressure can produce retaliation and negotiation, not just compliance

**Status:** bounded geoeconomic external-response finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in simple words

When the United States raises tariffs, other governments may answer. A CRS
timeline records China using tariffs, export restrictions, company designations,
and investigations. Canada and the European Union also announced or used
countermeasures, then paused, reduced, exempted, or negotiated over some of
them.

The simple route is:

```text
U.S. tariff pressure
  -> foreign government response
  -> retaliation, negotiation, pause, exemption, or partial reversal
  -> [open] firm costs, public prices, alliance durability, and strategic result
```

This means “the other side responded” is a real geopolitical event, but it is
not the same as either surrender or permanent escalation.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

The earlier geoeconomic model showed why pressure may target governments or
private firms. This record adds the next visible stage: the targeted government
can answer with measures aimed at U.S. goods and companies, and can later
change those measures while bargaining continues.

That makes external response a separate part of state power. A tariff can
create leverage, but it can also create a negotiation cycle, new pressure on
firms, and uncertainty for people who buy or sell across the border.

## What it does not show

- The CRS report is a dated government-action timeline, not a causal evaluation.
- It does not show which firms complied, passed costs through, or found substitutes.
- It does not measure household prices, jobs, or public opinion.
- A pause or reduction does not prove that the United States achieved its goal.
- A retaliatory action does not prove durable escalation or alliance breakdown.

## Next decisive test

For one retaliation episode, link the government action to affected firms,
prices or trade flows, domestic political pressure, later negotiation terms,
and an attributed policy change. Keep the external response separate from firm
compliance and public incidence.
