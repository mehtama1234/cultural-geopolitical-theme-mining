# Large-load operating event to household incidence stage audit

Status: fifty-fourth active depth artifact; checked 2026-10-18.

## Purpose

The infrastructure route asks whether a large computational load becomes a
fair, reliable, and locally legitimate public bargain. Existing records now
contain an operating event, emergency authority, cost-allocation rules, a
proposed household credit, and executed Georgia contract filings. This audit
separates those stages from actual facility payment, residential incidence,
environmental burden, and local acceptance.

## What we found

The public chain has advanced from forecast to operational response and formal
governance, but not yet to matched household outcome:

| Stage | Evidence | Signal | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating event | PJM, July 22, 2026 | Approximately 4,000 MW of data-center load disconnected; facilities switched to backup generation while operators managed imbalance and voltage/frequency spikes | No public facility roster, duration, backup emissions, residential interruption, or bill effect |
| Cost/reliability governance | FERC June order and PJM response | Large-load scope discussed as at least 50 MW at a site with transmission interconnection above 69 kV; cost transparency, upgrade recovery, and cost-shifting concerns were formally raised | Regulatory finding and proposed response are not adopted compliance or realized cost allocation |
| Emergency authority | September 2026 PJM/DOE route | PJM could direct backup generation at large loads as a last resort; one forecast anticipated about 700 MW below reserve margin, 133,285 MW demand, and 33,191 MW generation outages | Authority, forecast, and stated residential-protection target do not show dispatch, avoided outage, emissions, or customer experience |
| Virginia approved framework | SCC cost-allocation implementation | Modeled typical 1,000-kWh residential increase is $0.94 under amended method versus $2.90 under an earlier method; large loads must fund direct-connect costs and meet demand obligations | Modeled typical-customer values are not billed amounts or proof of large-load-only causation |
| Virginia household-relief proposal | September 2026 package | Proposed $10 monthly residential credit for four years; separate $0.011/kWh data-center consumption tax had collection machinery scheduled | Proposal/tax administration do not establish bill posting, eligibility, receipt, net burden, or adequacy |
| Georgia facility-linked governance | January–August 2026 PSC docket | At least four executed large-load contract filings visible for Commission review; one executed filing dated August 27 | Final terms, construction, energization, facility payment, reliability, and household incidence remain unobserved |
| Public legitimacy | Pew/APPC surveys and bounded discussion | 60% in Pew were not too/not at all comfortable with a new local data center; APPC opposition was 61%, up from 49% in spring | National or broad polling is not local exposure, participation, consent, or causal response |

The central finding is a stage mismatch. The most concrete technical event and
the most concrete governance response still do not reveal whether a named
facility paid, whether the household received a credit, whether service was
more reliable, or whether local participation changed a later decision.

## Operating-event follow-up — 2026-10-19

PJM's subsequent operating account makes the July event more reproducible than
the earlier aggregate description. According to [PJM's event
review](https://insidelines.pjm.com/pjm-dominion-review-large-load-transfer-event/),
 a mechanical failure removed a 230-kV Northern Virginia line at 7:56 a.m.; a
first 2,970 MW wave and a second 1,099 MW wave transferred data-center load to
backup generation. PJM reports an overall load change from 99,984 MW to 96,205
MW, control-area error recovery within nine minutes, and reactive-power action
to reduce voltage. It also identifies similar approximately 1,500 MW Dominion
zone events on February 17, 2025, and July 10, 2024.

This follow-up closes more of the operating-mechanism arrow and adds a useful
counterexample to a one-off-event interpretation: the grid did not experience
significant reliability impacts according to the operator, even though voltage
and frequency moved sharply. It still does not identify the facilities,
backup-generation duration or emissions, facility cost or tariff treatment,
residential interruption or bill effect, or whether the later ride-through
process changed behavior. The event is therefore a stronger operating record,
not a realized household-protection or fairness result.

The linked [PJM/Dominion preliminary presentation](https://www.pjm.com/-/media/DotCom/committees-groups/committees/oc/2026/20260806/20260806-item-05---dominion-july-loss-of-load-event---presentation.pdf)
adds a finer event clock and physical boundary: the 2031 line opened at
07:56:09, initial load loss of 2,970 MW occurred at 07:56:33, secondary loss of
1,099 MW at 07:57:40, and the presentation estimates 3,869 MW transferred in
total. It identifies the Loudoun and Prince William data-center pockets and
specific substations/circuits, reports a preliminary mechanical failure cause,
and says customers transferred load back to the grid within 30 minutes. The
presentation is explicitly preliminary; it does not name the individual
facilities, report backup-generator runtime or emissions, or connect the event
to a retail bill, residential service interruption, or facility payment.

The Virginia implementation route is more specific than the earlier modeled
charge alone. The [SCC summary](https://www.scc.virginia.gov/about-the-scc/scc-facts/)
states an 85% minimum monthly transmission/distribution cost obligation for
large-load customers and possible collateral covering up to 60% of minimum
charges for qualifying new customers. The [SCC tax guidance](https://www.scc.virginia.gov/regulated-industries/public-taxation/taxes-and-fees/)
describes data-center registration and monthly remittance, and the [enacted
budget](https://budget.lis.virginia.gov/item/2026/2/HB30/Enrolled/3/3-5.24/)
sets a September 2026 first collection, including arrears from July 1. These
are stronger obligation and administration records, but still do not show a
named facility's payment, a posted household credit, net bill effect, or local
acceptance.

An independent [CHPE first-summer market
analysis](https://modoenergy.com/research/es/chpe-market-impact-first-summer-nyiso)
reports a 20-day cable-splice outage after July 4, zero output in 83% of
five-minute intervals during June–August, a 13% first-summer capacity factor,
and an afternoon/evening operating pattern. It also reports a conditional
Zone J day-ahead spread difference on 85–90°F days when the line ran. This is a
useful availability and wholesale-market layer, but it is not a retail bill,
household reliability, route-community, or acceptance measure; the underlying
series and counterfactual are not reproduced in the public article.

## Evidence ledger

| Arrow | Current evidence | Missing decisive field |
|---|---|---|
| Load growth → operation | Forecasts and a dated PJM disconnection event | Facility identity, metered load, event duration, and counterfactual operation |
| Operation → reliability response | Backup generation, operator intervention, emergency authority, ride-through proposal | Actual dispatch, frequency/voltage outcome, outage avoided or caused, and emissions |
| Rule → facility obligation | FERC/PJM concerns, Virginia cost rule/tax, Georgia contract filing | Final contract terms, collateral/minimum bill, facility payment, compliance, and audit |
| Facility obligation → household incidence | Modeled $0.94/$2.90 charges and proposed $10 credit | Customer-class recovery, observed residential bills, credit posting/receipt, net affordability |
| Incidence → household/public meaning | Pew/APPC concern and public discussion about cost, land, water, and consent | Exposed household/place, local meeting participation, attributed burden, and later trust |
| Governance → durable acceptance | Community-engagement and transparency directions | Permit/decision change, local challenge or acceptance, repeated participation, and later conflict |

## What this connects to

- **Infrastructure and firm power:** large-load firms, utilities, operators, and
  regulators hold different parts of the causal chain.
- **Household room:** modeled charges, credits, outages, and environmental
  burdens affect households through different currencies.
- **Public legitimacy:** transparency and participation can matter even when
  technical reliability is maintained.
- **Geopolitical/technology dependence:** AI capacity depends on electricity,
  transmission, cooling, and public permission, not only compute hardware.
- **Local culture and politics:** opposition may reflect land, water, emissions,
  cost causation, or control rather than a single attitude toward AI.

## Deeper question

**When does a large-load reliability and cost rule become a realized public
bargain—paid by the responsible facility, visible in household service, and
accepted or contested by the affected place?**

This is high learning value because the route now has a dated operational shock,
formal rules, an enacted tax, a proposed benefit, and facility-linked filings.
The unresolved middle is narrow enough to test one tariff area or facility
without generalizing across all data centers.

## Bounded public discussion

Existing LinkedIn records connect the PJM event to ride-through and coordination,
the Virginia proposal to cost causation, and survey results to water, land,
pollution, jobs, transmission, and host-community value. These records expose
the public interpretations and counterarguments, but they do not verify
facility payment, household bills, reliability, or consent.

## Challenge checks

- A 4,000 MW disconnection was not treated as a residential outage.
- The PJM follow-up's 3,800 MW two-wave account and nine-minute ACE recovery
  were not treated as proof of either household protection or facility-level
  compliance; they establish operating response and a bounded system outcome.
- Forecast demand, emergency authority, and proposed standards were not treated
  as observed household protection.
- Modeled charges and proposed credits were not treated as bills or receipt.
- An executed contract filing was not treated as energization or payment.
- Poll opposition was not treated as local consent or a causal response.
- PJM, FERC, Virginia, Georgia, survey, and social records were not pooled as
  independent evidence of one effect.

## Next test

Select one Georgia contract route or Virginia tariff area and build:

```text
facility/project key | final disposition and contract terms | construction/energization |
forecast and metered load | event/dispatch/reliability | minimum bill/collateral/tax |
facility payment and compliance | customer-class recovery | residential bills/credit |
environmental or land/water burden | local hearing/comments | later acceptance/conflict
```

Require a dated facility identity, observed load or disruption, actual payment
or billed allocation, a household/service outcome, and local participation
before claiming realized fairness or legitimacy. If the primary docket and
utility records do not expose those fields, publish the bounded non-observation
and rotate.

## Stopping rule

Do not call data-center growth harmful, beneficial, reliable, or legitimate from
a load forecast, single operating event, modeled charge, proposed credit,
approved rule, executed filing, or opinion poll alone. Close this subtest only
when one matched route links operation to facility payment, household bill or
reliability, environmental/local incidence, and later acceptance or conflict—or
when the source owner confirms the missing fields are unavailable.
