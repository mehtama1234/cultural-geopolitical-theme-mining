# Large-load contracts to household incidence and local consent

## What we found

Large-load governance has moved beyond forecasts in some places, but the
public evidence still stops at different stages of the chain. Virginia has an
approved cost-allocation framework, a proposed household-credit route, and
implementation instructions. Texas has a proposed long-term contract and
security framework. Georgia's public docket shows at least four identified
executed large-load contract filings in January-August 2026 and a visible
Commission-review sequence.

Those are meaningful implementation signals. They are not yet evidence that a
facility paid its required share, that a project was energized and consumed
the forecast load, that a household bill fell, that reliability improved, or
that a host community accepted the result.

## The deeper question

When do large-load protections become a realized household bargain—actual
facility payment, bill or reliability outcome, and informed local consent—rather
than a filed contract, modeled promise, or public argument?

## Why this matters

The answer could change how we read the current data-center story. If contract
terms are paid, household incidence is limited, service remains reliable, and
local participation changes later decisions, the governance chain is doing
more than signaling protection. If the chain breaks between filing, payment,
and lived outcome, the public claim is weaker even when the rules look strong.

This is a high-priority next question because it has high decision value,
high missing-link value, high connection value, and high disconfirmation
value. Effort is medium and scope risk is medium: one tariff area or facility
route must be followed without generalizing across states.

## What we know so far

1. Virginia's framework assigns specified direct-connect costs to new large
   loads and imposes minimum-demand and contract protections. The public
   record also contains an approved modeled residential comparison and a
   separate proposed credit package.
2. Texas's record is still a proposed-rule stage: a 20-year minimum
   interconnection agreement, direct cash contribution, and annual
   customer-class revenue reporting are proposed mechanisms, not realized
   transfers.
3. Georgia's official docket provides the strongest bridge in this pass from
   governance to a facility-linked event: executed contract filings were
   submitted for Commission review. The public record does not yet expose the
   contract's load, minimum bill, security, payment, or outcome.
4. The public discussion layer reinforces the cost-causation dispute. It is a
   bounded interpretation layer, not a substitute for docket, billing, or
   reliability data.

## What could change the answer

The answer would move toward “realized” if primary records show all of the
following for a matched route: final contract terms; approval and conditions;
construction and energization dates; forecast and metered load; minimum bills,
collateral, taxes, or direct contributions actually paid; utility recovery by
customer class; residential bills and reliability measures; environmental or
land-use burden; and local participation followed by a later acceptance,
challenge, or conflict outcome.

It would move the other way if a contract is filed but not approved or built,
if a facility pays less than the attributed system cost, if billed incidence
differs materially from the model, if reliability worsens during load events,
or if procedural participation does not alter decisions or local trust.

## Challenge checks

- A contract filing is not contract performance or final approval.
- A modeled residential charge is not an observed household bill.
- A proposed credit is not a received credit or adequate affordability relief.
- Public concern is not measured local exposure, and local participation is
  not consent.
- Virginia, Texas, and Georgia terms are not interchangeable; their stages,
  utilities, statutes, and customer classes differ.
- Repeated official summaries and one LinkedIn relay may share the same
  underlying announcement and are not independent replication.
- The affected set includes residential customers, small businesses,
  utilities, large-load firms, host communities, local governments, workers,
  environmental interests, and customers who may benefit from added system
  revenue; no group is treated as a proxy for all others.

## Current answer

**Partly answered.** The governance chain is becoming observable: protections
are approved or proposed, and Georgia contract filings show movement into
facility-linked review. The household-incidence and local-consent endpoints
remain open. The current evidence supports a claim about institutional
implementation, not a claim about realized fairness or acceptance.

## What to look for next

```text
select one Georgia contract route or Virginia tariff area
  -> obtain final docket disposition and contract conditions
  -> match project identity to construction and energization records
  -> collect forecast, metered load, minimum-bill, collateral, tax, and payment data
  -> map utility recovery to customer classes and observed residential bills
  -> align reliability events and environmental/local records by date and place
  -> code local hearings, comments, participation, later decisions, and conflict
  -> compare against an unexposed or differently exposed area where feasible
  -> publish what is observed, modeled, proposed, missing, and disconfirming
```

## Stopping rule

Stop this pass when one matched route has a final disposition, facility
identity, observed load/payment record, customer-class incidence, and a dated
local participation or acceptance/conflict record—or when the primary records
remain unavailable after checking the commission docket, utility filings,
public billing/rate documents, reliability records, and local meeting records.
Record the missing link explicitly and rotate to the next highest-learning
open chain. Do not treat this bounded pass as completion of the ongoing
fourteen-theme research program.
