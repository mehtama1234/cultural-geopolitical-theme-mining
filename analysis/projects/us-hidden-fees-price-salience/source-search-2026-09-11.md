# Source search: US hidden fees and the price people can compare

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States and US online commerce evidence  
**Status:** first matched evidence pass completed; final household cost and enforcement remain open

## Working question

What happens when the price a shopper sees is not the price the shopper pays?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-PRICE-SALIENCE | [Price Salience and Product Choice](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186) | Detailed clickstream data show that hiding price information makes comparison harder and leads consumers to spend more than they otherwise would. | NBER working paper | The study concerns online product choice and does not measure every fee type or the later household budget effect. |
| US-NBER-CARD-FEES | [Do Credit Card Companies Screen for Behavioral Biases?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22360) | NBER finds that card issuers use more back-loaded and hidden fees with some customer groups and when customers are less exposed to negative cash-flow shocks. | NBER working paper | Card-fee patterns are not the same as retail platform fees, and the paper does not show each customer's later loss. |
| US-HBS-PRICE-COHERENCE | [Price Coherence and Consumers](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/its-called-price-coherence-and-its-surprisingly-bad-for-consumers) | HBS reports that a no-fee intermediary model can still produce higher retail prices, unnecessary intermediary use and lower consumer welfare. | HBS Working Knowledge | The setting is intermediary pricing and does not represent every marketplace. |
| US-HBS-PRICE-FORMAT | [Fixing Price Tag Confusion](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/fixing-price-tag-confusion) | HBS explains how partitioned prices can change how shoppers read a service bill, such as a cable bill with several separate charges. | HBS Working Knowledge | The article is a broad account of price display research, not a current estimate of household losses. |

## First pattern to test

```text
low headline price
  -> shopper commits time or attention
  -> fee appears later or is hard to compare
  -> purchase, product quality or search changes
  -> household pays more and trust or regulation becomes an issue
```

The sources support price display as part of the market outcome. They do not show that every shopper pays more, that the extra purchase was unwanted, or that a fee changed the household's wider budget.

## Matched evidence result

The evidence supports a timing effect: the first price can shape attention and commitment before the final price appears. Full-price display changes purchase and quality choices, while card and ticket markets show how later costs can be built into the offer. The next test is the household result after the purchase, not another claim about clicks alone.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Separate fees can make a service easier to price for different users, and some shoppers value the intermediary or the option it provides. The test is whether the full price is clear before commitment and whether competition still works.

## Main gaps

- final price and fee by product and household;
- when the fee becomes visible;
- income, liquidity and search differences;
- whether shoppers switch, cancel or complain;
- seller margins and service value;
- trust, regulation and repeat purchase.

## Decision rule

Measure the full price before calling the first price misleading. If only click behavior changes, record a choice effect and move on without claiming a household financial loss.
