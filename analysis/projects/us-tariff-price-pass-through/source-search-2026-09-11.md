# Source search: US tariffs and the price that arrives later

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

How does a trade policy become a household price, and why can the effect be hard to see at first?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NBER-W34496 | [NBER: Tracking the Short-Run Price Impact of US Tariffs](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34496) | Matching daily prices, country of origin and tariff rates, the authors estimate about 20% retail pass-through for the 2025 tariffs and a roughly 0.7 percentage-point contribution to the all-items CPI by September 2025. Imported goods rose about twice as much as domestic goods in the study. | Working paper using product-level retail data | It measures short-run average effects and cannot show the exact bill faced by each household or the full long-run response. |
| HBS-TARIFFS-2026 | [HBS: Tariffs Leave Consumers and Companies Splitting the Tab](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/tariffs-leave-consumers-and-companies-splitting-the-tab) | HBS reports updated tracker results using daily prices for nearly 360,000 products at five major US retailers; consumers carried an estimated 43% of the tariff burden after seven months, with firms carrying the rest. | Research summary and price tracker | The estimate depends on the tracker, period and tariff changes included; it is not a direct bill for every consumer. |
| NBER-W35064 | [NBER: Tariffs in 2025: Short-Run Impacts on the US Economy](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35064) | The study estimates that 90% of tariffs were passed through to tariff-inclusive prices paid by US importers, while the welfare and trade effects remain uncertain. | Working paper | Importer prices are not retail prices; the paper does not settle whether tariffs create the promised jobs or lower prices. |

## First pattern to test

```text
trade policy
  -> importer cost
  -> retailer margin, sourcing and product choice
  -> shelf price over time
  -> household trading down or buying less
  -> firm, worker and voter response
```

The sources support a partial and delayed path from tariff to retail price. They do not show that every price increase came from the tariff, or that the average effect is the same for every household.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Firms may absorb a cost to protect volume, change suppliers, reduce product choice or spread increases across goods. A tariff can also change production, jobs and bargaining power in ways not captured by the first shelf-price change.

## Main gaps

- price effects by income, product type, retailer and region;
- effects on quantities, quality, package size and product choice;
- the timing of margin changes and supplier changes;
- jobs, wages and investment alongside consumer prices;
- whether voters connect later price changes to the policy that caused them;
- what happens after tariff rollbacks or further changes.

## Decision rule

Pair product-level prices with household purchases, wages and firm margins. Do not treat the tariff rate, the importer cost and the checkout price as the same thing.
