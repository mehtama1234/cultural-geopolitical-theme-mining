# Finding 001: A carbon tariff can shift emissions abroad without being a complete climate policy

**Status:** bounded climate-trade and geopolitical coordination finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in simple words

A carbon tariff is not automatically a strong climate policy. In an NBER
quantitative model, a US carbon tariff by itself raises US emissions while
lowering foreign emissions. The combined policy—a carbon tariff plus a domestic
carbon tax—lowers emissions in both places and achieves a larger global result.

The model says the tariff-only approach achieves about half the global emissions
reductions of the combined approach. Both approaches raise modeled US GDP and
welfare, but the combined policy has the larger effect. In tariff-only
multilateral agreements, adding more countries does not produce a steadily
larger emissions reduction.

The route is:

```text
climate goal
  -> tariff, domestic tax, or both
  -> trade prices and production location
  -> US emissions and foreign emissions
  -> GDP and modeled welfare
  -> partner coordination
  -> [open] real implementation, household incidence, retaliation, legitimacy
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a geopolitical version of the program's “capacity versus consequence”
problem. An instrument can create leverage over foreign production and still
produce an incomplete domestic climate result. The policy label does not tell
us which country bears the emissions change or whether the global objective is
met.

It also shows why coordination is not just a bigger version of one country's
action. Under the model, more tariff-only participants do not automatically
produce more global reductions.

## What it does not show

- These are model results, not observed tariff or emissions outcomes.
- The paper does not measure household bills, firm relocation, employment, or
  consumer prices.
- It does not establish foreign-government acceptance, retaliation, or treaty
  durability.
- Modeled US GDP and welfare are not the same as equal household benefit.
- It does not prove that a domestic carbon tax is politically feasible.

## Next decisive test

Compare an implemented policy with observed trade, sector emissions, firm
adaptation, household prices, partner response, and public acceptance. Keep
domestic climate results, foreign effects, and geopolitical leverage separate.
