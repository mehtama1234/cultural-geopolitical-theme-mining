# Source search: US carbon tariffs, climate policy, and geopolitical coordination

**Search date:** 2026-09-22  
**Geography:** United States and international trade partners  
**Status:** bounded pass complete; model separates domestic emissions, foreign emissions, welfare, and multilateral coordination, real policy effects remain open

## Working question

Can a US carbon tariff reduce global emissions by itself, or does it need domestic policy and international coordination?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-CARBON-TARIFFS | [Are carbon tariffs climate policy?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35307) | A quantitative trade model finds that a US carbon tariff alone raises US emissions and lowers foreign emissions, achieving about half the global emissions reduction of a combined tariff-and-domestic-carbon-tax policy. Both policies raise modeled US GDP and welfare; tariff-only multilateral agreements have modest, non-monotonic global effects. | NBER working paper | This is a model, not observed tariff, emissions, firm, household, or foreign-government behavior. |

## First pattern

```text
domestic climate goal
  -> carbon tariff, domestic carbon tax, or both
  -> trade prices and production location
  -> US emissions, foreign emissions, GDP, and welfare
  -> partner participation and coordination
  -> [open] real implementation, retaliation, household incidence, and legitimacy
```

The source adds a geopolitical-policy boundary: a measure can lower foreign
emissions while raising domestic emissions, so “climate policy” cannot be
judged from the instrument name alone.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

A tariff can improve modeled US terms of trade and welfare even while being a
weaker climate instrument than a combined policy. More participating countries
do not automatically produce more reductions under tariff-only agreements.
Model welfare is not a household bill, firm response, or foreign acceptance.

## Decision rule

Keep modeled emissions, domestic welfare, trade coordination, foreign response,
household incidence, and real implementation separate. Reopen this lane only
with observed policy, trade, emissions, or partner-response data.
