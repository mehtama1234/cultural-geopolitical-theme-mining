# Legal help to eviction, household stability, and child outcomes

**Status:** fifty-sixth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** housing-court representation, eviction, household mobility,
homelessness, school continuity, and longer-run housing security

## What we found

Two NBER-linked studies fill different parts of the same housing chain.

First, New York City's Universal Access to Counsel program offered free
housing-court representation to tenants at or below 200% of the federal
poverty guideline. The NBER study uses the program rollout and address-level
housing-court data from 2016-2019. Tenants who gained lawyers were less likely
to receive possessory judgments, smaller money judgments, eviction warrants,
and completed evictions; effects were larger in poorer places and places with
more noncitizens.

Second, the Census CES/NBER eviction study links 774,464 baseline eviction
cases across Cook County and New York to school, homelessness-service, and
restricted Census records. Using quasi-random judge assignment, it estimates
that eviction increased residential moves by 13 percentage points in the case
year, increased homelessness by 7.0 points in Cook County and 3.1 points in
New York in the following year, and increased absences and school changes.
Test-score effects were small or null in the reported elementary and middle
school measures, while high-school graduation fell in the linked study
population.

These findings do not mean that legal representation automatically creates
long-term housing stability. The first study stops mainly at court outcomes;
the second identifies downstream effects of eviction orders in two cities.
The two studies are complementary, not a same-tenant longitudinal join.

## What this connects to

| Link | Evidence | What does not transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Legal representation -> court protection | The Universal Access study reports fewer possessory judgments, warrants, and completed evictions for tenants who gained lawyers. | Immediate court protection is not rent affordability, debt resolution, or later housing stability. |
| Eviction order -> residential and school disruption | The judge-assignment study estimates more moves, homelessness, absences, and school changes after eviction. | These effects are local to marginal cases in Chicago and New York; they are not a national estimate or proof for informal displacement. |
| Protection -> child stability | The two studies suggest a plausible policy bridge: preventing or changing the court event may avoid some downstream disruption. | The studies do not directly estimate the child or household outcome caused by receiving counsel. |
| Unequal exposure -> unequal protection | Larger legal-help effects appear in poorer places and places with more noncitizens; the eviction study reports differences by age, sex, and family-support context. | Subgroup differences do not identify the mechanism, lawyer quality, language access, or who could reach counsel before a hearing. |

## The deeper question

**When does legal representation prevent a housing-court event from becoming a
lasting household and child disruption, and when does it only delay or reshape
the court stage?**

### Why ask it

This question changes how housing policy is judged. A court intervention may
be highly valuable even if it does not remove the rent arrears or income shock.
But a smaller judgment or avoided warrant should not be called a complete
housing solution unless the household can remain safely housed, keep children
in school, resolve debt, and avoid repeat crisis.

Priority is **high** because decision value, missing-link value, cross-theme
connection, and disconfirmation value are high: the route joins housing,
household room, time, children, public systems, legal voice, and inequality.
Effort is bounded to one city or program; scope risk is controlled by retaining
the two studies as separate designs.

## What we know so far

- Legal representation can improve immediate housing-court outcomes.
- Eviction orders can cause measurable residential and school disruption in the
  studied urban settings.
- The strongest evidence does not yet join counsel receipt to later housing,
  child, work, health, debt, or repeat-court outcomes for the same household.
- A court win can coexist with arrears, informal moves, repayment burden, or a
  later crisis.
- The people most exposed to harm may also face language, disability,
  immigration-status, transportation, or information barriers before court.

## Bounded public discussion

The [Yale Tobin Center discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0046-eviction-children-linkedin-001.json)
circulates the eviction study as a child-welfare finding and connects formal
housing displacement to educational and developmental disruption. It is a
research announcement, not an independent sample or full estimate. It helps
show how the research is interpreted publicly, but it does not prove that legal
representation produced the downstream child effect.

## What could change the answer

- Counsel may prevent an eviction order but not resolve unaffordable rent or
  income loss, producing later instability.
- A court judgment may be avoided through an informal move that is not visible
  in court records.
- Family support, shelter access, school mobility, landlord behavior, and
  local housing supply may buffer or amplify the child effects.
- Legal representation may be most effective when paired with rental aid,
  payment plans, repairs, benefits, or emergency housing—not as a stand-alone
  service.
- A smaller money judgment may still be unaffordable, and a completed move may
  be safer for some households than remaining in unsafe housing.

## Challenge checks

- Court activity was not treated as housing recovery: judgments, warrants,
  completed eviction, safe housing, and durable stability remain separate.
- Correlation was not treated as cause: the eviction study uses judge
  assignment, but the legal-help study's reported program effects do not create
  a direct counsel-to-child causal estimate here.
- Scope and time were checked: New York 2016-2019 court rollout differs from
  Chicago/New York linked records spanning roughly 2000-2019.
- Sources were not double-counted: both papers are related NBER/Census research
  families, not independent population replications.
- Affected groups were retained: children, single mothers, doubled-up
  households, noncitizens, poorer neighborhoods, landlords, schools, lawyers,
  and people displaced informally or deterred before filing.
- The public post was treated as interpretation, not evidence.

## Current answer

**Partly answered.** The evidence supports two separate claims: legal help can
improve immediate court outcomes, and eviction orders can disrupt housing and
school continuity. It does not yet prove that legal help prevents the full
downstream cascade or produces durable household recovery.

## What to look for next

Follow the same household or a valid linked program cohort through:

```text
rent shock or housing problem
  -> notice, counsel access, and alternative/support
  -> court participation and decision
  -> payment, arrears, safe housing, or move
  -> school continuity, work/health effects, and repeat crisis
  -> later stability, trust, appeal, or exit
```

Record counsel eligibility and actual receipt, hearing timing, rent or debt,
payment plan or assistance, housing destination, school changes, and people
who were deterred or moved informally. Stop if the source ends at the court
judgment; report a missing-outcome boundary rather than a complete housing
solution.

## Stopping rule

Do not call housing-court representation a complete housing intervention until
a same-household or valid linked cohort observes counsel access, court outcome,
housing destination or stability, child or household consequences, and later
repeat crisis or recovery with compatible denominators and a credible
counterexample.
