# Finding 001: Workers may price security into mobility decisions

**Status:** bounded U.S. survey and modeled labor-market finding · **Checked:** 2026-10-18

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35580 elicits employed U.S. workers' trade-off between pay
and unemployment risk. On average, workers demand a 1.63% pay raise for each
additional percentage point of annual unemployment risk in a new job. The paper
then embeds that trade-off in a general-equilibrium search model. A complete-
markets counterfactual raises modeled job mobility by 12% and productivity by
0.19%; unemployment insurance raises modeled productivity by 1.3% by helping
employed workers accept riskier, higher-productivity offers, while also raising
job loss and unemployment in the model.

```text
job offer
  -> wage, layoff risk, benefits, location, and household buffer
  -> willingness to switch or stay
  -> mobility, productivity, vacancy filling, and job loss
  -> [open] realized household security, bargaining, identity, and political response
```

## Household-expectations counterpart

The [New York Fed's August 2026 Survey of Consumer Expectations](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260908)
adds a separate household-head panel of approximately 1,300 respondents. The
mean perceived probability that the national unemployment rate would be higher
in one year was **44.4%**; the mean probability of finding a job if the
respondent lost their current job was **45.4%**; and the mean perceived
probability of losing one's own job within 12 months was **13.8%**.

The three measures should not be averaged into a security index. They show a
useful split between macro labor-market worry, perceived outside option, and
personal job-loss risk. The New York Fed also reports that expected one-year
spending growth was 5.2% while expected income growth was 3.0%, and that the
mean perceived probability of missing a minimum debt payment in the next three
months was 13.2%. These are expectations, not realized layoffs, job finding,
spending, income, or delinquency; they show why job security can shape
household room before an observed employment transition.

## Bounded public discussion

The [labor-market interpretation discussion](../../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0041-employment-linkedin-001.json)
argues that a single payroll or unemployment headline can hide participation,
hours, wages, and durable-demand conditions. It helps frame the measurement
problem, but it is professional commentary rather than a representative worker
sample or a causal test.

## What this adds to the atlas

This makes “choice” more concrete: a worker may reject a higher-paying job not
because the wage is unimportant, but because the household cannot insure the
risk. It also reframes unemployment insurance as an employed-worker mobility
instrument, not only a post-layoff transfer. The result links practical room,
work control, public insurance, and firm productivity while preserving the
trade-off between dynamism and security.

## Limits and next test

- The 1.63% figure is a stated survey trade-off, not a realized switching rate.
- The mobility, productivity, and UI results are model estimates, not observed
  administrative outcomes.
- The abstract does not provide the respondent denominator or full subgroup
  estimates.
- Job security also includes benefits, schedule, location, health, identity,
  and advancement, not only annual unemployment risk.
- The New York Fed probabilities are subjective household expectations and do
  not establish realized job loss, re-employment, income, spending, or debt
  payment outcomes.

The next decisive test is a matched worker-job-offer panel joining risk,
compensation, benefits, household liquidity, UI eligibility, applications,
acceptance/rejection, realized layoff, mobility, wages, hours, productivity,
and later household security. The New York Fed panel supplies expectation
context, not the same-person realized transition.
