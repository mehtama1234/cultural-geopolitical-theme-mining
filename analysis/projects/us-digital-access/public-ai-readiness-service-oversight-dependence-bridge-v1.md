# Public AI readiness, service outcomes, oversight, and dependence bridge

**Status:** thirty-third active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** government AI readiness, public-service evaluation, cloud procurement, human oversight, disability access, continuity, and public trust

## Question

When governments adopt AI and cloud services, what turns model access into a
reliable public service that people can understand and challenge—and what turns
it instead into fragile, vendor-dependent infrastructure?

```text
public need / service bottleneck
  -> data, infrastructure, staff, and procurement readiness
  -> AI deployment and local adaptation
  -> service speed, accuracy, access, and continuity
  -> human review, appeal, correction, and public reporting
  -> trust, replaceability, equitable capability, or dependence
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage or link | Comparable observation | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Government AI adoption is broad but shallow | A World Bank survey across 60 economies finds most governments report some AI use, but adoption is often ad hoc and rarely institutionalized; 44% of internal use consists of individual public servants using AI for tasks such as search or summarization. | Survey of central digital agencies, sector ministries, and management-information leads. | Reported use is not audited system performance, citizen outcome, or causal productivity. |
| Readiness barriers differ by institutional context | Lack of data and budgets are common barriers across income groups; lower-income economies more often report missing policies, while higher-income economies more often emphasize privacy and ethics. | Cross-economy comparative survey and institutional-readiness typology. | Income patterns do not prove country-level causality or permanent archetypes. |
| Evaluation capacity is thin where stakes can be high | In a World Bank review of about 10,000 AI-based health-intervention studies, only 57 tested with real patients and providers in low-resource settings and 17 measured causal effects on health, care quality, or efficiency; 80% of surveyed governments in low and lower-middle-income economies reported no or only basic AI-evaluation mechanisms. | Evidence review plus government-capacity survey. | The study universe and country coverage are source-specific; counts do not estimate all public AI or service quality. |
| Context failure can appear after deployment | A Thailand diabetic-retinopathy screening tool rejected more than 20% of nurse-taken images when clinic lighting and image quality differed from training conditions. | Reported implementation example in public-service scaling analysis. | One case does not establish prevalence, harm, or general model failure; rejection is not the same as a wrong clinical decision. |
| Procurement determines whether capability is durable | The World Bank cloud-procurement guide treats cloud as a critical path for AI, data analytics, e-government, and disaster recovery; it identifies direct, indirect, and framework/marketplace routes and calls for evaluation beyond price. | Implementation and procurement guidance. | Recommendations are not evidence that centralization, frameworks, or multi-cloud improve live services. |
| Lock-in is a public-capability risk | The same guide identifies data portability, interoperability, non-proprietary services, multi-cloud options, exit plans, service-level requirements, incident response, and continuity as controls against vendor dependence. | Governance and risk framework. | A risk pathway is not an observed lock-in event, cost, outage, or citizen outcome. |
| Citizens want both AI capability and human control | A BCG survey of 48,500 respondents across 44 countries reports 64% weekly AI use, a 26-point increase over two years, and nearly two-thirds wanting human oversight embedded in digital services; 60% of intermediate-and-above users were comfortable letting agents act for them. | Global citizen survey and public-summary analysis. | The summary is not administrative service data, an observed appeal rate, or a causal deployment evaluation. |
| A named agency route has measurable targets but open outcomes | SSA planned 4 million automated calls in each of FY2025 and FY2026, AI-assisted IVR/chatbot use, and machine-readable disability evidence; FY2026 targets were 220 days for initial disability processing and 97% decisional accuracy. | Agency plan and oversight materials. | Targets are not realized claimant outcomes; public materials do not link AI exposure to explanation, appeal, correction, payment, or later health/work result. |
| Oversight infrastructure exists but is not yet a closed remedy chain | SSA OIG began an AI-use audit in March 2026 for planned FY2027 Q2 completion; denial-review systems define cases reviewed, substantive errors, and reversed decisions, but public materials do not connect these to claimant-level AI exposure, correction, or benefit receipt. | Agency/OIG status and public denial-review metadata. | Oversight status is not an audit finding or verified claimant remedy. |

## Cross-source finding

The central pattern is a **readiness-to-remedy gap**. Government AI becomes
public capability only when data, staff, procurement, model evaluation, local
adaptation, service continuity, human review, and correction operate together.
More model access can increase speed or scale while leaving people unable to
understand, challenge, or leave a bad decision.

The evidence requires six separate gates:

1. **Foundation:** records, data quality, connectivity, electricity, staff, and
   local language or institutional fit.
2. **Deployment:** named system, task, population, vendor, and human role.
3. **Performance:** speed, accuracy, rejection, reliability, accessibility, and
   subgroup outcomes in real operating conditions.
4. **Contestability:** explanation, human fallback, appeal, correction, and
   escalation.
5. **Continuity:** incident response, maintenance, portability, alternative
   vendors, and exit cost.
6. **Legitimacy:** whether people experience a usable service and a visible
   remedy, not merely an announced AI capability.

The strongest counter-signal is that people can be open to delegated AI and
still demand human oversight. Likewise, a system can meet a speed or accuracy
target while producing inaccessible explanations or unequal correction routes.

## Bounded public discussion

A UNCTAD relay links digitally deliverable services to infrastructure and
unequal country capability. A professional grid discussion argues that AI
data-center demand may outrun power, transformers, transmission, and labor. A
Milken Institute panel connects AI infrastructure finance to generation, rate
design, affordability, insurance, regulation, and public acceptance.

These discussions broaden the chain from software to physical and fiscal
infrastructure. They are institutional relays or curated panels, not household
bill data, service logs, or evidence of deployment outcomes.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- Ad hoc AI use can be a low-risk productivity aid, not institutional failure;
  formal governance can also create paperwork without safer decisions.
- More evaluation capacity does not guarantee better models if data, staff, or
  local adaptation remain weak.
- Cloud centralization can improve continuity and bargaining power while also
  increasing concentration; multi-cloud can reduce lock-in while increasing
  complexity and cost.
- A human-review requirement can preserve accountability or become a nominal
  rubber stamp if staff lack time, authority, or information.
- High citizen comfort with agents may reflect successful private-sector use and
  not willingness to delegate eligibility, disability, or health decisions.
- Faster processing can be a benefit for some claimants while increasing the
  speed or scale of erroneous denial for others.

## Stronger next test

Build a named public-service AI episode ledger that records:

1. service need, claimant characteristics, alternative access, and baseline
   queue or error rate;
2. system version, vendor, data source, model task, human role, and deployment
   date;
3. processing time, accuracy, rejection, accessibility, subgroup performance,
   outage, and continuity measures;
4. explanation, human review, appeal, correction, payment or service receipt,
   and time to remedy; and
5. later health, work, income, trust, switching, vendor exit, and public
   reporting outcomes.

Compare an AI-assisted route with a human-only or alternative digital route,
retain denied and corrected cases, and record the cost and feasibility of
switching vendors or restoring a non-AI route.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The corpus shows that public AI value depends on data,
staff, procurement, evaluation, local fit, human oversight, portability, and
correction—not model access alone. It does not yet follow one claimant from AI
exposure through a verified decision, appeal, remedy, service receipt, and
durable trust or exit.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call public AI capable, safe, equitable, or legitimate from adoption,
planned targets, citizen enthusiasm, or governance frameworks alone. Require a
named live service with real operating outcomes, human contestability, verified
correction, subgroup comparison, continuity, and an alternative or exit test.
