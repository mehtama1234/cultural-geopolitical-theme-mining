# Finding 001: Military service can correlate with immigrant integration without causing it

**Status:** bounded Census CES and American Political Science Review selection-and-integration finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

Census CES Working Paper 26-35 studies about 28,500 first-generation immigrant
men born from 1949 through 1952. Because Vietnam draft numbers were assigned by
birth date, the authors use draft risk to separate military service from the
kind of person who chooses to serve.

Veterans looked more integrated in ordinary comparisons: they were more likely
to be naturalized, use English, live outside immigrant enclaves, and marry across
national-origin lines. But those differences largely disappeared in the
lottery-based causal estimates. The study did find a college-completion benefit,
especially for non-Western immigrants.

```text
prior identity, resources, and willingness to serve
  -> military service or conscription
  -> education and other institutional experience
  -> later citizenship, language, residence, family, and civic outcomes
  -> [open] belonging, equal treatment, trust, and political action
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a useful cultural counterexample to the simple story that a common
institution automatically creates a common national identity. The institution
may provide a real resource—here, education—without producing every form of
social integration. And a strong correlation between service and belonging may
come from selection before the institution acts.

The finding also warns against reading veteran status as a single cultural
identity. Service, citizenship, language, neighborhood, marriage, education,
and political belonging can move on different tracks.

## Limits and next test

- The study concerns Vietnam-era draft-lottery compliers, not all immigrants, veterans, women, later cohorts, or voluntary enlistees.
- Outcomes come from the 2000 decennial census, not a continuous panel of service and life history.
- Military service was not randomly assigned to everyone; the lottery identifies a local effect for people whose service responded to draft risk.
- The paper does not measure unit relationships, discrimination, veteran health, wages, civic participation, or political trust directly.
- A null effect on the measured integration outcomes does not prove that no individual or subgroup experienced meaningful integration.

The next decisive test is a protected cohort or service record joining pre-service
identity and resources, service pathway, unit contact, discrimination, education
and citizenship benefits, health, work, family, civic participation, and later
trust or political action.
