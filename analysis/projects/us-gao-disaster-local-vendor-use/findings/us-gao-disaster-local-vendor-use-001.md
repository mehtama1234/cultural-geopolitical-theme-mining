# Finding 001: Disaster recovery money can enter a place without agencies knowing whether local firms received the work

**Status:** bounded disaster-recovery, firm, and public-systems finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO reviewed FEMA and Army Corps contracting after Hurricane Helene, the Maui
wildfires, and Hurricane Ian. Federal law and agency policies favor contracting
with businesses that reside or primarily do business in a declared disaster
area when feasible and practicable, partly to help restart the local economy.

But both agencies lacked reliable monitoring of local-vendor use. In FEMA's
data for the three disasters, about 3%—$85 million of $2.9 billion in contract
obligations—was identified as awarded to local vendors. GAO found that FEMA did
not monitor whether the local-vendor field was accurate or whether advance
contracts transitioned to local vendors within six months.

GAO's selected review also found process problems: four of 13 contracting
officers who awarded contracts to local vendors identified the local disaster
area incorrectly, and all selected Army Corps contracts lacked documents
related to local-vendor use. The sample was 40 contracts: 25 FEMA contracts and
15 Corps contracts, selected for review rather than designed as a national
estimate.

The practical route is:

```text
disaster and federal response money
  -> local-vendor rule and contracting process
  -> award, performance, payment, and local firm capacity [partly observed]
  -> local jobs, business survival, repair, and community recovery [open]
  -> household housing, income, health, and security [open]
```

The core pattern is a place-level version of “visible capacity versus usable
benefit.” The federal government can spend money for recovery while its records
do not show whether the affected place's firms, workers, and households gained
the intended recovery path.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This finding connects disaster infrastructure to firm and local economic power.
The question is not only whether debris is removed or temporary housing is
provided. It is also whether the recovery process builds local capability,
keeps revenue in the affected area, and gives local firms a realistic chance to
participate.

It also preserves a necessary counterargument. Disaster areas may not have
enough firms, equipment, workers, or functioning supply chains to meet urgent
needs. A nonlocal contractor can therefore be the fastest or only usable route.
The data problem matters because without monitoring, agencies cannot tell the
difference between necessary outside capacity, missed local opportunity, and
bad classification.

## Limits and next test

- The 3% FEMA figure is for three selected disasters and the recorded field,
  not a national local-vendor rate or a measure of contract quality.
- The 40-contract review sample is nongeneralizable and intentionally mixes
  local and nonlocal awards.
- Contract award does not establish work completion, payment receipt, local
  jobs, wages, business survival, or community repair.
- The report does not measure why local vendors did or did not bid, whether
  advance contracts displaced later local awards, or whether local firms were
  harmed by the preference rule.
- Household recovery, housing stability, health, and political response are
  not observed.

The next decisive evidence would join disaster contract records to vendor
location, subcontracting, payment, worker and business outcomes, project
completion, repair quality, and household recovery—while retaining the declared
area and local-capacity definition. Until then, the safe claim is that local
economic recovery is an intended part of disaster contracting but remains
poorly measured in the agencies' own records.
