# Uber / Warraich unfair-deactivation record v1

**Status:** primary Fair Work Commission merits decision; deactivation held
unfair; reactivation ordered; lost-pay issue left open
**Checked:** 2026-09-15

## Why this record matters

This case separates nominal human involvement from meaningful review. Uber
described internal Community Operations representatives and an assessment of
the worker's response, but the Commission found the process non-compliant and
unfair because the warnings lacked usable detail, the evidentiary basis was
poor, and the worker was not given a genuine chance to answer the allegations.

It is a useful contrast with Kumar: both involve Uber and the Australian
Digital Labour Platform Deactivation Code, but Kumar's rating-based process
was upheld while Warraich's complaint-based process failed.

## Source and scope

In *Sajid Saleem Warraich v Rasier Pacific Pty Ltd trading as Uber* ([2025]
FWC 3338; UDE2025/108), the Commission found that the Code applied and that
Warraich was protected from unfair deactivation. He had worked through Uber
since around 10 March 2021 and completed approximately 7,000 trips.

Uber relied on five rider complaints received between December 2022 and May
2025. The worker did not receive copies or substantive details of the
complaints before deactivation. Uber suspended access on 24 May 2025, issued
a preliminary deactivation notice on 30 May, and sent the final deactivation
notice on 7 June 2025.

## Evidence matrix

| Stage | Primary finding | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Decision surface | Repeated rider complaints alleging aggression, discriminatory comments, and refusal of service | The decision concerns this complaint sequence, not all Uber safety or discrimination cases |
| Warning | The 24 May message did not state that deactivation was at risk, did not explain how the issue could be remedied, and did not advise that support could be sought | The Code does not require disclosure of identifying information where that creates a safety risk, but the Commission rejected withholding detail as a blanket practice |
| Worker response | Warraich repeatedly denied wrongdoing and requested discussion; Uber did not provide the underlying complaint particulars | The record does not show that he could meaningfully test each allegation before the final decision |
| Human review | Uber relied on unnamed representatives and a statement that no further reasonable inquiries were warranted | The Commission found the evidence of the process and decision-makers insufficiently detailed; “human” labeling did not establish meaningful or independent review |
| Evidence quality | The Commission gave Warraich's first-hand evidence substantial weight and Uber's witness evidence little to no weight; Uber supplied little beyond complaint copies | This is an adjudicated assessment of the evidence in this case, not a finding that complaints are generally unreliable |
| Outcome | Deactivation was inconsistent with the Code and unfair; reactivation was ordered | The Commission directed the parties to confer about lost remuneration; the amount and payment are not established in this record |

## Remedy chain

```text
five complaint-based allegations
  -> temporary suspension and generic warning
  -> worker response without usable particulars
  -> nominal internal review with unclear inquiry and authority
  -> Code non-compliance and no valid reason established
  -> unfair deactivation finding
  -> reactivation ordered
  -> lost-pay issue deferred; amount and receipt not observed
```

The case demonstrates that explanation is not satisfied by naming a broad
category such as “discriminatory behaviour.” A worker must receive enough
information to understand the matter and respond to it, subject to a genuine
safety or security limitation. It also demonstrates that a platform's
description of a human review is evidence to be tested, not an outcome code.

## Comparative interpretation

```text
Bandameeda: process failure + conflicting rules/evidence -> reactivation + $12,126.31
Warraich:   vague warning + weak evidence + inadequate inquiry -> reactivation; pay open
Kumar:      compliant notice + sufficient human review + reasonable threshold -> dismissal
Bakar:      eligibility finding -> settlement/revocation before merits
```

The emerging Australian pattern is stage-dependent: some cases fail before
merits, some produce restoration where process and evidence fail, and at least
one produces a reasoned dismissal where the platform's rule and review were
accepted.

This record establishes the Commission's findings and orders in one case. It
