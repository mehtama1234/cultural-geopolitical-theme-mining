# Source record: SAG-AFTRA digital-replica protections

**Checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Source family:** worker organization / collective bargaining record  
**Use:** direct check of the ILO’s SAG-AFTRA case summary

## Sources

1. [SAG-AFTRA 2023 TV/Theatrical Contracts](https://www.sagaftra.org/contracts-industry-resources/contracts/2023-tvtheatrical-contracts), accessed 2026-09-11.
2. [SAG-AFTRA Digital Replicas 101](https://www.sagaftra.org/sites/default/files/sa_documents/DigitalReplicas.pdf), accessed 2026-09-11.
3. [SAG-AFTRA 2023 TV/Theatrical Summary Agreement](https://www.sagaftra.org/files/sa_documents/TV-Theatrical_23_Summary_Agreement_Final.pdf), accessed 2026-09-11.

These are union-published contract explanations and a summary agreement. They are direct evidence of the negotiated rules and the union’s interpretation of them. They are not independent evidence of compliance, producer behavior, or performer outcomes.

## What the record says

- The 2023 TV/Theatrical agreement covers digital replicas and digital alterations.
- A producer must obtain consent before creating a replica of a principal performer or background actor, with specific notice and documentation requirements.
- The union’s explainer says the performer must receive information about the intended use and, in most cases, compensation for use.
- The summary agreement distinguishes employment-based replicas, independently created replicas, background-actor replicas, and synthetic performers.
- For synthetic performers not based on a recognizable natural performer, the agreement provides notice to the union and an opportunity to bargain; the record is therefore not the same as a blanket veto over all synthetic use.

## Mechanism relevance

The record turns a broad claim about “worker voice” into observable rule types:

```text
collective bargaining -> notice and consent -> bounded use -> compensation or bargaining
```

The record also shows a limit: consent can improve control over a person’s replica without preventing every form of synthetic substitution. The remaining question is whether performers can use these rights in practice when refusing consent may affect future hiring.
