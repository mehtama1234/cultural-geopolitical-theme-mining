# Platform-work courts: status recognition before algorithmic remedy

**Status:** cross-country court comparator  
**Checked:** 2026-09-15  
**Cases:** Netherlands Deliveroo (2023) and Finland Wolt (2025–2026)  
**Unit:** national court and administrative record

## The comparative question

Before Directive (EU) 2024/2831 is transposed, national courts are already
being asked to decide whether platform workers are genuinely independent or
are working under platform direction and control. These cases reveal an
important distinction: a court can recognize dependency and employment status
without providing the newer directive-style rights to algorithmic explanation,
human review, correction, or compensation.

## Case map

| Dimension | Netherlands: Deliveroo | Finland: Wolt |
|---|---|---|
| Final decision | Hoge Raad, 24 March 2023, ECLI:NL:HR:2023:443 | Supreme Administrative Court, KHO:2025:41, 22 May 2025; reopening rejected 19 March 2026 |
| Legal route | Civil employment-status dispute brought by FNV | Administrative labour-supervision and employment-status route |
| Platform mechanism in record | Deliveroo's digital platform and algorithm “Frank” organized delivery work | Wolt platform data supported direction, supervision, quality monitoring, fee-reduction and termination powers |
| Status result | Couriers treated as employees under the circumstances of the relationship | Couriers treated as employees under Finnish employment law |
| Flexibility boundary | Freedom to choose work and substitution did not decide the case | Working-time autonomy remained sufficient to exclude working-time legislation |
| Downstream consequence | Status judgment; no directive-style automated remedy record | Tax Administration changed wage-income and employer-obligation treatment from 2027 |
| New directive applied? | No | No |

## Shared mechanism

```text
platform organizes and monitors work
  -> contractual label conflicts with practical dependency
  -> court evaluates the relationship as a whole
  -> employment status is recognized
  -> statutory consequences attach
```

Both cases reject a simple “flexibility equals independence” reading. A worker
may choose when to log in or have some substitution capacity while the platform
still organizes, monitors, prices, or controls the work in ways that matter to
legal status.

## Important difference: status is not the whole remedy

The Finnish record makes the boundary especially clear. The court recognized
employment status and the Tax Administration attached wage and employer
obligations, yet the court also held that working-time legislation did not
apply because of working-time autonomy. Legal recognition can therefore open
some protections while leaving other dimensions unresolved.

Neither case shows a worker obtaining:

- a copy of the data or ranking used in an individual decision;
- an explanation of an automated allocation, rating, or cancellation;
- human review within a defined deadline;
- correction of a platform record or account decision;
- compensation for an uncorrectable automated decision; or
- protection from retaliation after exercising those rights.

Those are the missing outcome arrows that the EU directive and Dutch draft
attempt to make more explicit.

## Implication for the national implementation atlas

The court cases should not be coded as proof that the Netherlands or Finland
has implemented Directive (EU) 2024/2831. They should be coded as **pre-directive
institutional capacity**: national legal systems can sometimes translate
platform control into status and administrative consequences before the new
directive architecture is operational.

This creates a more precise comparative test:

```text
pre-existing status law and court capacity
  + directive transposition
  -> new data, explanation, review, correction, and anti-retaliation routes
  -> whether more workers can obtain a remedy without a collective status case
```

The key question is not only whether the directive changes the legal result. It
is whether it changes who can invoke the system, what evidence platforms must
provide, how quickly a decision can be reviewed, and whether the remedy reaches
the worker before lost work or income becomes irreversible.

## Relation to AIM-WORK

The cases provide institution-level examples related to task allocation,
performance monitoring, work organization, and contractual status. AIM-WORK
provides worker-level cross-country associations for automated direction and
evaluation. They cannot be pooled as a causal estimate because the units,
populations, periods, and exposures differ.

The valid bridge is a hypothesis:

```text
where platform direction is legally recognized
  -> status and control rights may become more accessible
  -> worker discretion, stress, breaks, and exit options may differ
  -> matched country/sector/workplace evidence is still required
```

## New remedy comparator

The [Uber/Ola automated-decision remedy case](uber-ola-automated-decision-remedy-case-record-v1.md)
adds a pre-Directive court outcome involving the missing middle directly. The
Amsterdam Court of Appeal required information about factors behind account
deactivation, trip allocation, fares, and internal scores, allowing drivers to
exercise GDPR rights such as rectification. It records an information remedy,
not completed correction, compensation, reinstatement, or anti-retaliation.

The comparative pathway is now:

```text
Deliveroo/Wolt: status and control recognition
Uber/Ola: information remedy for automated decisions
Directive/national drafts: prospective review, correction, and compensation rules
post-Directive case: still required to test exercised implementation
```

## Decisive next test

Find an official post-directive or transposition-period case involving a
specific automated decision—account restriction, ranking, task allocation,
payment refusal, or deactivation—and code whether the person obtained
explanation, review, correction, compensation, or protection from adverse
treatment. That would test the new remedy architecture directly.

## Boundary

This is a comparative research synthesis, not a legal opinion. It does not
generalize either decision to all platform workers and does not claim that
status recognition itself improved autonomy, stress, earnings, health, or
political trust.
