# GAO 2026 critical-minerals substitution, recycling, and dependence layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Sources:** US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-108687; U.S. Geological Survey, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026
**Scope:** US battery and semiconductor supply chains; selected critical minerals
**Status:** technology and supply-chain capability finding; not a forecast of strategic independence

## Why this update matters

The atlas already tracks AI infrastructure, energy load, defense production,
trade, and state leverage. This layer adds the material-input constraint below
those systems. A country can announce domestic factories or strategic
technology while still depending on imported minerals whose processing,
refining, recycling, or substitution is controlled elsewhere.

The important question is not simply whether the United States imports a
mineral. It is whether a credible alternative exists at the required purity,
performance, price, volume, and time—and whether the US controls the collection,
processing, manufacturing, and recovery systems that make that alternative
usable.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Critical-material scope | The US considers 60 minerals critical because they are important to the economy or security and have supply chains vulnerable to disruption | A critical designation is not a forecast of shortage or a measure of current economic harm |
| Import reliance | USGS reports 2025 net import reliance of 100% for selected materials including arsenic, gallium, graphite, indium, and manganese; lithium, cobalt, copper, nickel, and other materials remain materially import-reliant | Net import reliance is an apparent-consumption measure, not a complete resilience or strategic-dependence score |
| China exposure | USGS reports China as a major source for 14 of the 33 critical minerals on which the US most depends; China restricts exports of 14 minerals covered in the report’s trade discussion | Source concentration is not the same as a cutoff, coercion, or realized geopolitical concession |
| Economic reach | USGS reports mineral-reliant industries represented about $4.09 trillion in value in 2025; US mineral production value rose 5.6% to $112 billion | Industry value does not show which firms, workers, consumers, or regions bear supply risk |
| Recycling horizon | GAO finds battery recycling could reduce import reliance on some minerals in about 2–3 years | This is a technology-assessment possibility, not realized capacity, output, or price parity |
| Semiconductor constraint | GAO finds substitution and recycling may not reduce short-term semiconductor import reliance; substitute materials must meet demanding performance requirements | “May not” is a near-term technical boundary, not proof that future substitution will fail |
| Technology maturity | Research could improve battery energy density, semiconductor performance, or recovery rates, but GAO notes research, development, and testing may not produce mature technologies in the near term | R&D potential is not a commercial process or domestic supply |
| System bottleneck | Recycling requires collection and processing infrastructure and consistent feedstock; substitution requires materials research and manufacturing capability | A policy option or facility announcement is not an operating loop |

## The central tension

Strategic dependence moves through a chain with different clocks:

```text
mineral geology and foreign processing
  -> imported input, price, quality, and shipment risk
  -> battery, chip, defense, grid, or consumer-electronics production
  -> firm substitution, inventory, redesign, recycling, or delay
  -> domestic cost, capacity, jobs, and technology availability
  -> state bargaining room, alliance dependence, or external leverage
```

The same mineral can be replaceable in one application and irreplaceable in
another. A battery chemistry may substitute for cobalt or nickel more readily
than a semiconductor can substitute for gallium, germanium, arsenic, or indium
without sacrificing performance. The correct unit is therefore the
mineral-application-process, not the mineral alone.

Recycling also has a timing problem. A future stock of used batteries or
electronics may contain recoverable material, but collection, sorting,
transport, processing yield, contamination, permitting, and demand for the
recovered product determine whether that stock becomes a strategic supply.
Recycling can reduce dependence only when the full loop operates at scale.

## What this changes in the broader trend map

This finding links geopolitical dependence to ordinary US systems: vehicles,
grid storage, chips, data centers, defense equipment, construction, and
consumer electronics. A supply disruption may arrive as a product delay,
higher price, redesign, lower performance, reduced investment, or a changed
alliance choice. Conversely, a domestic facility may create capability without
removing dependence if it still imports refined feedstock or lacks a second
supplier.

It also gives the atlas a material counterexample to “domestic production equals
autonomy.” US production value can rise while import reliance remains high.
Import reliance can fall while processing, technology, or ownership remains
concentrated abroad. Strategic room requires substitute performance, usable
inventory, recovery infrastructure, and the ability to switch suppliers under
stress.

## What remains unproven

These sources do not establish that a particular export restriction caused a US
shortage, price increase, factory shutdown, consumer loss, or defense delay.
They do not show realized recycling volumes sufficient to change import
reliance, nor do they identify which firms can switch inputs and at what cost.

They also do not measure environmental burden, local permitting conflict,
worker conditions, community exposure, consumer prices, or the distribution of
benefits from domestic mineral processing. The 2–3 year battery-recycling
window is an assessment of potential, not a completed resilience outcome.

## Next test

Build a compact mineral-application event ledger with:

```text
export control, mine/processor disruption, or policy change
  -> shipment, price, purity, inventory, and supplier exposure
  -> firm redesign, substitution, recycling, or production interruption
  -> output, employment, investment, product price/quality, and local burden
  -> government response, alliance coordination, supplier switch, or concession
```

Start with one battery material and one semiconductor material. Record country
and processing stage, contract and shipment dates, substitute qualification,
recycling yield, inventory, facility ownership, workforce, environmental
permit, and customer/defense output. Compare firms or applications with a
validated alternative against those without one. Only then can import
dependence be translated into realized US economic or geopolitical leverage.
