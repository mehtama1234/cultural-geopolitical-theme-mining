# Uber Eats / Mohamed merits-compliant-process record v1

**Decision:** [2025] FWC 2337, *Mohamed Mohamed v Portier Pacific Pty Ltd*
(`UDE2025/120`)

**Finding:** the application was dismissed after the Commission found Uber had
complied with the Digital Labour Platform Deactivation Code.

## Why this case matters

Mohamed is a merits-level counterweight to the restoration-heavy Australian
packet. It records a case in which the Commission accepted a low-rating
deactivation process, including a worker response and human consideration, as
lawful under the Code. It should not be read as proof that all rating systems
are fair or that no downstream harm occurred; it is a bounded adjudicated
finding about this process and record.

## Primary record

Mohamed’s satisfaction rating stayed below Uber’s 85% Melbourne average for
approximately 16 weeks. The Commission found that Uber gave a compliant warning,
was entitled to begin the process, issued a compliant preliminary notice, and
lawfully suspended access. It also found that Mohamed had an opportunity to
respond and that a human representative considered his response of 30 May
2025.

The Commission accepted Uber’s evidence that the rating average was 56% for the
period ending 25 March 2025, 40% for the period ending 18 April, and 31% for
the period ending 30 May. It found the 85% requirement reasonable and concluded
that Uber had reasonable grounds to regard the platform obligation as
established. The application was dismissed because the Code-compliance
condition for unfair deactivation was not met.

## Important legal distinction

The decision expressly distinguishes the Code’s “valid reason” and “reasonable
grounds” framework from the more demanding fact-substantiation approach used
in unfair-dismissal cases. The Commission stated that the Code tests the
quality of the reason and whether the operator reasonably considered it
established. It also found that direct evidence from customers was not
necessary or realistic in a sustained low-rating dispute.

This distinction is central to the atlas: the same phrase—“valid reason”—can
carry different evidentiary work in different institutional regimes. A case
coded as “human review sufficient” must therefore retain the governing rule,
the evidence considered, and the remedy consequence.

## Stage coding

```text
explanation                 observed adjudicated sufficient
human review                observed adjudicated sufficient
correction / restoration    not observed
payment / compensation      not observed
post-order access           not applicable in located decision
```

The decision establishes process compliance and dismissal. It does not
establish rating accuracy, actual customer-level service failure, correction of
the rating, continued access, earnings recovery, or absence of hardship.

## Cross-case use

Read Mohamed alongside Kumar and Dar as rating-based merits dismissals, and
alongside Warraich, Waheed, and Hotak as a counterexample to any claim that the
Code systematically produces restoration. The contrast is not simply
“algorithm good” versus “algorithm bad.” It is a comparison of evidentiary
standards, notice, response opportunity, human involvement, reason quality, and
whether the institution can order a material repair.
