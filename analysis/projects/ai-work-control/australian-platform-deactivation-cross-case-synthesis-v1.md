# Australian platform-deactivation cross-case synthesis v1

**Status:** bounded primary-decision comparison; not a representative sample  
**Checked:** 2026-09-15

## Question

When a worker challenges platform deactivation under Australia's Fair Work
regime, which parts of the remedy chain become observable, and what determines
whether the case ends in restoration, refusal, or no merits outcome?

## Selection and boundary

This comparison includes three public Fair Work Commission proceedings selected
because together they expose different stages and outcomes:

- *Bandameeda v Amazon* ([2025] FWC 3842): merits finding of unfair
  deactivation, reactivation, and lost-remuneration order.
- *Kumar v Portier Pacific* ([2025] FWC 2275): merits finding that Uber's
  process and rating rule complied with the Code; claim dismissed.
- *Bakar v Rasier Pacific* ([2025] FWC 1874; revocation [2025] FWC 2278):
  eligibility finding later revoked by consent after settlement and
  discontinuance.

This is a mechanism sample, not a case rate, prevalence estimate, or ranking
of Uber, Amazon, or the Australian system. The three cases have different
platforms, decision surfaces, evidence, and procedural endpoints.

## Cross-case matrix

| Case | Decision surface | Worker route | Review observed | Outcome | Strongest open link |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bandameeda | Customer complaint; alleged entry into a home; permanent deactivation | Response, request for human discussion, statutory application | Commission found no real representative discussion, no demonstrated human consideration, and inadequate further inquiry | Reactivation on prior terms; $12,126.31 lost remuneration ordered | Receipt, continued access, recurrence prevention, anti-retaliation |
| Kumar | Customer-satisfaction rating below 85% threshold | Written response, platform review, statutory application | Commission identified a human Community Operations review and found notice, timing, inquiry, and rating threshold compliant | Application dismissed; no restoration or payment | Individual rating accuracy, correction route, downstream worker effects |
| Bakar | Uber/Uber Eats deactivation; six-month eligibility and platform-operator identity | Statutory application and threshold hearing | Eligibility initially recognized, then decision revoked by consent after settlement | No public merits outcome or remedy | Settlement terms, responsible entity, enforceability, merits fairness |

## What varies across the cases

### 1. Human review is an authority-and-inquiry test

“Human review” has opposite meanings in the two merits cases. In Bandameeda,
the Commission found the worker's request for discussion was not meaningfully
accommodated and the platform did not make necessary inquiries. In Kumar, the
Commission found that a human Community Operations representative considered
the response and reasonably decided no further inquiry was warranted. The
presence of a human label is therefore insufficient; the record must identify
the reviewer, response considered, inquiry made, and authority to affect the
decision.

### 2. A defensible refusal can still leave the underlying data question open

Kumar shows that a claim can be dismissed even while the Commission recognizes
that an individual customer rating may be unfair or reflect conduct outside a
worker's control. The outcome turned on the reasonableness of the threshold,
the evidence in that case, notice, and process compliance. The decision does
not create a general rating-correction remedy or prove that every rating is
accurate.

### 3. A right can fail before merits review

Bakar shows that eligibility and entity identity are not administrative
details. The initial threshold finding was later revoked after settlement and
discontinuance, and the Commission raised whether the named Australian entity
or Uber Technologies Inc. operated the App. If the entity capable of restoring
access or paying lost remuneration is not correctly named and served, the
formal right may not reach a merits remedy in the public record.

### 4. Restoration is not compensation and is not durable reform

Bandameeda's lost-remuneration order restores the financial position the worker
would likely have occupied absent deactivation; it is not coded as punitive or
general compensation. The public materials establish orders, but not payment
receipt, continued access, changed platform rules, or protection from
retaliation. Outcome depth must therefore be carried forward into a post-order
follow-up design.

## Provisional finding

The Australian cases support a narrow mechanism claim:

> A platform-deactivation remedy becomes materially effective only when worker
> eligibility, respondent identity, notice, human review, evidentiary inquiry,
> and remedial authority align. The same statutory framework can produce
> restoration, a reasoned refusal, or no public merits outcome depending on
> those conditions.

The claim is stronger than “regulation works” and narrower than “workers are
protected.” It identifies the institutional joins that should be tested in
other countries and later Australian cases.

## Boundary

This synthesis does not estimate how often Australian platform workers obtain
remedies. It uses three selected primary proceedings to identify mechanisms,
preserves missing settlement and post-order evidence, and does not infer
autonomous algorithmic causation where the decisions establish only
platform-mediated management.
