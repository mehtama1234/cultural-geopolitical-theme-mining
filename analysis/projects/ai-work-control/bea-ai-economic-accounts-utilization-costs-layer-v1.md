# BEA AI economic-accounts, utilization, and cost layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis early estimates and Working
Papers 2026-8 and 2026-18  
**Unit:** industry accounts, state-industry cells, and industry-year
observations  
**Status:** early estimates and descriptive associations; not a settled causal
AI effect

## Why BEA needs its own layer

BEA measures a different object from worker surveys, firm experiments, or
household panels. Its contribution is to test how AI may appear in output,
productivity, prices, input costs, labor composition, and cost shares. That
measurement role is itself part of the social story: AI may be embedded in
software, cloud services, and organizational routines before national accounts
can identify a direct AI line item.

## Three distinct evidence roles

### Early industry-account estimates

BEA says there is not yet a direct AI line item in U.S. national accounts. Its
indirect baseline estimate is consistent with productivity enhancement and
input saving and with a shift toward younger, relatively less educated workers.
An alternative timing specification is less robust and also suggests labor
saving. These are early estimates, not observed employment or wage effects.

### State-industry utilization and performance

Working Paper 2026-18 links worker-reported frequent AI use from the Gallup
Workforce Panel to LEHD employment/earnings and BEA real output. It uses 75,292
worker observations from Q2 2025 through Q1 2026 and links them to annual
outcomes from 2016–2024. A one-percentage-point higher frequent-user share is
associated with roughly 0.4–0.6% higher employment and 0.1–0.2% higher real
output after 2020. Employment is noisy; the paper calls the estimates
descriptive rather than causal, and an industry-only employer-reported
specification does not recover the same output pattern.

### Industry costs and prices

Working Paper 2026-8 uses 1,586 industry-year observations across 61 private
industries from 1997–2023. Its reported contributions include a −0.0228
gross-output interaction, −0.0113 labor-price contribution, −0.0071 noncollege
labor contribution, −0.0019 materials contribution, −0.011 labor-share
change, and +0.0084 capital-share change. These are industry-account
contributions, not household price reductions.

## The measurement finding

Realized use, economic performance, cost structure, and household incidence are
related but not interchangeable. Worker-reported use can exceed firm-reported
production use because workers apply AI to more tasks than firms identify as
core production. A positive state-industry association can coexist with noisy
employment estimates. An industry cost shift can coexist with unclear
purchaser incidence.

```text
reported use and measurement choice
  -> output, productivity, labor composition, and input cost
  -> firm price, wage, employment, and ownership decisions
  -> household purchasing power and worker bargaining
```

BEA reaches the middle of this chain. It does not carry the final distributional
or social arrows.

## Counterinterpretations and limits

Retrospective utilization measured after much of the outcome window creates a
reverse-causality concern. Managerial quality, local demand, intangible capital,
cell composition, timing assumptions, imputation, and unmeasured AI costs can
shape the results. Lower industry output-price growth does not establish that
consumers paid less or that savings were shared.

## Next test

Align BEA accounts with firm adoption and investment, worker task/control data,
BLS wages and hours, Federal Reserve wealth and household spending, and
consumer prices. Preserve worker versus firm denominators, state-industry
versus industry-only units, and the timing of AI measurement relative to
outcomes. Test whether cost changes reach households and whether labor-share
changes reach pay or bargaining.
