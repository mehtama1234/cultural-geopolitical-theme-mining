# BLS union coverage and bargaining-room baseline v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Unit:** US employed wage and salary workers, Current Population Survey annual averages  
**Period:** 2024–2025, with historical comparison to 1983  
**Status:** population bargaining-context comparison; not a causal union wage estimate

## Why this pass matters

The work-and-control theme asks who can influence a schedule, contest a rule,
share productivity gains, or leave without carrying all of the risk. Union
membership and contract coverage are not the whole of worker power, but they are
observable institutional channels for collective voice and negotiated terms.

```text
workplace rule, technology, schedule, or risk
  -> worker experiences pay, hours, control, safety, or uncertainty
  -> individual exit, voice, collective representation, or acceptance
  -> employer adjusts terms, work organization, monitoring, or distribution
  -> household security, status, local dependence, and political meaning change
```

This source measures the representation context and earnings association. It
does not identify a particular employer decision or show that representation
caused a particular worker's pay, autonomy, health, or exit.

## Reported national baseline

According to the BLS 2025 Union Members release:

- 10.0% of US wage and salary workers were union members in 2025, or about
  14.7 million workers.
- 11.2% were represented by a union or employee association contract when
  workers who were not members but were covered are included.
- The 2024 membership rate was 9.9%, so the one-year national movement was
  small.
- The comparable 1983 membership rate was 20.1%, with 17.7 million members.

The rate comparison captures institutional reach, not the total amount of
worker voice. A nonunion worker may have meaningful individual bargaining power,
and a union member may have weak practical influence in a particular workplace.

## Distribution is part of the story

The BLS tables show substantial variation by age, occupation, industry, and
state. For example, in 2025 the membership rate was 4.7% for workers age 16–24
and 10.8% for workers age 25 and over. Representation was 5.6% and 12.1% for
those groups respectively.

The state table also shows a wide range rather than one national labor
condition. In 2025, California's reported membership rate was 14.9% and
Alabama's was 6.3%; state estimates should be treated cautiously because of
the survey change described below and the smaller state samples.

Industry and occupation differences matter for technology and firm-power
questions. In 2025, union membership among management, business, and financial
operations occupations was 4.3%, while professional and related occupations
were 14.7%. These categories are broad and should not be read as direct
measures of autonomy, AI exposure, or worker satisfaction.

## Earnings association: useful context, not a causal result

For full-time wage and salary workers in 2025, BLS reports median usual weekly
earnings of $1,404 for union members, $1,386 for workers represented by a union,
and $1,174 for nonunion workers. The comparison is descriptive. Members and
nonmembers differ in occupation, industry, tenure, age, geography, hours,
selection into jobs, and other conditions. These tables do not estimate the
counterfactual earnings of the same worker under a different representation
status.

The distinction between **members** and **represented workers** is itself useful:
formal coverage can extend beyond individual membership, while membership may
provide a separate channel for participation, dues, leadership, and collective
action. Neither measure alone tells us whether a worker can challenge an
algorithm, refuse unsafe work, alter a schedule, or change employers.

## What this adds to the broader societal map

### 1. Worker power is institutionally uneven

The labor market does not offer one common level of bargaining room. Collective
representation is distributed by sector, occupation, age, and place. Therefore
the same technology, schedule policy, benefit rule, or productivity claim can
land in workplaces with very different capacities to contest or redistribute it.

### 2. The long decline is about voice infrastructure, not only wages

The fall from a 20.1% membership rate in 1983 to roughly 10% in 2025 describes
a reduced reach of one formal institution. It does not prove that every worker
lost autonomy or that unions are the only source of power. It does show why
workplace change should be studied alongside the institutions that can negotiate
its terms.

### 3. Headline productivity or employment can hide distributional power

A firm can retain employment or introduce a useful tool while changing who sets
the pace, owns the data, bears error risk, receives the gain, or can appeal a
decision. Union coverage is a conditioning variable for that next question, not
the answer to it.

### 4. Worker voice connects to culture and politics through lived control

Representation can become a social meaning—security, dignity, status,
solidarity, dependence, or exclusion—and may shape collective action. The BLS
tables do not measure those meanings. They identify where to look for them and
where a work-rule comparison should be conditioned on institutional voice.

## Important data boundary

The October 2025 CPS was not collected because of a federal government shutdown.
BLS therefore constructed 2025 annual household-survey estimates from 11 months
excluding October. The agency says 2025 annual estimates are not strictly
comparable with other annual averages and that union and earnings estimates are
especially affected because they use one quarter of the CPS sample. State
estimates require particular caution.

## Arrow status

| Arrow | Status | Safe conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Workplace population → formal representation context | Observed | Union membership and contract representation vary across worker groups, industries, and states. |
| Representation → pay or security | Associated/descriptive | BLS reports different median earnings; these tables do not identify a causal effect. |
| Representation → control, voice, or exit | Open in this source | Membership is an institutional channel, not a direct measure of schedule control or practical exit. |
| Work rule/technology → household and political meaning | Open | Requires worker-level exposure, interpretation, action, and follow-up. |

## Next bounded comparison

Pair the BLS representation baseline with a worker-event record containing:

1. tool, schedule, monitoring, benefit, or safety change;
2. pay, hours, pace, discretion, health, and error-risk change;
3. union/contract coverage, worker voice, and appeal route;
4. actual exit, transfer, absence, collective action, or acceptance;
5. household time, income, care, and security response; and
6. employer, regulator, or political response.

The counterexample is essential: find workplaces with similar technology or
productivity changes where worker control and bargaining room stayed stable or
improved. That prevents the program from treating representation, automation,
or employment status as a complete explanation.
