# Capability, dependency, local distribution, and state power layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Status:** cross-level infrastructure synthesis; realized outcomes and geopolitical effects remain open

## The broad question

When digital or AI infrastructure arrives, does it create durable capability
for the place, or mostly create a new dependency on the firms, capital, energy,
data, and suppliers that control it?

```text
tool, cloud, model, or data-centre investment
  -> public, firm, research, or consumer capability
  -> demand for power, land, water, chips, capital, skills, and networks
  -> ownership, procurement, interoperability, and provider dependence
  -> local jobs, prices, services, privacy, resilience, and public control
  -> national bargaining power, strategic choice, and external leverage
```

This is not a claim that infrastructure investment is either good or bad. It is
a way to examine capability and dependence together.

## Evidence layers already available

| Layer | Unit | What it establishes | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| World Bank AI capability framework | Country and institutional system | Adoption requires local infrastructure, skills, data, institutions, and the ability to adapt and advance; concentration in models, chips, and data centres can create dependency | Whether a particular country converts imported access into durable local capability |
| IEA energy and AI | Global energy system and data-centre scenarios | AI depends on electricity, grids, fuels, minerals, and connection capacity; demand can affect affordability and energy security | Which locality pays the marginal cost and whether the investment improves or strains its grid |
| BIS firm evidence | Matched firm and worker records | Adoption can coincide with productivity and wage gains, with larger gains for medium and large firms in the cited comparison | Who owns the complementary capital and whether gains persist or diffuse to smaller firms and workers |

| OFR public analytical system | Financial regulator and public workforce | A public institution can use AI while its workforce, budget, and analytical infrastructure change | Whether AI increases inspection capacity or hides a loss of human expertise and accountability |

## Provisional cross-level patterns

### 1. Capability is a stack, not a software license

The technology layer is only one part of the outcome. Power, connectivity,
capital, data rights, local skills, procurement, maintenance, and institutional
authority determine whether a system can be adapted, audited, and replaced.
Counting adoption without those complements confuses access with capability.

### 2. Investment can create capability and dependence simultaneously

The Romania case offers a public-service route into cloud dependence; the
Malaysia case offers a private-campus route into regional compute capacity.
Both can increase available capability while making the place more reliant on
providers, contractors, investors, grids, or renewal terms. The net result is
an empirical question, not a slogan about sovereignty.

### 3. Local benefit is a distributional outcome

Jobs, tax revenue, service quality, skills, energy costs, water use, land value,
privacy, and outage risk may accrue to different people. A project can produce
investment and still leave local residents with little control or high public
cost. “Capacity added” must therefore be reported beside ownership, incidence,
and replaceability.

### 4. State power depends on exit and inspection

The meaningful strategic variables are not only compute volume or announced
investment. They include provider diversity, data portability, contract and
termination rights, local technical knowledge, public audit access, supply-chain
substitutability, and the ability to continue service during a dispute or
outage. These determine whether a state can inspect, bargain, switch, or refuse.

## Keep these outcomes separate

| Outcome | Do not treat it as |
|---|---|
| Announced investment | Operational capacity |
| Cloud migration | Better public service |
| Data-centre capacity | Local ownership or sovereignty |
| Jobs promised | Jobs realized, well-paid, or locally retained |
| Foreign capital | Local development or strategic dependence by itself |
| AI adoption | Productivity, public capability, or worker benefit |
| Energy connection | Affordable, reliable, or fairly distributed power |
| Data location | Public control, privacy, or legal access |
| Provider scale | Replaceability or bargaining power |

## Evidence status by arrow

| Arrow | Status | Safe current conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure → required complements | Reported/compared | AI and cloud systems require physical, financial, technical, and institutional complements. |
| Adoption → firm or public capability | Reported/open | Productivity and service capability are plausible outcomes, but must be measured at the adopting unit. |
| Investment → local distribution | Open | Jobs, wages, taxes, prices, energy, water, and service effects are not established by project announcements. |

| Capability/dependence → state power | Open | Bargaining power requires evidence of switching, inspection, refusal, or external response. |

## Next bounded comparison

For one public-cloud migration and one data-centre campus, build a time-ordered
ledger with:

1. ownership, funders, contractors, customers, and control rights;
2. capital cost, operating cost, renewal, termination, and portability terms;
3. power, water, land, network, emissions, and outage records;
4. local jobs, wages, training, suppliers, taxes, and skill retention;
5. public service reliability, access, privacy, security, and appeal outcomes;
6. provider concentration, alternative suppliers, and switching cost;
7. local distribution of benefit and burden;
8. state negotiation, policy change, alliance response, or external leverage.

Use a delayed project, locally operated system, or multi-provider arrangement as
a counterexample. Compare promised and realized outcomes, and do not infer a
household effect unless the same people or service cases are observed.

## What would change the interpretation

The working picture would weaken if imported infrastructure consistently built
local skills and interchangeable providers; if ownership and procurement did
not affect exit or inspection; if projects produced broadly distributed local
benefits without material public costs; or if measured capacity did not alter
state bargaining, resilience, or external dependence.

## Relationship to the broad program

This layer extends the 14-theme map from technology adoption to firm structure,
public capability, unequal place effects, consumer and worker control, and
geopolitical state consequences. Its core question is simple: who can use the
system, who controls the complements, who pays when it fails, and who can leave?
