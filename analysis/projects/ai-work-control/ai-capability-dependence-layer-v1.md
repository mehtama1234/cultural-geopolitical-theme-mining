# AI capability, infrastructure, and dependence layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Sources:** World Bank WDR 2026, IEA *Energy and AI*, Romanian public-cloud records, and Malaysia data-centre finance/ownership records  
**Unit:** government system, data centre, firm, grid, country, and cross-border capital chain  
**Status:** comparative infrastructure layer; realized local capability and geopolitical effect remain open

## The broad question

The important question is not simply whether a country “uses AI.” It is whether
the country can turn access into durable capability: local skills, adaptable
systems, public oversight, multiple providers, and the ability to exit.

```text
AI tool or infrastructure arrives
  -> public service, firm, or research capability changes
  -> electricity, capital, skills, data, and suppliers become necessary
  -> ownership and dependency shape who controls the capability
  -> local gains, costs, public capacity, and bargaining power are distributed
  -> state strategy and geopolitical leverage change
```

This is a state, firm, infrastructure, and political-economy chain. It is not a
direct household-level causal claim.

## What the current evidence shows

The World Bank’s 2026 World Development Report frames AI development as
“adopt, adapt, and advance.” It argues that importing tools is not enough:
countries need local languages, institutions, data, skills, reliable
electricity, internet access, and systems that can work across multiple
platforms and providers. It also identifies concentration in advanced models,
chips, and data centres as a dependency risk.

The IEA’s *Energy and AI* report makes the physical complement explicit: data
centres require electricity, and AI deployment affects grids, energy security,
emissions, innovation, and affordability. A software capability can therefore
create a local connection, land, fuel, water, and financing question.

The Romania and Malaysia cases show two different entry routes:

| Case | Capability promise | Dependency to test |
|---|---|---|
| Romania public cloud migration | modernized government applications and digital public services | cloud provider, contractor, data location, portability, renewal cost, public audit, and citizen service quality |
| Malaysia Johor data-centre campus | regional compute capacity, investment, jobs, and data-sovereignty benefits | operator and customer control, foreign capital, power and land terms, local skills, actual jobs, and ability to switch |

The cases establish announced projects, financing, and intended uses. They do
not yet establish realized public benefit, local spillovers, data sovereignty,
or stronger bargaining power.

## What this adds to the 14-theme map

- **Infrastructure, technology, and dependency:** capability depends on
  physical and institutional complements, not software alone.
- **Firm, sector, and market power:** operators, hyperscalers, lenders, and
  public buyers divide ownership, risk, and exit options.
- **Public systems and policy feedback:** procurement and evaluation determine
  whether a pilot becomes durable public capacity.
- **Unequal exposure and status:** regions may receive investment while bearing
  different energy, land, water, privacy, or workforce costs.
- **Geopolitical/state consequences:** infrastructure can add national capacity
  and external dependence simultaneously.
- **Consumer and worker power:** service reliability, data rights, appeal, and
  labor control are downstream outcomes that must be measured separately.

## Limits and next test

The current records combine official reports, project disclosures, and operator
announcements. They are strong for stated plans and dependencies to inspect,
but weak for realized outputs and distribution.

For each case, collect contracts, procurement, service-level records, energy and
water data, ownership changes, customer concentration, local jobs and wages,
training, taxes, data-transfer terms, portability, outage history, and exit
cost. Compare a locally controlled or multi-provider system, a delayed project,
or a failed deployment. Report capability, cost, control, and replaceability as
separate outcomes.
