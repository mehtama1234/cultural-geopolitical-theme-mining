# Australia–Malaysia–Cambodia stage-compatible comparison v1

**Status:** cross-country stage comparison; unlike evidence intentionally not
pooled
**Checked:** 2026-09-15

## Question

How far can workers move from an adverse platform-mediated decision toward an
auditable remedy in three different institutional settings?

The answer must compare stages reached, not count every formal right or hearing
as a successful remedy.

## Evidence architecture

| Jurisdiction | Unit of evidence | Strongest observed stage | What the unit cannot establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 11 identifiable FWC proceeding surfaces in a bounded official-bulletin frame | Eligibility decisions, merits findings, reactivation orders, and lost-remuneration orders | Prevalence, actual payment/access after orders, and system-wide reform |
| Malaysia | Act 872, Tribunal architecture, and one publicly reported GrabCar hearing | Formal institution and a case reaching a hearing with evidence/witness examination reportedly pending | Claim details, legal reasoning, award, payment, correction, or enforcement |
| Cambodia | Official ILO mixed-method diagnostic: 1,237 surveys, 29 worker interviews, 10 key informants | Worker-reported complaint access, complaint use, and low reported resolution; state authority named | Individual case files, reviewer authority, corrected decisions, payment, or state caseload |

These are not three samples of the same population. Australia is an
adjudication-centered case inventory; Malaysia is an institutional-use
observation; Cambodia is a worker-experience diagnostic.

## Common remedy chain

```text
adverse platform decision
  -> worker knows or can identify the reason
  -> worker contests through an available channel
  -> reviewer or institution examines the dispute
  -> decision, access, rating, or payment changes
  -> worker receives the remedy
  -> recurrence and retaliation are controlled
```

## Stage matrix

| Stage | Australia | Malaysia | Cambodia | Safe comparative statement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adverse decision visible | Deactivation, suspension, ratings, and complaint-based platform actions are visible in primary cases | Act 872 expressly covers algorithmic monitoring/decision systems and deactivation; GrabCar dispute concerned trips/incentives/booking, but exact automated function is open | Workers report ratings, allocation, refusal penalties, and suspension effects | All three expose platform-mediated control, but only Australia currently supplies detailed case-level adjudication |
| Reason/explanation | Detailed notices and their defects were tested in merits decisions | Statutory disclosure and written-reason architecture exists; use in the GrabCar case is unknown | Workers can report complaints, but specific decision reasons are not independently observed | Formal explanation rules and usable explanations are distinct variables |
| Contest channel | FWC application plus platform response/discussion routes | Tribunal hearing, conciliation, and statutory complaint routes exist; first hearing reported | Platform complaint channel reported by 78% of tuk-tuk, 88% of delivery, and 96% of taxi workers; use was 50%, 52%, and 36% | Access is observable in all three, but channel use has different evidence designs |
| Review | FWC directly evaluated platform inquiry; findings range from inadequate inquiry to sufficient human Community Operations review | Tribunal hearing reportedly included evidence and additional witness examination | Independent human review is not observed; approximately two-thirds of complainants said issues were seldom resolved | A hearing or complaint channel is not proof that a reviewer could change the decision |
| Correction/restoration | Multiple reactivation orders; Bandameeda and Mansoor include public lost-pay orders | No award or correction located | No verified correction, restoration, or payment located | Australia reaches adjudicated remedy; the other two remain open at or before outcome |
| Receipt/durability | Payment receipt, continued access, recurrence prevention, and anti-retaliation generally open | Enforcement and worker receipt open | Resolution quality and state enforcement open | The final practical-remedy stages are missing across the comparison |

## Australia: adjudicated remedy depth

The bounded Australian census identifies eleven proceeding surfaces. The cases
include eligibility failure, settlement/revocation, timing review, a merits
dismissal, and merits-level reactivation. The FWC primary record confirms:

- Bandameeda (`UDE2025/62`): Amazon's process failed to provide a meaningful
  discussion and further inquiry; reactivation and `$12,126.31` lost
  remuneration were ordered.
- Mansoor (`UDE2025/141`): serious sexual-misconduct allegations were not
  proven on the record; reactivation and `$6,073.23` lost earnings were
  ordered.
- Warraich (`UDE2025/108`): vague complaint warnings and weakly evidenced
  internal inquiry failed the Code; reactivation was ordered and lost pay was
  left for later determination.
- Kumar (`UDE2025/68`): warnings, human review, and an 85% rating threshold
  were accepted; the claim was dismissed.
- Hotak (`UDE2025/53`): later voluntary reactivation did not extinguish the
  Commission's jurisdiction, preserving scrutiny of the original loss.

The Australian contribution is therefore outcome depth and reasoned
institutional findings. It still does not show how common these outcomes are
among all platform workers or whether the orders were implemented in full.

## Malaysia: institution-to-hearing transition

Malaysia's Act 872 creates a formal route involving disclosure, non-automated
review, written reasons, conciliation, and a Gig Workers Tribunal. The first
publicly reported GrabCar matter crossed the threshold from institutional
design to a hearing: a claimant appeared personally, GrabCar used an employee
representative, and the matter reportedly continued for evidence and witness
examination.

That is a real implementation event, but it is not yet an outcome. The public
record does not identify the claim amount, precise relief, legal provisions
applied, Tribunal reasoning, award, payment, correction, or enforcement. The
appropriate code is therefore:

```text
statutory route                 observed_official
institutional use               observed_case_report
hearing                         observed_case_report
merits review                   not_observed
correction / restoration        not_observed
payment                         not_observed
enforcement                     not_observed
```

The Malaysia test is whether a low-cost, lawyer-limited Tribunal can convert
formal access into an auditable order despite asymmetry in platform evidence
and technical knowledge.

## Cambodia: access-to-resolution gap

The ILO diagnostic shows a different layer of reality. Most surveyed workers
said a platform complaint channel was available, and a substantial minority
reported using it. Yet roughly two-thirds of complainants said their issue was
seldom resolved; serious accidents or theft were less likely to be resolved
than minor technical or payment problems.

The report also names the Ministry of Labour and Vocational Training as the
state dispute authority under Notification No. 1107. That creates a visible
state route, but no caseload, decision, enforcement, or worker-level correction
is documented. Cambodia therefore supplies breadth of reported exposure and
resolution experience, but not adjudicated remedy depth.

The appropriate code is:

```text
platform exposure               observed_self_report
complaint channel access        observed_self_report
complaint use                   observed_self_report
resolution quality              observed_self_report (mostly seldom resolved)
state authority                 observed_official_action
specific review                 not_observed
correction / restoration        not_observed
payment                         not_observed
anti-retaliation                not_observed
```

The Cambodia test is whether complaint access can become meaningful recovery
when workers are commonly classified as independent contractors and social
security is thin or voluntary.

## Cross-country mechanism finding

The comparison supports a narrow, stage-specific proposition:

> Institutional maturity is not one dimension. Australia currently shows the
> greatest public remedy depth, Malaysia shows a transition from statutory
> architecture to initial hearing use, and Cambodia shows broad reported access
> with weak reported resolution. None of the three public records verifies the
> full chain through durable, non-retaliatory system change.

This avoids two common errors. First, Malaysia's hearing should not be counted
as equivalent to Australia's reactivation order. Second, Cambodia's complaint
access should not be treated as failure merely because no tribunal case file is
public; it measures a different layer, and its worker-reported low resolution
is itself important evidence.

## Cultural and geopolitical interpretation

The difference is not adequately described as “developed-country protection”
versus “developing-country absence of rights.” The three systems distribute
contestability differently:

```text
Australia  -> adjudicated case reasoning and enforceable restoration orders
Malaysia   -> statutory institution and low-cost hearing architecture
Cambodia   -> worker experience of app-level access amid classification/social-protection gaps
```

The power question is who controls the evidentiary bridge between a worker's
challenge and a changed consequence. Australia makes that bridge visible to a
tribunal. Malaysia is testing whether a new Tribunal can build it. Cambodia
shows what happens when an app channel exists but the worker cannot reliably
convert contact into resolution.

## Boundary

This comparison is not a legal ranking, national prevalence estimate, or pooled
remedy rate. It is a cross-country measurement design that preserves different
units, stages, evidence strengths, and missing implementation links.
