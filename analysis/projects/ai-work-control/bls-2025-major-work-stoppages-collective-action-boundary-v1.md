# BLS 2025 major work stoppages and collective-action boundary v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Work Stoppages Program
**Reference period:** work stoppages beginning in calendar year 2025; release February 20, 2026
**Status:** reported collective-action and public-sector bargaining layer; not a total strike prevalence estimate

## Why this update matters

The atlas already measures union coverage, bargaining context, worker mobility,
productivity, and political action as separate surfaces. A work stoppage is a
more concrete collective-action event: workers withhold labor, employers and
public bodies face operational and bargaining pressure, and the conflict can
affect service users and communities. It is still not the same as worker
control, successful bargaining, or broad labor movement strength.

## Reported BLS facts

| Surface | Result | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| Major stoppages | 30 began in 2025 | BLS counts stoppages involving at least 1,000 workers lasting at least one weekday shift; smaller actions are excluded |
| Workers involved | 306,800 workers were involved in stoppages beginning in 2025 | Workers can be counted more than once across the reference period; this is not a unique-worker rate |
| Service concentration | Service-providing industries accounted for 300,600 workers, or 98.0% of the total; education and health services accounted for 196,500 | Collective action is concentrated in sectors that also deliver socially essential services, but the release does not measure service disruption or public response |
| Public ownership | Six local-government and 11 state-government stoppages began in 2025, involving 232,800 workers and 711,600 cumulative idle days | Public-sector bargaining is a major part of the reported 2025 total; the release does not establish policy success or public legitimacy |
| Private industry | 74,000 workers were idled in private-industry stoppages beginning in 2025, with 784,400 cumulative idle days | Private-sector action is smaller in this measure but still has firm, customer, and household channels |
| Recent comparison | 31 major stoppages began in 2024 and involved 271,500 workers; 33 began in 2023 and involved 458,900 workers | Count and worker involvement do not move together, so “more strikes” and “more labor power” are not interchangeable |

## What this adds to the broad goal

This layer adds an observed event form to the work/control and political-action
lanes. It separates:

1. **Representation:** formal union membership or coverage.
2. **Conflict:** a stoppage that withholds labor.
3. **Bargaining outcome:** settlement, contract terms, or unresolved dispute.
4. **Public incidence:** service delay, substitution, safety, or community cost.
5. **Worker consequence:** pay, schedule, protection, control, and household
   security.
6. **Political meaning:** public support, blame, organizing, or policy change.

The BLS release observes the second surface and parts of its scale and sector
context. It does not close the other arrows. In particular, a stoppage is an
action, not proof that workers had equal bargaining power or achieved their
demands.

## Counterexample and open arrows

The 2023–2025 comparison is a useful reversal: 2025 had fewer involved workers
than 2023 despite a similar number of major stoppages. The event count alone
therefore cannot stand in for collective power. The next stronger design would
join each named stoppage to union/worker demands, settlement or contract terms,
employer response, service-user exposure, worker pay and safety outcomes, and
later membership or political action.

The current BLS series also cannot describe small workplace actions, informal
organizing, work-to-rule, individual refusal, lockout/strike composition, or
the full universe of labor conflict. BLS uses “work stoppages” for both strikes
and lockouts.
