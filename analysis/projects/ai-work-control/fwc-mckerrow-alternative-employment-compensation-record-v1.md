# Fair Work Commission McKerrow alternative-employment remedy record v1

**Status:** bounded official case example; alternative work and remedy
calculation observed, payment receipt and later recovery open  
**Checked:** 2026-09-19

## Why this case matters

The Fair Work Commission's McKerrow example shows a remedy being calculated
while the worker is already in new employment. The worker had started a new
job after dismissal, earning a fortnightly gross salary of $1,882.69 plus
$169.44 in superannuation. The Commission also considered the 21-day period
between the compensation order and expected receipt, and deducted $2,824 in
wages and $254.16 in superannuation from the compensation amount.

This makes a specific institutional rule visible:

```text
dismissal -> alternative employment -> remedy calculation
                         -> expected replacement earnings reduce the award
```

The case does not show that the new job was equivalent, freely chosen, durable,
or sufficient for household recovery. It also does not show that the ordered
compensation was actually received.

## Evidence chain

| Stage | What the official page reports | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Original loss | The worker had been dismissed and sought an unfair-dismissal remedy | The public example does not provide the full dismissal episode, household budget, or alternative-offer set |
| Alternative work | New employment paid $1,882.69 gross per fortnight plus $169.44 superannuation | Job duration, security, travel, hours, quality, and voluntariness are not reported |
| Remedy clock | The expected time from order to actual receipt was 21 days | An expected receipt window is not proof of payment or worker confirmation |
| Remedy adjustment | $2,824 wages and $254.16 superannuation were deducted from compensation | Net award, tax, remaining loss, and adequacy are not reported here |
| Later life | Not reported | Household recovery, later employment, trust, recurrence, switching, and exit remain open |

## What it adds to the broader atlas

Alternative employment is not just a worker-side adaptation. It also enters the
institution's calculation of what compensation is owed. That can reduce formal
redress while the worker may still have absorbed search costs, insecurity,
lost continuity, or a job-quality loss. The correct interpretation is not that
the deduction was improper; it is that formal remedy and lived recovery use
different currencies and clocks.

This is one case example, not a compensation rule estimate, a worker-welfare
measure, or a causal finding.
