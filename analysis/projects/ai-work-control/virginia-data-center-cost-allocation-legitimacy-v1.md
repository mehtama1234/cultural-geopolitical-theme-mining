# Virginia data centers, cost allocation, and public legitimacy

**Status:** bounded institutional-legitimacy writeup  
**Checked:** 2026-09-15  
**Unit:** Virginia utility governance, large-load customers, residential rate classes, and public regulatory records

## The question

When data-center growth requires new transmission and distribution capacity,
who is expected to pay, who is protected, and what evidence would show that
the arrangement is viewed as legitimate? The current record can follow the
issue from physical expansion and grid stress into rate design and public cost
attribution. It cannot yet follow one household bill, one facility account,
and one later judgment through the same episode.

## The bounded chain

```text
large-load growth and planned transmission expansion
  -> grid-upgrade and reliability costs
  -> Rider T1 / rate-class / direct-assignment rules
  -> modeled or reported residential incidence
  -> public attribution and institutional response
  -> actual bill, payment, hardship, trust, or political action
```

The first five stages have records. The final stage remains open.

## What the evidence establishes

| Stage | Evidence | Safe interpretation | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical and system pressure | LBNL national load estimates; Prince William capacity/revenue reporting; Virginia 1,551 MW and 1,800 MW load-drop events | Data-center growth is being handled as a physical and reliability governance problem | National modeling, county aggregates, and utility events do not share a household denominator |
| Place and utility investment | Proposed Hornbaker–Devlin 230 kV line and switching station in Prince William County | A named utility-planning response is tied to a data-center growth zone | Proposed infrastructure is not construction, energization, or facility-specific service |
| Customer governance | Virginia high-load filing names industry participants, a 50 MW threshold, and account-aggregation rules | Regulators and utilities are formalizing who counts as a large load and how accounts may be grouped | A filing/recommended stipulation is not final account-level implementation |
| Cost attribution | SCC direct-assignment framework; 85% minimum transmission/distribution charge and 14-year obligations; a modeled Rider T1 illustration from $2.90 to $0.94 per 1,000 kWh | Policy is attempting to move some infrastructure risk toward the customers that create it | The illustration is modeled, not an observed bill or payment |
| Public attribution | SCC docket record describes a $9.69 monthly Rider T1 charge for a typical 1,000-kWh residential customer and argues that socialized upgrades can subsidize a large-load customer | The distributional issue is legible in the regulatory record | Testimony does not identify the facility’s causal share or show household hardship or opinion |
| Legitimacy endpoint | No same-household bill, payment, hardship, trust, organizing, or political-action join | The institutional meaning and response are open research questions | Do not call a policy protective, fair, or legitimacy-restoring from rules alone |

## What changed in the institutional chain

The record shows movement from attraction and expansion toward allocation and
accountability. Earlier evidence could show that data-center activity produced
local revenue and large system requirements. The newer SCC materials make the
distributional mechanism more explicit: separate rate classes, minimum
charges, collateral, direct assignment, and a supplemental docket for line
extension policy.

That is a change in institutional design, not yet a measured change in lived
burden. A rule can redistribute expected cost while leaving actual bills,
service quality, facility behavior, and public judgment unobserved. Likewise,
a lower modeled residential increase can coexist with higher bills from other
causes or with residents who oppose the project for land, water, reliability,
or control reasons.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Reported county data-center revenue can rise while households face separate
  utility, infrastructure, environmental, or service costs.
- A customer may pay a minimum charge without paying every system cost caused
  by the project; direct assignment requires account- and facility-level
  implementation records.
- A modeled $0.94 illustration is not the same object as the docket’s reported
  $9.69 Rider T1 charge; they differ in method, rate component, and time
  context.
- A large-load customer’s ability to ride through or trip offline during a
  fault does not establish whether residential customers lost service or who
  bore the cost.
- A public comment or expert filing shows attribution and contestation, not
  representative public opinion.

The [Golden-Mars public-input comparator](data/virginia-golden-mars-public-input-source-note.md)
shows how a related Virginia transmission case can produce visible public
contestation: the SCC reports more than 600 registered speakers and hundreds
of pages of comments concerning undergrounding, home value, landscape, and
health. This is a useful meaning/participation comparator, not evidence about
data-center attribution or Prince William household outcomes.

## Next decisive test

Join one public utility case to one named facility or account and one place:

1. identify the facility, owner, substation, contract capacity, and
   interconnection date;
2. obtain the relevant CPCN, line-extension, tariff, and account-allocation
   records;
3. separate approved, forecast, billed, paid, recovered, and disputed amounts;
4. identify whether the facility participated in a reported fault and its
   response mode; and
5. connect the place and timing to household bills, service interruptions,
   public comments, trust, organizing, or other measured response.

Until that design is possible, the strongest claim is that Virginia is building
an institutional mechanism to allocate and contest the costs of strategic
digital infrastructure. Whether the mechanism is effective, fair, trusted, or
geopolitically empowering remains open.
