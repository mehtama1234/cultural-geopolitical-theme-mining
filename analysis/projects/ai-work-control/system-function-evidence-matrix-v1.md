# System-function evidence matrix v1

**Status:** working research instrument  
**Last checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Project:** AI, work, and control  
**Purpose:** compare what workplace systems do, rather than treating “AI use” as one treatment

## Why this matrix exists

The current evidence does not study one thing called AI at work. It studies assistants, recommendations, task direction, monitoring, allocation, worker evaluation, and negotiated limits on personnel decisions. Those uses can have different effects even when a firm gives them the same AI label.

This matrix is a coding tool, not a new causal estimate. A row records what a source lets us say and what it does not let us say.

## Coding rule

For every source, record:

1. **Function:** what the system does;
2. **Decision:** what a worker, manager, firm, or platform can decide with it;
3. **Data path:** what information enters the system and whether it can be reused;
4. **Worker channel:** time, skill, pace, discretion, evaluation, pay, safety, or remedy;
5. **Authority:** who can accept, reject, inspect, correct, or appeal;
6. **Evidence strength:** observed result, survey association, written rule, company statement, or inference.

## What the matrix shows so far

The strongest repeated distinction is between a system that helps a worker choose and a system that sets the worker’s pace, score, access, or employment status. Assistance has measurable time or productivity gains in some settings. Monitoring and direct task direction show different risks. Written rules can change the authority structure, but the current packet has little direct evidence that workers can use those rules quickly and safely.

This is a bounded synthesis, not a claim that one function always helps or harms. The same product may perform several functions, and data collected for assistance may later be reused for evaluation or discipline.
