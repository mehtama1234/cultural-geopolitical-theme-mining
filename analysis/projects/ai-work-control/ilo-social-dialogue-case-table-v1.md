# ILO social-dialogue case table v1

**Status:** provisional case extraction  
**Checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Source:** Doellgast, Appalla, Ginzburg, Kim, and Thian, *Global case studies of social dialogue on AI and algorithmic management*, ILO Working Paper 144, July 2025.

## Why this table exists

The project question is not simply whether workers were consulted. It is whether worker knowledge and collective action changed the purpose, limits, data, pace, ownership, or protection around an AI or algorithmic system.

The ILO paper is a working paper based on case studies, interviews, archival sources, academic research, and news reports. The table therefore records what the paper reports; it does not treat each case as a controlled test or as proof that participation works everywhere.

## Case table

| Case | Place and level | System or problem | Worker action or voice | Reported change | What the case can support | Main limit or open question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SAG-AFTRA | United States; sectoral bargaining | Digital replicas of actors’ voices and likenesses | The union studied the technology, formed an AI working group and member task force, struck, and bargained with producers. | The 2023 agreement required advance notice and written informed consent for digital replicas and payment at the rate of a human performance. | Collective power can turn technical knowledge and public conflict into rules over identity, consent, and pay before routine deployment. | The agreement did not secure the same protection for purely synthetic performers; the paper reports no long-run outcome study. |
| Deutsche Telekom and IBM | Germany; company and workplace agreements | Workforce analytics, performance monitoring, and other algorithmic-management tools | Works councils and unions negotiated technology review, data limits, human oversight, retraining, job-security commitments, and ethics groups. | Reported rules included group-level metrics, limits on individual monitoring, no automated decisions without human oversight, and joint review of employee-data use. | Worker voice can enter system procurement, data design, and management rules—not only post-decision appeals. | The case rests on institutional rights and negotiated agreements; the paper does not estimate effects on productivity, pay, or all workers covered. |
| Culinary Union | United States; sector/company bargaining in Las Vegas hospitality | A housekeeping app that set task order and generated workload, location, and room-status data | Workers mapped inefficient routes, used backend data to identify quota violations, and brought the developer into bargaining. | Bargaining restored worker discretion and made workload violations easier for the union to monitor; training was added through the union-supported Culinary Academy. | Workers can produce technical counter-knowledge and use it to change an algorithmic workflow. | The report is based on an interview and does not provide a representative before/after measure of worker outcomes. |
| HD Hyundai Heavy Industries | South Korea; company and subcontractor workforce | Facial-recognition access system applied to about 18,000 subcontracted workers | Unions, subcontractor workers, human-rights groups, and the regional labor federation challenged the rollout through public action and complaints to labor and privacy authorities. | The case records a conflict over consultation, consent under precarious employment, and biometric data; more than 4,000 workers joined a signature campaign. | Collective action can join labor rights, privacy law, and subcontractor solidarity when the system targets workers with less security. | The paper records the challenge and legal route, but does not establish the final system outcome or a measured change in safety. |

The underlying merits were not decided by these judgments. | AI governance includes the labor hidden behind model development; legal reach, interim protection, and durable remedy can move in different directions. | The case is still legally and organizationally unsettled; reported harms and claims should not be read as a causal estimate for all content work. |

## First pattern

The cases suggest a three-part distinction:

```text
information -> bargaining or collective action -> enforceable change
```

Information alone is not the same as power. The reported changes become stronger when workers have a recognized forum, a legal right, a credible strike or public-action threat, access to system data, or a way to reach the lead firm behind a contractor.

This is an inference from the cases, not a causal result estimated by the ILO paper. The paper’s own comparison says social-dialogue efforts tend to be more effective where employer exit is constrained, collective worker voice is supported, and labor solidarity is inclusive.

## Four-map reading

| Map | What these cases add | What remains missing |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Task order, workload, retraining, pay, and job security are concrete bargaining objects. | Comparable before/after measures across cases. |
| Social | Voice reaches identity, dignity, privacy, health, and the status of subcontracted work. | Worker experience by gender, race, migration status, disability, and contract type. |
| Institution | Unions, works councils, courts, privacy bodies, training centers, and lead firms interact. | Which institution actually enforces a promise after the agreement is signed. |
| Power | Data access, consent, veto-like rights, strike capacity, and cross-chain reach determine leverage. | How power changes when workers cannot organize or when the lead firm is outside the country. |

## Finding this table supports

Worker voice is part of system design, but voice only becomes a control point when it can alter a rule, obtain evidence, delay or condition deployment, or impose a cost on refusal. The table strengthens the project’s finding 008 while keeping it provisional.

## Next tests

1. Obtain the actual agreements, contract language, and legal decisions where available.
2. Separate consultation, negotiation, veto, enforcement, and remedy in the coding scheme.
3. Add failed or symbolic consultation cases to avoid selecting only visible successes.
4. Compare direct employees with subcontracted, migrant, disabled, and platform workers.
5. Track whether the change persists after a new vendor, manager, contract, or model version arrives.
