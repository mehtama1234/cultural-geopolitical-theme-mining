# Modeled infrastructure protection is not yet lived incidence v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-19  
**Status:** bounded Virginia infrastructure/consumer synthesis; projections are not observed household outcomes

## The finding

Virginia’s large-load governance record now shows a real institutional response
to data-center and other high-demand growth: reliability events, new customer
classes, minimum-demand obligations, collateral and term rules, cost-allocation
changes, public hearings, and route mitigation.

But the record also exposes a crucial gap between modeled protection and lived
incidence:

```text
large-load growth and reliability risk
  -> regulator models cost allocation and minimum obligations
  -> projected bill/risk reduction
  -> [open] actual household bill, reliability, compensation, acceptance,
     and ability to replace or refuse the project
```

## What is visible

- The SCC modeled a typical-residential Rider T1 increase falling from $2.90 to
  $0.94 per month under an amended allocation method, a projected 67.5%
  reduction.
- The SCC separately reported typical residential bill increases of $11.24 per
  month in 2026 and $2.36 in 2027 at 1,000 kWh. Those figures are not attributed
  solely to data centers.
- Dominion reported 1,551 MW and later 1,800 MW of data-center load dropping
  after 230 kV outages and shifting to backup generation. Household duration,
  reliability, and local service consequences are not observed.
- More than 600 registered speakers and hundreds of written comments appeared
  in the Golden-Mars route proceedings. Route 4 and mitigation findings were
  selected, but resident compensation, acceptance, and measured property or
  health outcomes are not established.

These are different denominators and stages. A modeled bill change is not an
observed bill; a registered speaker is not the full affected population; and a
route decision is not proof of consent or restored local control.

## Consumer and political meaning

Infrastructure governance is often experienced indirectly. Households may not
know which portion of a bill, outage, landscape change, or service constraint
comes from a large-load decision. A regulatory model can therefore improve
technical allocation while leaving attribution, fairness, and trust unresolved.

Likewise, public participation can show contestation without showing that the
final decision was accepted, that the burden was repaired, or that people had
a practical way to refuse or move. The relevant cultural endpoint is not only
whether a hearing occurred, but whether residents could connect the project to
their lived costs and see a credible remedy.

## Required next fields

For one realized infrastructure case, retain:

1. forecast, approved, energized, and actual operating load;
2. tariff, collateral, direct contribution, and realized payment;
3. household bills, reliability, outage duration, water/road burden, and local
   service effects;
4. affected resident denominator, notice, participation, attribution, and
   requested remedy;
5. compensation, mitigation completion, acceptance, complaint, trust, or
   organized response; and
6. ownership, portability, replacement providers, renegotiation, and exit.

The same discipline applies at the geopolitical level: a capability or
procurement milestone is not leverage until an external actor’s choice changes.

## Boundary

This finding does not claim that data centers caused the projected bill changes,
that the tariff failed, or that public participation was ineffective. It says
only that the current public record closes governance and modeled-allocation
stages more strongly than household incidence, local acceptance, or
replaceability.
