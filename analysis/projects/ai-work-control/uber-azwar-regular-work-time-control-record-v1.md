# Uber / Azwar regular-work and time-control record v1

**Status:** primary adjudicated eligibility record; no merits or remedy outcome
**Checked:** 2026-09-16

## Why this record matters

This case adds a precise time-and-access boundary to the platform-work atlas.
The worker argued that time logged into the Uber Driver App and waiting for a
trip was work because he had to remain available; the platform counted only
trip time for which he was entitled to payment. The Fair Work Commission
rejected the logged-on-hours argument for the 60-hours-per-month test, but
found that the worker nevertheless performed paid work on a regular basis
because he worked on average on at least three days per week.

The case therefore separates three things that are often collapsed:

```text
account access / logged-on availability
  != legally countable paid-work time
  != regular work pattern for protection from deactivation
```

## Source and scope

The Fair Work Commission decided *Umair Azwar v Rasier Pacific Pty Ltd*
(`[2025] FWC 2370`, `UDE2025/50`). The decision, as typographically amended
on 3 September 2025, concerns a jurisdictional objection—not whether the
deactivation was fair on the merits.

Azwar performed Uber Eats work from October 2022 until September 2024. He
created an Uber Driver App account on 12 September 2024, first performed work
through it on 15 September, was suspended on 4 April 2025, and was finally
deactivated on 11 April 2025.

## Evidence matrix

| Stage | What the decision establishes | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Platform entry | The worker accepted a services agreement with Rasier Pacific and Uber Technologies Inc. and began Driver App work | This is one proceeding; it does not establish platform-wide entry patterns |
| Available time | The worker reported approximately 400 logged-on hours across October 2024–March 2025 and described waiting time as essential availability | The worker’s online-hours calculation was self-reported and not accepted as paid-work time under the governing rule |
| Platform measure | The respondent’s trip-log analysis found 33.7 average paid hours per month over the activation period and 34.6 hours over the six months before deactivation | Trip duration captures paid work under the statutory definition but not necessarily the full economic opportunity cost of being available |
| Work pattern | Chambers’ trip-log analysis found an average of 4.76 paid-work days per week across 29 weeks, and 4.33 days per week over the six-month period | A work-day pattern is not an hourly wage, income, profitability, or household-security measure |
| Eligibility | The Commission held that one paid trip on each of an average of three days per week satisfied the Code’s regular-work example, even though the 60-hour monthly threshold was not met | Eligibility for protection is not a finding that deactivation was unfair |
| Remedy | The matter was to proceed to case management after the jurisdictional objection was dismissed | No merits finding, reactivation order, lost-pay order, receipt, alternative work, or exit outcome is established here |

## Interpretation for the broad atlas

The case reveals a form of platform dependence that a paid-hours measure can
understate. A worker may devote time to being available without that time
qualifying as paid work. At the same time, a low volume of paid hours can still
form a repetitive work pattern sufficient to reach a legal protection gate.
This matters for the broad question of practical alternatives: “not paid while
waiting” does not mean “no time or control was committed,” while “protected
from deactivation” does not mean the worker has recovered income or gained a
usable alternative.

The Commission’s analysis also demonstrates that a threshold can be a safe
harbor rather than a complete definition. The three-days-per-week pattern was
enough even though the worker fell well short of 60 paid hours per month. The
atlas should therefore retain both continuous measures and patterned
participation measures when studying access, dependence, and work control.

## Decisive next test

Pair platform trip logs with logged-on time, offered/declined trips, earnings
after expenses, other work, schedule constraints, and a later access or
deactivation outcome. The key test is whether unpaid availability reduces
practical alternatives and household room even when the worker satisfies a
legal regular-work threshold.
