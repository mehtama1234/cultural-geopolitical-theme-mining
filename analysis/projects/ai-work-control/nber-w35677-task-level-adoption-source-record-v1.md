# NBER W35677 source record: task-level generative-AI adoption

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Project:** AI, work, and control  
**Source type:** NBER working paper and nationally representative worker survey  
**Evidence status:** full-paper descriptive review completed; raw index files, data documentation, and detailed subgroup review remain open

## Source

[What Work Does Generative AI Do?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35677), Alexander
Bick, Adam Blandin, David J. Deming, and Tyler R. Schumacher, NBER Working
Paper 35677, issue date August 2026.

## What the source measures

The paper links reported generative-AI use to detailed occupations and tasks in
a nationally representative survey. It constructs task-level adoption indexes
and distinguishes those indexes from measures built from platform chat logs.

The useful unit is the worker-task relationship, with occupation and task
context. This adds a layer between broad occupational exposure and firm-level
adoption: workers doing similar work may still use the technology differently.

## Directly reported findings

- The pooled occupation comparison covers 13,920 employed RPS respondents ages
  18–64 across four waves; the worker-task regression sample contains 55,634
  person-task observations.
- Generative AI is used across many occupations and tasks.
- Adoption is widespread but shallow: within most occupations and tasks, fewer
  than half of workers adopt.
- Exposure scores explain some, but not all, of the variation in adoption
  across occupations and tasks.
- Platform chat-log activity and worker-reported task adoption are conceptually
  different measures and can classify activities differently.

## Why it matters to the project

This source supports a more precise version of the working theme that work can
become reorganized or made legible before average pay or employment changes.
It suggests that the relevant question is not simply which occupations are
“exposed” to AI, but which workers adopt, for which tasks, under what local
rules, and with what consequences for discretion, skill, evaluation, and
opportunity.

It also supplies a counterweight to broad adoption headlines: high aggregate
use can coexist with shallow and uneven adoption among workers performing
similar tasks.

## What it does not establish

- It does not by itself show that AI caused a change in pay, hours, promotion,
  job quality, bargaining power, or worker wellbeing.
- It does not establish that workers who adopt receive the same tool access,
  training, managerial support, or decision rights.
- It does not connect worker adoption to a particular employer rule, household
  outcome, cultural meaning, political response, or state dependence.
- A nationally representative survey does not replace detailed workplace,
  administrative, or longitudinal evidence.

## Placement in the evidence chain

```text
occupation/task exposure
  -> worker adoption varies within similar work
  -> workplace rules and support may shape the use
  -> discretion, skill, pace, evaluation, and responsibility may change
  -> pay, status, trust, household security, and bargaining may change
```

The source directly informs the first two stages. The later stages remain open
tests requiring worker-level longitudinal evidence, workplace rules, and
outcome measures.

## Required follow-up

1. Read the full paper and appendices for sample construction, question wording,
   weighting, task coding, missingness, and uncertainty.
2. Compare adoption within occupations by age, education, gender, disability,
   race, contract type, industry, and employer size where the data permit.
3. Pair adoption with evidence on training, monitoring, autonomy, correction,
   promotion, pay, health, and worker voice.
4. Compare worker-reported adoption with firm disclosures and field experiments
   without treating either source as a universal adoption or outcome rate.

## Claim-register entry

**Bounded claim:** In a nationally representative worker survey, generative-AI
use spans many occupations and tasks but remains shallow within most of them,
so occupation-level exposure does not identify which workers adopt or what
their work outcomes become.

**Status:** observed/reported from the full working paper; index-file and subgroup review open.  
**Theme links:** work, control, unequal exposure, platforms/data, firm power.  
**Missing join:** worker adoption → changed decision rights or worker outcome.
