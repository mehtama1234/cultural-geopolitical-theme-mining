# World Bank WDR 2026 AI capability, complements, and governance layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Source:** World Bank, *World Development Report 2026: The Promise of
Artificial Intelligence*  
**Unit:** report framework, chapter synthesis, and cited comparative cases  
**Status:** reported framework and policy analysis; not a US causal estimate

## Why this source belongs in the atlas

The World Bank report provides the clearest source-specific account in the
current program of why access to AI is not the same as capability, and why
capability is not the same as sovereignty or public benefit. Its developing-
country perspective is useful for the US-centered atlas because it makes the
value chain visible: infrastructure, chips, data, models, applications,
skills, institutions, procurement, and regulation.

The report organizes the path as **adopt, adapt, and advance**. Adoption uses
existing tools. Adaptation fits them to local languages, institutions, data,
and needs. Advancement builds frontier models and the infrastructure behind
them. Each step requires more capital, skills, infrastructure, and institutional
capacity.

## What the report directly establishes

The report describes AI as diffusing faster than earlier general-purpose
technologies: roughly 80 years for the steam engine, 40 for electricity, and 20
for the internet to reach lower-income countries, while middle-income
countries accounted for half of ChatGPT's global traffic within six months of
launch. These are comparative report claims, not a common diffusion estimator.

It identifies three conditions that determine whether access becomes useful:

1. **Capabilities:** AI can perform or assist cognitive tasks where expertise is
   scarce, including health, agriculture, education, and administration.
2. **Concentration:** advanced models, chips, and data centers are controlled by
   a small number of companies and countries, creating dependency risks while
   also lowering the cost of accessing existing capability.
3. **Complements:** reliable electricity and internet, education, institutions,
   data, local-language fit, capital, and experimentation capacity determine
   whether AI can be adopted and adapted productively.

The report says AI's economic, social, and political effects are not guaranteed
to distribute evenly across geographic areas and population segments. It also
identifies the lack of evidence for selecting, procuring, and evaluating
front-end public-service applications as a constraint on public benefit.

| Report layer | What it adds | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Adopt/adapt/advance | A staged capability framework | Which US firm, agency, or state has completed a stage |
| Value-chain concentration | A mechanism for dependency and entry-cost tradeoffs | A US supplier concentration ratio or switching-cost estimate |
| Complements | Infrastructure, skill, data, language, and institutional prerequisites | That a particular complement caused a measured productivity gain |
| Public-service use | Examples and procurement/evaluation requirements | Improved citizen outcomes or equal access |
| Political power | Relationships among states, firms, governments, and people | An observed change in US leverage, legitimacy, or alliance behavior |

## The deeper interpretation: imported capability can be useful and dependent

The report rejects two simple stories. A country need not build a trillion-dollar
frontier model to benefit from AI, but importing a tool is not enough. The
useful middle is adaptation: making the tool work with local language,
institutions, data, and constraints. This is an important cultural and social
point, not merely an engineering one. A system that does not understand local
practice can produce a technically available service that is socially unusable
or normatively misaligned.

At the same time, the report treats concentration as ambivalent. The same
concentration that creates dependence can make powerful tools affordable to
places that could not build them. The relevant question is therefore not
“domestic or foreign” in the abstract. It is whether the actor can inspect,
adapt, bargain, switch providers, protect data, and maintain service when the
provider or infrastructure changes terms.

This connects directly to the program's unequal-optionality finding. A state,
agency, firm, or household with alternatives can use imported capability as a
choice. One without alternatives may accept a system's data rules, model
limits, energy demands, or operating cost as a condition of participation.

## Public institutions are both users and governors

The report assigns governments three roles: enablers, users, and regulators.
Enablers build analog and digital foundations. Users employ purchasing power to
test and scale applications in public services. Regulators develop standards,
apply existing law, address privacy and cybersecurity harms, and cooperate
internationally.

That three-role structure matters for the US program because public capacity is
not only a budget line or an AI deployment count. It includes staff who can
evaluate systems, procurement rules that preserve contestability, data that can
be used responsibly, and institutions that can correct errors. A government can
have an AI pilot and still lack the capacity to supervise it.

The report also warns that investment incentives can squeeze public budgets
while offering uncertain returns. This is the public version of a household
tradeoff: a visible capability gain can coexist with a less visible transfer of
cost, risk, or control.

## Political and geopolitical meaning

The report identifies power asymmetries among countries, governments,
corporations, and individuals. It names dependency on frontier models, chips,
and data centers; concentration of capital and market power; surveillance and
propaganda; and limited regulatory capacity. These are mechanisms to measure,
not completed geopolitical findings.

For the atlas, the relevant sequence is:

```text
AI value-chain concentration
  -> local complement and provider constraints
  -> adoption, adaptation, or public procurement choice
  -> service, labor, fiscal, privacy, or energy outcome
  -> switching, regulation, bargaining, or external response
```

Only the last stage establishes leverage. A country may depend on an external
provider without changing a negotiation, and a state may own infrastructure
without being able to operate or replace it. The program must therefore pair
the World Bank framework with actual ownership, contract, load, procurement,
service, and response records.

## Counterinterpretations

- Imported tools may expand access to expertise rather than weaken sovereignty.
- Local adaptation can improve fit but may introduce cost, fragmentation, or
  new data risks.
- Public procurement can create local capability, but buying locally does not
  guarantee quality, scale, or contestability.
- Concentration can reduce entry costs while still raising dependency risk.
- A reported public-service application may be useful in a pilot but fail at
  scale because evaluation, staffing, or infrastructure is missing.

## What remains open

The report does not provide a US-wide measure of AI capability, a firm-level
adoption estimate, a household outcome, a public-service quality result, or an
observed geopolitical concession. Its report-level claims also combine
different countries, sectors, and evidence types. The World Bank
reproducibility package is valuable for tracing chapter methods and outputs,
but includes mixed data-access conditions and does not turn the framework into
a common respondent-level dataset.
