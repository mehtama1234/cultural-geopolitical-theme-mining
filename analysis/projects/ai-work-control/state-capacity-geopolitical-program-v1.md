# State capacity, procurement, and geopolitical leverage

**Status:** reader synthesis of the current geopolitical lane · **Checked:** 2026-09-15  
**Scope:** defense resources, fiscal trade-offs, industrial networks, infrastructure dependence, alliance implementation, and observed external response

## The question

When a state spends more, signs a contract, builds an industrial partnership,
or hosts a strategically important infrastructure system, what has actually
changed? The answer may be a budget signal, a procurement obligation, a
supplier relationship, a production capability, an alliance commitment, or
real leverage. Those are different stages. The program follows them without
promoting an announcement into capability or capability into influence.

```text
threat, alliance commitment, or strategic objective
        ↓
budget allocation and financing
        ↓
contract obligation, supplier network, and ownership
        ↓
production, workforce, delivery, maintenance, and interoperability
        ↓
credible continuation, substitution, refusal, or retaliation
        ↓
changed negotiation, alliance terms, deterrence, or external behavior
```

The central rule is simple: **leverage requires an observed option and an
observed response.** Spending, a signed agreement, local assembly, or official
language can make an option plausible; none proves that another actor changed
behavior because of it.

## What the current evidence measures

### Resource allocation: the scale of the bet

The [SIPRI state-capacity record](../../records/us-sipri-military-expenditure-2025-state-capacity.json)
places the current defense allocation in global and alliance context. It shows
the scale and direction of military spending, including rising European and
Asian expenditure alongside the US vintage used in the atlas. This is the
resource stage: it identifies what governments allocate, not what forces can
field, maintain, replace, or use.

The [IMF geopolitical/fiscal layer](imf-weo-geopolitical-fiscal-shock-2026-layer-v1.md)
adds the macro constraint. In its cross-country defense-boom analysis, higher
defense outlays can be deficit-financed and associated with higher debt, while
wartime episodes can coincide with lower social spending. Those are historical
and conditional patterns, not a forecast for every state. Growth, debt, and
defense allocation therefore cannot be collapsed into a single “state power”
score.

The first domestic implication is distributional: a state can increase
strategic resources while households, firms, regions, or public services face
different prices, taxes, debt service, labor demand, or opportunity costs. The
next step is always to identify the actual budget line and its incidence.

### Procurement: an obligation, not yet a usable system

The [defense resource-to-capability finding](findings/ai-work-control-048.md)
follows one bounded US award and a Poland-linked procurement route across
SIPRI, IMF, USAspending, and official government announcements. The
[JASSM/LRASM award detail](usaspending-jassm-lrasm-award-detail-v1.md) records
obligations, modifications, subawards, program labels, and place fields. The
subaward extract contains named recipients and reported amounts, including
component-description structure.

This is valuable because it turns an abstract spending number into a map of
entities and possible dependencies. It is not a complete supply chain. A
returned recipient is not automatically a producing facility; a subaward is not
proof of delivery; a prime's ownership label cannot simply be copied onto each
subordinate entity; and repeated or administrative records can make a network
appear more or less diversified than the physical production system.

The correct question is not “how many suppliers are listed?” It is “which
critical input, design authority, facility, software, certification, or export
permission could stop the system—and how costly would switching be?”

### Industrial cooperation: implementation language creates tests

The [Poland reciprocity and co-production finding](findings/ai-work-control-042.md)
records official 2026 announcements describing buyer/seller access,
interoperability, regional cooperation, assembly followed by production,
technology or know-how transfer, financing, and local competence-building.
These statements matter because they move beyond a buyer-only signal and
describe a possible route to local industrial capacity.

They still remain agreement- or plan-stage evidence until the atlas observes a
commissioned line, completed units, named supplier contracts, actual local
content, maintenance authority, component control, delivery, or a customer
that can switch or refuse. Local assembly can coexist with external control of
design, software, tooling, certification, finance, export approval, or critical
components.

The [infrastructure realization finding](findings/ai-work-control-036.md)
uses the same discipline for strategic digital infrastructure: a milestone or
ownership arrangement can show implementation progress while leaving service
quality, application migration, portability, local incidence, and control
rights open.

### The latest realization-stage update: activity is becoming more concrete

The JASSM/LRASM case has now moved beyond a pure announcement story. The
[production-support finding](findings/ai-work-control-080.md) records a July
2025 DoD production-support and sustainment IDIQ, a reported 225,000-square-
foot intelligent factory, and completion of a first LRASM F-35C flight-science
phase. These observations establish support commitment, capacity investment,
and integration testing. They do not establish accepted quantities, customer
delivery, operational inventory, or strategic leverage.

The Poland route remains deliberately separate. The [delivery
watchpoint](findings/ai-work-control-081.md) finds a 2024 JASSM-ER agreement and
planned delivery windows, but no reviewed official public record of quantity,
acceptance, fielding, training, or inventory change for that order. The
[NIK control finding](findings/ai-work-control-082.md) shows why production
settlement documentation can be incomplete, while the [FMS timing
finding](findings/ai-work-control-083.md) shows that schedule, physical
delivery, acceptance, and accounting settlement can sit on different clocks.

The reusable [strategic capability realization ledger protocol](realization-ledger-protocol-v1.md)
turns that distinction into an event contract. It requires separate rows for
authorization, agreement, production, shipment, acceptance, training,
inventory, operational use, and external response, with source locators,
date precision, quantities, actors, and bounded non-observation. It is the
promotion gate for the next delivery or leverage claim.

This is not a contradiction. It is a more precise realization ledger:

```text
award / agreement
  -> production support and capacity investment       [observed]
  -> integration testing                               [observed]
  -> physical shipment or delivery                     [case-specific]
  -> acceptance, training, and inventory                [open for Poland order]
  -> operational availability                           [open]
  -> changed alliance or external behavior              [open]
```

The comparator evidence also prevents a false negative: a missing settlement
form cannot prove that no equipment was delivered. Conversely, a delivery
notice cannot by itself prove acceptance, readiness, maintainability, or
geopolitical leverage.

The data-center case shows the same separation in a domestic infrastructure
setting. LBNL estimates 176 TWh of US data-center electricity use in 2023 and
models 649 TWh in its 2030 reference case, with a 521–843 TWh sensitivity
range. Prince William County reports $465.9 million in 2025 data-center tax
revenue, 621 MW of new capacity, and 1,483 MW of turnkey powered-shell
capacity. Virginia large-load filings separately show modeled residential
incidence illustrations of $0.94–$2.90 per month at 1,000 kWh, while reported
large-load drop events reached 1,551 MW and 1,800 MW in two separate events.

These figures belong to different stages and denominators. The modeled
national load is not observed demand; county revenue is not household net
benefit; a filed residential illustration is not an observed bill; and a load
drop is not by itself a reliability failure or a permanent loss. The bounded
insight is narrower but useful: strategic infrastructure can create local
fiscal capacity while also creating a governance problem about who pays for
capacity, who bears interruption risk, and who can inspect or renegotiate the
terms. Public benefit and state leverage remain open until customer, utility,
provider-control, and observed policy-response evidence are joined.

## The stage map

| Stage | Current evidence | Safe interpretation | Missing evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allocation | SIPRI military-expenditure estimates; IMF fiscal/macro layer | States are assigning resources under fiscal and geopolitical constraints | Actual budget composition, readiness, household/service incidence |
| Contract | USAspending award, modification, subaward, and recipient records | A bounded procurement obligation and supplier map exist | Complete tier map, competition, termination, performance, and delivery |
| Ownership and place | DLA CAGE and public entity records for prime/parent/facility relationships | Legal-entity and jurisdictional chains can be traced | Facility-level assignment, workforce, inputs, and output |
| Production and integration | Official production targets, tests, lot notices, and implementation plans | Capability-building activity and intended milestones are visible | Commissioned production, accepted units, inventory, maintenance, and interoperability |
| Alliance response | Poland-US procurement, financing, reciprocity, and regional cooperation announcements | Governments are creating commitments and implementation expectations | Delivery, fielding, local control, switching, and behavior after capability exists |
| Leverage | No promoted end-state yet | The necessary response test is specified | Observed negotiation, refusal, substitution, deterrence, retaliation, or concession |

## The most important distinctions

- **Spending is not readiness.** Money may be delayed by procurement,
  maintenance, integration, workforce, or supply constraints.
- **A contract is not delivery.** Award actions and planned windows do not
  establish accepted or fielded quantities.
- **A supplier list is not redundancy.** Multiple legal entities may share a
  parent, facility, design, component, or export dependency.
- **Co-production is not autonomy.** Local assembly can coexist with external
  control of software, certification, finance, or critical inputs.
- **Financing is not capability.** A foreign-military-financing agreement can
  accelerate purchases without creating local production or bargaining power.
- **Strategic language is not public reception.** Security, dignity, industrial
  revival, dependence, burden, and opportunity require resident, worker, firm,
  and voter evidence—not only official statements.
- **Capability is not leverage.** Leverage requires a credible option and a
  documented external actor whose behavior changes in response.

## Domestic incidence and cultural meaning

The geopolitical lane is not separate from the societal atlas. Defense and
strategic infrastructure can create wages, supplier demand, skills, and local
pride; they can also create public cost, environmental burden, import exposure,
service trade-offs, surveillance, or concentrated risk. The same project can be
read as protection by one community, dependence by another, and a fiscal burden
by a third.

The current evidence does not yet join those meanings to the procurement case.
The missing bridge requires a named place and time, local employment and public
cost, exposure to prices or services, attribution to an institution, and later
trust, organizing, electoral action, or exit. Without that bridge, “national
security” remains an official rationale rather than a measured societal effect.

## The next decisive case test

The program should follow one weapon system or critical infrastructure case
through a complete realization ledger:

1. objective, threat, alliance commitment, budget line, and financing source;
2. contract, modifications, prime, subawards, ownership, facility, and
   critical-input map;
3. workforce, training, plant capacity, production start, and completed units;
4. acceptance, delivery, maintenance, inventory, integration, and operational
   availability;
5. local wages, public cost, environmental burden, household/service exposure,
   and public attribution;
6. alternative suppliers, portability, termination rights, and switching cost;
7. partner-state or external-actor negotiation, refusal, substitution,
   concession, or other observed response; and
8. a counterexample with similar exposure but greater supplier diversity,
   public control, local ownership, or a successful switch.

The Poland JASSM-ER route is not yet at the delivery/acceptance endpoint: the
public record contains planned windows and schedule-vintage differences. The
Barracuda and related co-production route is not yet at the commissioned-line
endpoint. Those are not failures of the program; they are explicit watchpoints
that prevent the atlas from calling intention, financing, or agreement
“leverage.”

## Reader's bottom line

The current geopolitical insight is a chain-of-stages insight. States are
allocating resources, placing contracts, mapping industrial dependencies, and
announcing reciprocal production ambitions. The evidence is strong enough to
study capability formation and domestic trade-offs. It is not yet strong enough
to claim realized operational capacity, strategic autonomy, or changed
geopolitical leverage.

The next meaningful advance is not another aggregate spending total. It is a
verified delivery or production record tied to a facility, workforce, input,
local incidence, replaceability, and an observed external response.
