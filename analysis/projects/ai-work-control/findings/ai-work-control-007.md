# Finding 007: Flexibility is not the same as freedom

**Status:** provisional  
**Last checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Project:** AI, work, and control

## Finding

Platform work can give people more control over when they log in while leaving them constrained by safety, household time, assets, location, customer behavior, and the platform’s rules. The limits are especially visible for women in India and Indonesia. At the country level, AI opportunity also depends on infrastructure: exposure to a tool is not the same as the ability to use it.

## What we directly know

- An NBER study using survey and administrative data from major two-wheeler platforms finds very low female participation in India and Indonesia. Women who enter work fewer hours, value flexibility and supplemental earnings, and face distinct safety, harassment, discrimination, accident, and hourly-earning gaps. [NBER Working Paper 35467](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35467)
- The World Bank and ILO estimate that 30–40% of jobs in Latin America and the Caribbean have some generative-AI exposure. They estimate that 8–12% of jobs could gain productivity, but that up to half of those potential gains may be blocked by inadequate digital infrastructure. [World Bank/ILO LAC AI analysis](https://www.worldbank.org/en/results/2025/04/15/quantifying-the-jobs-potential-of-ai-in-latin-america-and-the-caribbean)

## Causal chain under test

```text
platform or AI creates flexible access -> worker chooses when and where to work
-> choice is limited by safety, care, assets, infrastructure, and allocation rules
-> access and returns differ by gender, place, and household
-> “flexibility” can coexist with dependence and unequal bargaining power
```

The evidence supports the existence of different constraints. It does not show that the platform caused every gender or regional difference.

## Cultural and social meaning

Flexibility is often described as freedom. For many workers it may instead mean fitting paid work around unpaid care, safety concerns, or a lack of other jobs. The same work system can be liberating for one person and limiting for another.

This matters for AI because systems often treat the available worker, device, vehicle, connection, or time window as a neutral input. Those inputs are shaped by household roles, wealth, gender, local safety, and public infrastructure.

## Four-map reading

| Map | First signal | Missing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Material | access, hours, earnings, infrastructure, and assets shape returns | household income, costs, insurance, and long-run mobility |
| Social | flexibility interacts with care, safety, discrimination, and gender norms | worker voice and within-household decisions |
| Institution | platforms and states set access and protection rules | enforcement, classification, social protection, appeals |
| Power | platform allocation meets unequal outside options | data ownership, exit, collective bargaining, public infrastructure |

## Strongest challenge

The India and Indonesia study is descriptive and concerns platform driving, while the World Bank/ILO figures are modeled regional estimates. Neither is a direct test of generative AI in ordinary employment. Their value is to show why access, place, and household conditions must be part of the model.

## What would change our mind

- Evidence that flexible platform work produces similar access and returns across gender, household, and safety conditions.
- Evidence that infrastructure gaps do not change the ability to turn AI exposure into productivity.
- Evidence that workers can freely reject platform assignments without losing future access or income.

## Next tests

1. Add worker-level studies from Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia.
2. Track platform rules for assignment, pricing, deactivation, and appeals.
3. Compare workers who own assets and connections with workers who rent or share them.
4. Add household care, safety, and social-protection measures.
5. Test whether AI tools expand choice or simply optimize around existing constraints.
