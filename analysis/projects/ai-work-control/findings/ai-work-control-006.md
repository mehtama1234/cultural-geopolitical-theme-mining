# Finding 006: Technology becomes a worker benefit only through the social bargain

**Status:** provisional  
**Last checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Project:** AI, work, and control

## Finding

Two non-Western studies show why “technology adoption” is too broad a measure. In Indian garment factories, a communication tool did nothing by itself but improved productivity and earnings when paired with better HR incentives. In a Chinese food-delivery platform, an AI text-to-speech tool helped deaf or hard-of-hearing workers and closed part of their hourly-pay gap.

The tool matters. But the result also depends on the surrounding deal: who can use it, what problem it solves, who responds to worker information, and how the gain is shared.

## What we directly know

- A randomized trial in Indian garment factories found no effect from an anonymous worker-management communication technology alone. Pairing the technology with incentives for HR managers increased productivity by 5%, reduced absenteeism by 13%, and raised worker earnings by 3%. The effects were driven by more responsive HR and more worker reporting of production problems. [NBER Working Paper 35445](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35445)
- A study of a major Chinese food-delivery platform found that deaf or hard-of-hearing workers initially had slower deliveries and worse customer ratings but worked more, quit less, and generated higher platform profits. After an AI text-to-speech tool was introduced, deliveries, ratings, labor supply, retention, and profits improved, and the tool closed one-third of the disability hourly-pay gap. [NBER Working Paper 35372](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372)

## Causal chain under test

```text
worker barrier or missing information -> tool is introduced
-> managers, workers, or customers can respond better
-> work becomes more accessible or more coordinated
-> output and worker outcomes can improve
-> the gain depends on incentives, access, and who captures it
```

The two studies support the importance of fit and organizational response. They do not show that every AI tool improves inclusion or pay.

## Why this matters

This is the project’s clearest warning against treating adoption as a standalone event. A tool can be technically capable but socially useless if no one responds to the information it produces. A tool can also be valuable because it removes a barrier that a broad productivity average would miss.

The cultural question is who is treated as part of the normal work system. The same system can make a previously excluded worker more able to participate, or make an already-visible worker more closely watched.

## Four-map reading

| Map | First signal | Missing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Material | productivity, absenteeism, retention, profits, and pay can move together | long-run pay, workload, and distribution of gains |
| Social | communication and accessibility change who can participate | dignity, belonging, status, and worker voice |
| Institution | HR response and platform design shape the result | rules for consultation, correction, and appeals |
| Power | the firm decides whether information is acted on and how gains are shared | bargaining, ownership, and worker control |

## Strongest challenge

Neither study is a general test of workplace AI. One studies a communication intervention in Indian factories; the other studies a specific accessibility tool on a Chinese platform. Their value is in showing mechanisms and neglected groups, not in providing a universal effect size.

## What would change our mind

- Evidence that organizational incentives do not change the effect of workplace technology.
- Evidence that accessibility tools improve platform outcomes without improving worker outcomes.
- Evidence that the same design works equally well for workers with different barriers, jobs, and bargaining positions.

## Next tests

1. Find worker-level evidence on AI training, pay, promotion, and assignment by disability, gender, age, and contract type.
2. Compare tools that remove barriers with tools that evaluate or discipline.
3. Test whether worker reporting leads to management action or merely creates more data.
4. Add Latin American, African, and Southeast Asian workplace evidence.
5. Track who captures productivity gains when inclusion also raises firm profit.
