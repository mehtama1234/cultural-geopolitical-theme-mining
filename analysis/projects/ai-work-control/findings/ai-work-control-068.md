# Finding 068: Worker voice is the conversion point between AI exposure and control

**Status:** provisional comparative worker-control finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-14

## The bounded finding

The current evidence supports a sharper end-to-end question than “does AI
help or hurt workers?” The important question is whether a change in work is
converted into a change in control.

The JRC AIM-WORK survey supplies worker-side evidence: algorithmic management
is associated, overall, with less autonomy, less ability to take breaks, and
more work stress, with direct direction of tasks and pace showing the strongest
negative pattern. The ILO case studies supply the institutional hinge: workers
sometimes changed consent rules, monitoring limits, human oversight, workflow
discretion, pay, or legal protection—but some challenges remained unresolved.

Read against the US NBER/OECD/BLS layer, this produces a useful architecture:

```text
AI assistance or algorithmic direction
  -> exposure, pace, monitoring, and discretion
  -> worker information and collective voice
  -> changed rule, appeal, consent, workload, or remedy
  -> durable household, institutional, and political consequence
```

The first two arrows are increasingly measurable. The conversion from voice to
enforceable control, and the later household and political consequences, are
not yet established in one US workplace event.

The archived 2020 IBM Central Holding GmbH group agreement makes that
conversion problem more concrete. It turns several abstract stages into
written, inspectable rules: a human must make the final decision; AI systems
receive a five-level risk classification; the highest-risk class is prohibited;
higher-risk systems require more transparency; false recommendations must have
a correction route; and the works council receives review and inspection
rights. This is institutional-design evidence, not proof that the rules were
applied or effective.

The OECD's 2025 worker-consultation experiment adds a small but more direct
design-negotiation layer. Across three German manufacturing firms and 16
participants, workers, managers, and works-council representatives discussed
simulated algorithmic-management features. Discussion reduced the number of
controversial firm-feature assessments from 17 of 24 to 4 of 24, but only one
of the three groups reached agreement on all eight features. The other two
groups retained disagreements centered on worker autonomy. The expected
productivity and job-quality gains were participant expectations, not realized
production or worker outcomes.

## What the two comparative sources add

| Evidence surface | What it observes | What it does not observe |
|---|---|---|
| JRC AIM-WORK | 70,316 working-age respondents across the 27 EU Member States; about one third report work-related AI use; automated allocation of shifts or time is prominent; algorithmic-management practices are associated with autonomy, break, stress, and intensity differences. | A matched pre/post workplace, a single vendor’s causal effect, enforcement, bargaining, or whether a worker can refuse or appeal. |
| ILO social-dialogue cases | Five illustrative cases show bargaining, works-council review, system-data use, public action, and legal challenge reaching consent, pay, monitoring, oversight, workflow discretion, training, or protection. | A representative effect, durability after deployment or vendor change, common denominators, or proof that every consultation changed power. |
| IBM Germany group agreement | A 2020 written rule specifies human final decisions, risk categories, a prohibited highest-risk class, transparency, error correction, fairness review, an AI Ethics Council, and works-council inspection rights. | System coverage, implementation, enforcement, worker experience, correction rates, and employment outcomes. |
| OECD worker-consultation experiment | A small German manufacturing laboratory experiment observes workers, managers, and works-council representatives negotiating eight simulated design features; autonomy conflicts remain in two of three groups. | A 16-person comparative experiment with expected—not realized—productivity and job-quality outcomes; no US estimate or production deployment. |

These are deliberately different objects. A large worker survey can describe
the lived association between management practices and job quality. A case
table can show how collective action operates in particular institutions. One
cannot be used as the denominator for the other.

## The useful distinction: information, voice, control, remedy

“Worker voice” is too broad to carry the whole claim. The program should code
four separate stages:

1. **Information:** workers know a system exists, what data it uses, and what
   decision it influences.
2. **Voice:** workers or representatives can question, negotiate, organize,
   or supply counter-knowledge.
3. **Control:** that voice changes a deployment rule, data boundary, pace,
   workload, consent requirement, human review, or refusal right.
4. **Remedy:** a worker can correct an error, obtain relief, reverse a decision,
   or impose a cost when the rule is violated.

The ILO cases are valuable precisely because they occupy different cells. The
SAG-AFTRA case reaches consent and pay for digital replicas. The Culinary Union
case shows workers using backend data to challenge workflow quotas. The German
works-council cases show review, data limits, human oversight, retraining, and
job-security commitments. The HD Hyundai and Kenyan cases keep conflict,
subcontracting, privacy, legal process, and incomplete remedy visible.

None of this means that a signed agreement automatically produces good jobs.
It means that control has to be observed as a rule or remedy, not inferred from
consultation, governance language, productivity, or formal union coverage.

## What this changes in the US program

The existing US layer can now be read as a set of gates rather than one AI
story:

- NBER worker and task studies measure selected output, time, adoption, and
  performance changes.
- OECD employer data measure provision and manager-reported governance around
  algorithmic management.
- NBER executive data measure current use and expectations about future
  productivity and employment.
- BLS measures formal representation, a possible institution of voice.
- JRC measures worker-side job-quality associations in Europe.
- ILO cases show mechanisms by which voice may or may not become a changed
  rule.

The bridge is therefore not “AI causes worker empowerment” or “AI causes job
loss.” It is: **workplace technology creates a control problem whose outcome
depends on whether workers can convert information and collective action into
enforceable rights.**

## The IBM agreement supplies a coding template, not an outcome score

The IBM text is valuable for comparative extraction because it separates
controls that are often bundled together under “responsible AI”:

| Control to code | Written IBM provision | Implementation evidence still needed |
|---|---|---|
| Decision authority | AI prepares recommendations; a human retains the final decision | Whether the human can disagree, how disagreement is recorded, and whether workers can appeal |
| Risk gate | Categories 1–5 are linked to potential worker harm; category 5 personnel recommendations or automatic decisions without worker benefit are barred | System inventory, classifications, exceptions, and rejected or redesigned systems |
| Explanation | Higher-risk categories require information about inputs, influence, and confidence | Actual notices, accessibility, timing, vendor disclosures, and worker comprehension |
| Error route | A defined process must report and correct false recommendations; high-risk cases can reach the AI Ethics Council | Complaint volume, response time, correction logs, model changes, and repeat errors |
| Bias control | The employer must demonstrate fairness and non-discrimination testing | Test populations, metrics, thresholds, independent review, and post-deployment drift |
| Collective oversight | The works council can assess systems and inspect data-quality, non-bias, transparency, purpose, and objective information | Meeting records, technical access, bargaining outcomes, refusals, and enforcement |
| Employment transition | Equal work should be offered where possible and retraining provided when required by changed work | Offers, completion, pay and quality of replacement work, and excluded workers |

This template prevents a common analytical error: treating a policy's presence
as if it were a worker outcome. It also makes the next evidence request more
specific. A future US workplace case can be compared on the same dimensions
without assuming that a US employer, a German works council, and an EU survey
share the same legal or institutional setting.

The OECD experiment also supplies a useful non-binary outcome coding:

```text
consultation
  -> agreement unchanged
  -> agreement with added safeguards
  -> unresolved disagreement
  -> refusal or no deployment
```

That is more informative than coding consultation as simply present or absent.
It preserves cases where discussion narrows disagreement but does not produce
consent, and it makes autonomy a concrete design dimension rather than a broad
claim about worker sentiment.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A worker may know about a monitoring system and still have no meaningful
  ability to contest it.
- A governance policy may constrain data collection without changing pace or
  job security.
- A productivity gain may be captured by the firm or converted into higher
  targets rather than worker time.
- A collective agreement may protect one class of workers while excluding
  synthetic performers, subcontractors, migrants, or platform workers.
- A court may preserve interim protection without deciding the underlying
  merits; public conflict is not the same as final remedy.
- European associations and international cases illuminate mechanisms, but do
  not estimate US prevalence or US causal effects.

## Next decisive test

Select one US workplace technology and build a same-workplace event panel with
implementation date, worker exposure, training, monitoring, pace, hours, pay,
health, discretion, data visibility, appeal, representation, household-time
effects, and later enforcement. The decisive outcome is not merely adoption or
productivity. It is whether a worker or representative can change a rule or
obtain remedy—and whether that change persists after a manager, vendor, model,
or contract changes.
