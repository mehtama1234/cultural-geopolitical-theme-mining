# Finding 093: Legitimacy has three audiences before it becomes geopolitical power

**Status:** provisional international-reception and domestic-meaning synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

The retained Pew 2026 global-role layer shows that geopolitical legitimacy is
not one public mood. It has at least three audiences: people in other
countries, Americans as a national public, and American partisan/generational
groups. They can interpret the same U.S. conduct differently. Material
capability may remain intact while trust, cooperation, reputation, or domestic
political durability changes—or those legitimacy signals may move without an
observable change in capability.

The safe conclusion is:

> The United States has a measurable gap between domestic and foreign
> interpretations of its global role, plus sharp domestic partisan divergence.
> This identifies a legitimacy and meaning structure, not a measure of actual
> diplomatic power, alliance behavior, or geopolitical leverage.

## The chain under test

```text
foreign-policy, conflict, alliance, aid, trade, or rights event
  -> external public interpretation
  -> domestic partisan and generational interpretation
  -> respect, reliability, interference, freedom, and compromise judgment
  -> support, opposition, cooperation, reputation, or policy pressure
  -> institutional/diplomatic response and later capability or legitimacy
```

The survey observes the interpretation stage and some normative preferences.
It does not observe the same event causing a government decision, alliance
coordination, consumer choice, or changed external behavior.

## Evidence comparison

| Audience | Observed result | Safe interpretation | Still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-U.S. publics | Pew surveyed 42,151 adults in 36 countries; a median 35% said the U.S. contributes to peace and stability, while 63% said it does not | Foreign publics are a distinct reception environment | Whether views change aid acceptance, alliance coordination, markets, migration, tourism, or diplomatic behavior |
| Americans overall | 57% said the U.S. contributes to global peace and stability; 57% said the government respects personal freedoms | Domestic public judgment is more favorable than the foreign median on these measures | Whether favorable judgment translates into policy support, institutional permission, or actual foreign-policy durability |
| Republicans and Democrats | On peace/stability, Republicans were at 82% and Democrats at 35%; on whether the U.S. considers other countries’ interests, Republicans were about 70% versus Democrats at 24% | Partisan identity changes the meaning assigned to national strength, respect, and compromise | Media exposure, event attribution, policy preference, turnout/action, and later institutional response |
| Shared domestic judgment | 83% of Americans said the U.S. interferes in other countries’ affairs a great deal or fair amount, with 89% of Democrats and 77% of Republicans saying so | Polarization is not total; Americans can agree on a descriptive role while disagreeing about its legitimacy | Whether agreement produces a common policy demand or only a shared description |
| Normative audience | 91% said it is important that the U.S. be respected; 65% said the U.S. should consider other countries’ interests even if compromise is required | Respect and compromise are valued norms, not proof that current policy meets them | Which event, institution, or identity makes those norms politically actionable |

The international figures are equal-country medians, not population-weighted
global estimates. The U.S. supplement has a separate sample and field period.
Party, age, country, question wording, and event context are not interchangeable
denominators.

## Three audiences, three clocks

### External reception

Foreign publics judge whether the U.S. contributes to peace, considers their
interests, and respects freedoms. These judgments can affect the social and
political environment in which U.S. firms, media, aid, institutions, and
alliances operate. A negative judgment is not itself a diplomatic refusal; a
positive judgment is not proof of cooperation.

### Domestic partisan meaning

The large Republican–Democratic gaps show that “strength,” “respect,”
“interference,” and “compromise” are not merely factual labels. They are
interpreted through political identity. A policy can be read as restoring
influence by one group and violating legitimacy by another. The broad atlas
should therefore keep policy exposure, attribution, identity, judgment, and
action as separate fields.

### Institutional behavior

Governments, firms, allies, and publics make decisions on yet another clock.
Polling can reveal a legitimacy environment before any observable policy
change. Conversely, an alliance may coordinate despite poor public opinion, or
a government may change policy without an immediate public shift. The missing
bridge is an event-country-party-year record that connects interpretation to
behavior.

## Why the shared interference judgment matters

The 83% domestic judgment that the U.S. interferes in other countries' affairs
is a useful counterexample to a simple polarization story. Democrats and
Republicans are far apart on whether the U.S. contributes to peace and whether
it considers other countries' interests, but both majorities report a high
level of interference. That means common descriptive perception can coexist
with divergent normative evaluation.

This is analytically richer than assigning respondents to an
“internationalist” or “isolationist” axis. Someone may believe the U.S. is
highly influential, disapprove of the way it uses that influence, and still
support compromise. Another may see the same influence as evidence of strength
and reject compromise. The survey identifies the interpretive ingredients;
it does not tell us which combination produces action.

## Connection to material capability

This layer belongs beside—not on top of—the mineral, semiconductor, energy,
data-center, and defense realization layers. Those records track resources,
infrastructure, production, delivery, and dependence. Pew tracks perceived
legitimacy and reception. A country can possess a missile, chip plant, or power
system while losing public trust; it can also receive poor foreign ratings
without losing the ability to act.

The geopolitical question becomes testable only when a capability and a
legitimacy event share a time-ordered path:

```text
material capability or policy event
  -> foreign and domestic interpretation
  -> cooperation, refusal, market/cultural response, or political pressure
  -> state adjustment, alliance response, or continued operation
```

Do not call the first or second stage leverage. Leverage requires another actor
to change behavior because refusal, switching, retaliation, or continued
operation is credible.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Negative foreign opinion does not prove loss of military, financial, or
  diplomatic capability.
- Favorable domestic opinion does not prove successful policy or alliance
  cooperation.
- Partisan differences are descriptive; they do not identify media causation,
  event attribution, or a stable realignment.
- Agreement that the U.S. interferes does not reveal whether respondents judge
  interference good, bad, effective, or necessary.
- Normative support for respect or compromise is not a measure of later voting,
  organizing, consumer behavior, or government action.
- A country median gives surveyed countries equal weight and excludes many
  countries; the U.S. and international samples have different designs.

## Next decisive test

Build an event-country-party-year ledger containing:

```text
dated U.S. foreign-policy, conflict, alliance, aid, trade, or rights event
  -> external views of peace, reliability, interests, freedoms, and respect
  -> U.S. partisan/generational judgment and media framing
  -> alliance statement, aid acceptance, market/cultural signal, or policy action
  -> later reputation, cooperation, capability, trust, and domestic action
```

Compare countries with similar exposure but different alliance, regional,
economic, and historical positions. The minimum promotion event is not a
polling shift; it is an observed change in cooperation, refusal, policy,
market/cultural behavior, or institutional action tied to the dated event.
