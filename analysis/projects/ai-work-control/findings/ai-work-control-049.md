# Finding 049: Data-center capacity, public revenue, and utility governance do not identify household benefit

**Status:** provisional non-pooled infrastructure-to-governance bridge · **Checked:** 2026-09-14

## The bounded finding

The atlas can now follow the data-center pathway across four distinct evidence surfaces:

1. LBNL estimates national historical data-center electricity use and models a 2030 reference and sensitivity range.
2. Prince William County reports local data-center tax revenue, capacity additions, and facility counts.
3. Virginia, Texas, and Georgia records show how commissions are moving toward minimum charges, collateral, interconnection terms, cost allocation, reporting, and disclosure boundaries.
4. Virginia records report large-load operational stress events that make reliability and continuity testable.

The defensible synthesis is: **digital demand can become physical infrastructure demand, fiscal capacity, and regulatory risk allocation without becoming demonstrated household benefit or public control.** The chain is observable in stages, but the stages are not one dataset and do not prove a net local welfare effect.

## Evidence surfaces kept separate

| Stage | Direct evidence | Unit | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| National physical demand | LBNL estimates 176 TWh and 4.4% of implied US electricity in 2023; its 2030 reference case is 649 TWh and 11.8%, with a 521–843 TWh sensitivity range. | Historical estimate and modeled scenarios | Actual load, siting, water/land incidence, ownership, and capability |
| Local fiscal capacity | Prince William reports $465.9M in TY2025 data-center tax revenue, 59% year-over-year growth, 621 MW added, 1,483 MW turnkey capacity, and 55 facilities. | County industry aggregate | Service costs, household benefit, jobs/wages, environmental burden, and fiscal dependence |
| Utility/state governance | Virginia uses 85% minimum transmission/distribution charges and 14-year obligations for covered customers; Texas proposed 20-year interconnection terms; Georgia records requested cost/allocation data while a contract filing is publicly bounded as unexecuted and trade secret. | Approved, proposed, modeled, filed, and disclosure-stage policy records | Actual bills, defaults, final rules, contract terms, cost recovery, and trust |
| Operational stress | Virginia reports separate 1,551 MW and 1,800 MW large-load drop events. | Utility-reported event magnitudes | Duration, facility exposure, household effect, independent causality, and long-run resilience |

These objects are intentionally not pooled. A national scenario, county tax aggregate, state tariff term, and utility event do not share a denominator or outcome.

## The infrastructure-to-public-control chain under test

```text
digital demand and investment
  -> electricity, land, water, transmission, and construction demand
  -> utility investment and rate/risk allocation
  -> county revenue, services, household costs, and local exposure
  -> attribution, trust, contestation, and political action
  -> state bargaining power, provider replaceability, and geopolitical leverage
```

The current evidence reaches physical demand, fiscal capacity, governance design, and operational stress. It does not close the household, legitimacy, replaceability, or external-leverage nodes.

## What the comparison changes

### Capacity is not incidence

LBNL's 2030 values are useful for bounding the scale of a national infrastructure question. They are not an approved interconnection, actual operating load, or local AI-capability outcome. A scenario cannot tell us which communities bear electricity, water, land, noise, tax, or construction burdens.

### Revenue is not public benefit

Prince William's TY2025 revenue report establishes a large local fiscal inflow and a rapidly expanding physical base. It does not show whether revenue covers public-service costs, lowers other taxes, improves services, raises wages, or creates durable bargaining room. The reported 59% increase is also confounded by the equipment tax-rate change and assessment/completion cycles.

### Governance terms are risk-allocation instruments, not observed fairness

Virginia's minimum-charge and contract terms, Texas's proposed security and interconnection rules, and Georgia's data and disclosure questions make cost causation more testable. The Virginia $0.94 and $2.90 monthly figures are modeled/filed illustrations for a 1,000-kWh reference customer, not observed bills or data-center-only effects. Georgia's public filing boundary means the most consequential contract terms remain unavailable in the current record.

### Operational stress is a reliability signal, not a social outcome

The 1,551 MW and 1,800 MW reported load drops establish that large-load behavior enters system operations. Without duration, customer roster, feeder geography, and household or firm outcomes, they do not establish who lost service, who paid, or whether the system gained durable resilience.

## Counterexamples kept visible

- A national load projection can rise while actual projects are delayed, canceled, underutilized, or concentrated in a few places.
- A county can receive substantial tax revenue while residents face higher utility, housing, infrastructure, or environmental costs.
- A minimum-charge rule can reduce cross-subsidy risk while leaving public-service, water, land, and health costs outside the tariff.
- A filed residential bill illustration can change before approval and is not a causal household panel.
- A large-load drop can protect system reliability while disrupting a facility; the event alone does not identify the net welfare effect.
- Public hearing participation demonstrates an institutional route for contestation, not representative opinion, consensus, or causal political influence.
- Domestic data-center capacity can increase without proving domestic ownership, interoperability, provider replaceability, or state leverage.

## Next decisive test

Select one local load or transmission case and join, with explicit provenance:

1. facility/utility load, interconnection, and outage or curtailment records;
2. rate class, minimum bill, collateral, cost-of-service, and approved recovery;
3. county revenue, service expenditure, jobs/wages, housing, water, and land indicators;
4. household bills, firm continuity, resident exposure, and environmental measures;
5. public comments, organized action, attribution, trust, and remedy; and
6. ownership, critical inputs, switching cost, alternative providers, and any observed bargaining or external response.

Do not promote this bridge to “local benefit,” “public control,” or “geopolitical leverage” until a compatible place/customer panel observes incidence and a later institutional or behavioral response.
