# Global military spending rose as the US share fell and allied capacity accelerated

**Status:** provisional geopolitical state-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-13

## The bounded finding

SIPRI's 2025 vintage shows a changing distribution of military-resource
allocation. Global military expenditure rose 2.9% in real terms to $2.887
trillion, but US spending fell 7.5% to $954 billion while European spending
rose 14% to $864 billion and Asia/Oceania rose 8.1% to $681 billion. China
rose 7.4% to an estimated $336 billion. The US remained the largest spender,
but its share and lead over China narrowed.

This is evidence of changing budgetary context and potential alliance
burden-sharing pressure. It is not evidence by itself of greater operational
capability, successful deterrence, procurement delivery, or a shift in public
support.

## Direct measurements

| Measure | 2025 result | Boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| World spending | $2,887 billion; +2.9% real | Open-source monetary estimate; some country values estimated |
| World military burden | 2.5% of global GDP | Not a welfare or capability measure |
| United States | $954 billion; −7.5% real; still 2.8× China's spending | Decline partly reflects reduced supplemental overseas assistance; not a full capability inventory |
| China | Estimated $336 billion; +7.4% real | Exchange rates, classified spending, and estimates affect comparison |
| Europe | $864 billion; +14% real | Regional aggregate, not interoperable force output |
| NATO | $1,581 billion across 32 members; 55% of world total | Does not establish burden-sharing quality or readiness |

## End-to-end route under test

```text
threat and alliance pressure
  -> national budget and assistance choices
  -> procurement demand and industrial production
  -> readiness, interoperability, and state fiscal trade-offs
  -> public legitimacy and alliance behavior
  -> deterrence, dependence, or geopolitical leverage
```

SIPRI supports the budget-allocation stage. The program must join it to
procurement awards, delivery, production capacity, fiscal data, public
attitudes, and observed external decisions before making a state-capacity
claim.

## Counterexamples

- Higher spending can coexist with procurement delay, supply bottlenecks, or
  weak readiness.
- Lower US spending can reflect lower supplemental aid rather than broad
  reductions in strategic capability.
- Higher allied spending can fail to produce interoperable or politically
  durable capacity.
- A high military burden can indicate wartime necessity while reducing fiscal
  room for social spending, but SIPRI alone does not measure that displacement.

## Next test

Match the 2025 SIPRI vintage to USASpending awards and deliveries, supplier and
production records, IMF fiscal projections, energy/industrial capacity, public
legitimacy measures, and dated alliance or external responses. Retain states
where allocation rose without realized capability and where capability was
replaceable without a strategic concession.

**Evidence status:** current official SIPRI monetary context with explicit
estimation and capability limits; procurement, household, legitimacy, and
geopolitical-effect arrows remain open.
