# Finding 308: A transmission refund can show regulatory correction before household recovery

**Status:** bounded infrastructure/consumer-power finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The bounded finding

New York's Department of Public Service announced a settlement requiring
NextEra Energy Transmission New York to return more than $13 million, plus
interest, to New York ratepayers in 2026. The agency said its review found
apparently abnormal operating and maintenance expenses and costs for preparing
transmission proposals that had been collected from customers. The settlement
also requires future proposal costs to receive authorization before being
included in rates.

The route advanced in June 2026 when FERC formally instituted a Section 206
investigation in docket EL26-69-000 and established a refund-effective date
through the Federal Register notice. This is a real federal implementation
stage: the legal and accounting path for possible relief is active. It is
still not the same as a populated retail bill credit, cash payment, or verified
customer outcome.

A current NYSEG/RG&E pricing-page recheck exposes August 2026 recovery-charge
statements and other tariff materials, but does not identify a NextEra
settlement credit, affected customer class, bill period, or credited amount.
This is a useful negative check: the utility-facing rate surface exists, while
the settlement-specific retail implementation remains unobserved.

An implementation explanation hosted by the Village of Skaneateles adds the
allocation mechanics: the expected refund would appear as credits to monthly
transmission charges on a load-ratio-share basis, but individual systems might
not see a separately itemized invoice credit. This sharpens the route from
regulatory correction to wholesale/customer-class allocation without proving
that a retail customer saw or received the benefit.

This is a distinct middle stage from infrastructure construction: a regulator
identified a cost-governance problem and created a future customer-refund route.
It still does not show which customers receive a credit or payment, when it
appears on a bill, whether it is adequate, or whether the correction changes
trust, switching, or household affordability.

```text
transmission cost collection
  -> regulatory review and settlement
  -> federal proceeding and refund-effective date
  -> expected ratepayer refund and forward cost control
  -> bill posting / receipt / adequacy / durable affordability [open]
```

## Why it matters for the larger atlas

This adds a consumer-power version of the infrastructure realization problem.
The system can correct a charge at the regulatory level before the lived result
is visible at the household level. It also shows why a refund amount is not a
recovery rate: the affected denominator, allocation, timing, and receipt still
matter.

## Evidence boundary

- The source is an official regulatory announcement, not a customer-level bill
  or payment file.
- The refund was expected in 2026 when announced; the announcement does not
  establish later posting, cash receipt, or unclaimed/returned status. The
  later FERC proceeding and refund-effective date advance the legal route but
  do not close those customer-level fields.
- The $13 million amount is not a per-household amount and cannot be treated as
  a ratepayer recovery percentage.
- The settlement addresses named transmission-cost practices; it does not show
  total household electricity affordability or reliability.

## Next test

Join the settlement to the FERC/utility implementation filing, transmission
charge line item, customer class, bill period, refund allocation, and later
payment or credit status. Then test whether the correction changes continued
use, trust, complaint, switching, or household financial room.
