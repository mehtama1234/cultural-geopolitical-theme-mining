# Finding 015: AI adoption is broad, but shallow and uneven within work

**Status:** provisional  
**Last checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Project:** AI, work, and control

## Finding

Generative AI is entering work quickly, but adoption is not a uniform
occupation-wide change. Workers doing similar tasks may use it at different
depths, and controlled studies show that access can change productivity or
working time without automatically changing the composition of work. The
important long-term question is therefore not only which jobs are exposed, but
which workers gain usable capability and who controls the resulting workflow.

## What the sources directly show

- In a staggered-access study of 5,179 customer-support agents, generative-AI
  access increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with a larger
  improvement for novice and lower-skilled workers and little effect for
  experienced and highly skilled workers. [NBER W31161](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)
- In a field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers, treated
  users spent less time on email and reduced work outside regular hours, while
  individual AI provision did not shift the quantity or composition of tasks.
  [NBER W33795](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795)
- A nationally representative worker survey finds adoption across many
  occupations and tasks but fewer than half of workers adopting within most
  occupations and tasks. [NBER W35677](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35677)
- In a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults, AI increased performance
  for all education groups but reduced the measured higher- versus lower-
  education performance gap by about three-quarters on the defined task; a
  follow-up without AI showed that a substantial gap re-emerged. [NBER W34851](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851)
- Repeated nationally representative US surveys report substantial overall
  adoption by late 2024, including work use in the prior week and daily use,
  while also showing that adoption intensity varies across workers.
  [NBER W32966](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32966)

## Evidence chain

```text
AI becomes available across occupations
  -> workers doing similar tasks adopt unevenly
  -> organizational support and task rules shape actual use
  -> time, productivity, skill, discretion, and evaluation may change
  -> pay, status, trust, bargaining, and household security may change
```

The sources directly support availability, adoption heterogeneity, and selected
productivity/time outcomes. The later worker-power and social-meaning arrows
remain open.

## What this does not establish

- Broad AI adoption has not been shown here to cause a general change in pay,
  employment, promotion, health, or job quality.
- Time saved by an individual worker is not necessarily time returned to the
  worker; an employer may reallocate it to additional tasks.
- Occupation-level exposure is not a substitute for worker-level access,
  training, monitoring, discretion, correction, or worker voice.
- The studies use different populations, interventions, outcomes, and time
  windows, so they should not be collapsed into one effect size.

## Cultural and societal meaning

The early social change may be a new difference between people doing similar
work: some receive a capability that helps them learn or finish tasks, while
others encounter the technology mainly as a monitoring, ranking, or allocation
system. Whether workers experience AI as assistance, pressure, opportunity, or
loss of control is a meaning question that requires direct worker evidence;
adoption alone cannot answer it.

## Counterevidence

The field experiment found time savings without a detectable change in task
quantity or composition after individual provision. That result limits any claim
that access automatically reorganizes work. The productivity experiment also
found strong heterogeneity by worker experience, limiting a simple “AI raises
productivity” interpretation. The education-gap experiment is a counterexample
to a universal widening story: AI narrowed the measured task-performance gap,
although it did not erase underlying differences or establish workplace gains.

## Next test

Follow workers within the same occupation or workplace over time, separating
assistance, recommendation, allocation, monitoring, and evaluation. Measure
training, task assignment, pace, autonomy, correction, promotion, pay, health,
worker voice, and whether saved time is retained by workers or absorbed into
new demands. Then connect the strongest differences to household security,
trust, status, and political or collective response.

**Evidence status:** observed and compared; broader causal and cultural claims
remain open.
