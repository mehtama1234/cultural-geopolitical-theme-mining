# Finding 089: Grid-security authority is expanding as electricity becomes strategic infrastructure

**Status:** provisional infrastructure/state-power policy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

Executive Order 14421, signed August 26, 2026 and published in the Federal
Register on August 31, declares a national emergency concerning the U.S.
bulk-power system. The order identifies foreign-supplied equipment, software,
firmware, remote access, sabotage, cyber exploitation, and supply disruption as
security risks. It also links the stakes to growing electricity dependence from
data centers, artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, and defense
production.

The order authorizes the Secretary of Energy, after specified findings and
interagency consultation, to restrict certain transactions and impose
conditions on existing equipment, including identification, monitoring,
isolation, replacement, or removal. It directs attention to reliability,
safety, secure replacement, and continuity of essential service.

The safe interpretation is:

> Electricity infrastructure is being governed simultaneously as a household
> service, an industrial bottleneck, and a national-security asset. The order
> expands federal control over supply-chain risk, but does not by itself show
> improved reliability, lower bills, local incidence, or public agreement.

## Event chain

```text
AI, data-center, manufacturing, and defense load growth
  -> greater dependence on reliable bulk-power equipment and software
  -> foreign-supply-chain and cyber-risk framing
  -> emergency authority over transactions and existing equipment
  -> possible procurement, replacement, and cost shifts
  -> [open] local rate, outage, reliability, household, worker, and political effects
```

## The first implementation and voice stage

On September 9, 2026, DOE issued a Request for Information seeking practical,
risk-informed, and administrable approaches to implementation. It invited
utilities, equipment and software suppliers, investors, insurers, labor
organizations, governments, consumer representatives, and other stakeholders
to identify current practices, alternatives, quantitative evidence, and
reliability, safety, cost, schedule, workforce, competition, and implementation
considerations.

This is meaningful institutional participation infrastructure, but it is not
yet a rule, transaction determination, procurement change, or evidence that
any participant influenced the final policy. The notice itself says responses
are voluntary and planning-only. The next missing stage is therefore a public
submission set or later rule that shows whose alternatives were incorporated,
what burdens were assigned, and what changed in equipment, procurement,
reliability, or cost.

## What the public record supplies

| Stage | Observed evidence | Still open |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic condition | The order identifies rising electricity dependence from data centers, AI, advanced manufacturing, and defense production | Actual load, affected facilities, and regional incidence under this order |
| Risk | Foreign equipment, software, firmware, remote access, sabotage, and supply disruption are named risks | Verified incident probability, vendor-specific exposure, and comparative risk |
| Authority | Energy may restrict qualifying transactions and set conditions on existing equipment after required determinations | Which transactions are restricted, enforcement, exemptions, and due process |
| Capability response | Identification, monitoring, securing, disconnecting, replacing, and pre-qualifying equipment are authorized or contemplated | Procurement completion, secure replacement availability, interoperability, and realized reliability |
| Distribution | The order requires consideration of reliability, safety, replacement availability, and essential-service continuity | Who pays, who waits, who benefits, and effects on rates, outages, jobs, and local investment |
| Participation and meaning/action | DOE solicits input from utilities, suppliers, labor, governments, consumer representatives, and other stakeholders | Public trust, state/federal conflict, local consent, political action, influence on final rules, and geopolitical response |

## Why this matters to the broad atlas

This case advances the infrastructure-to-state-power lane. A grid component is
not only a technical asset: its ownership, software update path, remote access,
replacement market, and procurement origin can alter who controls essential
service continuity. The policy also shows how new load from private technology
investment can turn a local capacity question into a federal security and
industrial-policy question.

The order should not be read as proof that foreign equipment caused an outage,
that domestic equipment is safer, or that emergency authority improves public
reliability. It is a policy and governance event. The missing empirical bridge
is implementation: named equipment or transactions, replacement cost and
timing, reliability outcomes, affected communities, and public response.
The RFI adds a visible voice/consultation stage, but not evidence of influence
or consensus.

## Coding consequence

```text
national-emergency declaration != observed grid failure
security authority           != realized reliability
domestic-preference signal   != domestic capability
replacement power            != household benefit
```

Code this as **reported federal security authority and supply-chain governance
under rising strategic electricity dependence; local incidence and realized
service outcomes open**.

## Next decisive test

The smallest useful follow-up is one implementation artifact: a covered
transaction, vendor/equipment determination, procurement rule, replacement
action, or reliability filing with date, geography, actor, cost, continuity
impact, and public or state response. That artifact can then be connected to
local load, rates, outages, jobs, and political contestation without importing
a broad household-to-geopolitical claim.
