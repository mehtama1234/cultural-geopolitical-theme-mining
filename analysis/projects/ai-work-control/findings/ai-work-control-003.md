# Finding 003: Control changes through meaning, not only measurement

**Status:** provisional  
**Last checked:** 2026-09-11  
**Project:** AI, work, and control

## Finding

The early evidence does not support a simple story in which more digital monitoring automatically raises performance. A randomized study suggests that surveillance by itself had no average performance effect, while unclear explanations for why monitoring was used reduced output. A separate field experiment found that AI access saved time without changing the amount or mix of recorded tasks.

The deeper issue is therefore not only how much work is measured. It is whether workers understand the system, can question it, and know who is responsible when it fails.

## What we directly know

- In an online labor-market experiment, surveillance had no significant average effect on performance. But when the reason for using or removing surveillance was not explained in relation to worker performance, output fell. [NBER Working Paper 33348](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33348)
- In a field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers, individual access to a generative-AI tool reduced email time and time spent working outside regular hours. The study did not detect changes in task quantity or composition during the experiment. [NBER Working Paper 33795](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795)
- HBS’s current adoption research story describes trust in the tool, trust in its builders, and clear responsibility when something goes wrong as conditions for adoption. [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/from-pg-to-microsoft-how-companies-speed-ai-adoption)

## Causal chain under test

```text
new AI or monitoring system -> workers ask what it is for and who controls it
-> explanation and recourse shape trust and effort
-> time, task choice, and review behavior change
-> performance and job quality change unevenly
```

The experiment supports the importance of explanation for one performance setting. The wider claims about trust, discretion, and job quality remain open.

## Why this matters

A firm can use the same tool in two different ways. It can remove wasted time and let workers keep judgment, or it can turn every action into a score without giving workers a way to challenge the score. The technical system may look similar while the social meaning is different.

This is the bridge to the project’s cultural and geopolitical work: control is experienced through daily rules, blame, status, and the ability to refuse or correct a system. It also raises a state-level question about who owns workplace data and which rules govern automated decisions.

## Four-map reading

| Map | First signal | Missing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Material | less time on email and possible output effects | pay, workload, margins, and long-run productivity |
| Social | explanation affects how workers respond to control | trust, stress, dignity, status, and worker voice |
| Institution | firms choose monitoring and AI-use rules | unions, regulators, courts, and grievance systems |
| Power | control depends on recourse and responsibility | data ownership, audit rights, exit, and bargaining |

## Strongest challenge

The surveillance experiment and the field experiment study different tools, populations, and outcomes. They do not prove that explanation always matters or that AI access is harmless. They show why the project must not collapse monitoring, assistance, and management into one variable.

## What would change our mind

- Evidence that surveillance has a large positive average effect even when its purpose is unclear.
- Evidence that AI time savings reliably change task mix, pay, or job quality across many firms.
- Evidence that workers with no explanation or recourse respond the same as workers with clear rules and review rights.
