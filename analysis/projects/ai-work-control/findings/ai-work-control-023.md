# Capacity is not leverage until an actor can refuse, switch, or impose a cost

**Status:** provisional geopolitical/state-capacity synthesis; observed external response remains open  
**Checked:** 2026-09-14  
**Project:** AI, work, and control

## The finding

The atlas now contains several kinds of state and infrastructure evidence:
SIPRI spending totals, defense awards and integration tests, a Polish
procurement agreement, public-cloud and data-center milestones, ownership
transitions, and domestic large-load governance. These records document
resources and commitments, but they do not yet document leverage.

The defensible finding is: **capacity, ownership, and commitment create the
conditions for leverage, but leverage exists only when an actor can use a
credible alternative, refusal, switch, regulation, retaliation, or continued
operation to change another actor's behavior.**

## Evidence architecture

| Stage | Current evidence | What it supports | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resource allocation | SIPRI's 2025 vintage estimates world expenditure at $2.887T, US expenditure at $954B, European expenditure at $864B, and NATO expenditure at $1.581T | Resource scale, changing growth, and alliance concentration are measurable | Readiness, cohesion, deterrence, or leverage; the US decline partly reflects reduced supplemental overseas assistance |
| Macro-fiscal transmission | IMF's April 2026 WEO estimates a typical defense-spending boom at +2.7 percentage points of GDP over about 2.5 years, with about 67% deficit-financed; the associated average deficit rises 2.6 points and public debt 7 points within three years, while wartime episodes show about a 14-point debt increase and falling social spending | Defense mobilization can create fiscal and social-allocation trade-offs before any external bargaining result is observed | A forecast for the US, a household burden estimate, a causal effect of defense spending, or proof that social spending will fall in a particular case |
| Contract/commitment | Poland announced an approximately $735M JASSM-ER agreement; the May 2024 page says 2026–2030, while a December 2024 MOD briefing states 2028–2030 in the JASSM discussion | A stated procurement response plus a schedule-vintage conflict | Delivered, accepted, or operational capability; the pages do not establish a contract amendment |
| Production and integration | JASSM/LRASM records document capacity investment and F-35B/F-35C flight-test milestones | Production-support and integration stages are real and time-ordered | Accepted inventory, fielding, combat performance, or changed adversary behavior |
| Supplier footprint | A reported General Dynamics-OTS JASSM/LRASM subaward maps to three active DLA CAGE facilities under one parent | Corporate/facility structure and possible geographic redundancy are visible | The subaward cannot be assigned to a plant; production, workforce, resilience, and replaceability remain open |
| Public infrastructure | Romania reports government-cloud infrastructure completion; Malaysia records financing, planned capacity, and ownership transition | Physical and financial capacity can expand across public/private systems | Portability, provider substitution, service outcomes, or public control |
| Domestic risk allocation | US large-load cases record tariff, reliability, disclosure, and cost-allocation responses | States are assigning infrastructure risk through policy | Actual household burden, firm exit, or political legitimacy |

The source-specific [state-leverage bridge](../domestic-capacity-dependence-state-leverage-cross-source-bridge-v1.md)
and the linked machine records preserve the distinct units. The comparisons
are deliberately not pooled.

## The mechanism

```text
resource, infrastructure, or supplier commitment
  -> realized capacity and control rights
  -> alternatives, inspection, portability, and ability to absorb disruption
  -> credible refusal, switch, regulation, retaliation, or continued service
  -> another actor changes price, terms, behavior, or policy
  -> bargaining power, alliance autonomy, or state legitimacy
```

The current atlas measures much of the first two stages and defines the fields
needed for the third. It does not yet observe the decisive fourth stage.

The IMF layer adds an important intermediate constraint to the interpretation.
Resources committed to defense can be large enough to alter deficits, debt, and
social-spending room before they generate a usable alternative or change an
external actor's terms. That does not make fiscal pressure a geopolitical
failure: defense can provide insurance, industrial capacity, deterrence, or
alliance assurance. It means that the program must follow both sides of the
capacity ledger—what capability is purchased and what domestic allocation or
financing path accompanies it.

## What each record contributes

SIPRI's 2025 vintage updates the scale and concentration of military resources,
including a 7.5% real US decline alongside higher European and Asian/Oceanian
spending. It is a context for state capacity, not a capability score. The
JASSM/LRASM records then
separate a DoD/firm commitment, production-support action, and flight-test
milestones. A planned delivery or successful test increases what may become
available; it does not show that a state can use or withhold the system in a
way that changes another actor's behavior.

The IMF WEO supplies the macro-fiscal middle that those procurement records do
not contain. Its defense-boom estimates imply that financing and social
allocation are part of the state-capacity question, but they are averages over
heterogeneous countries and episodes. The US WEO series is an aggregate
projection, not evidence that a particular US defense program caused debt,
inflation, or a household sacrifice. The correct use is to define the budget,
social-spending, import-content, and household-incidence fields that a country
or program ledger must collect.

The USAspending/DLA pass adds a supplier-structure caution. One reported
General Dynamics-OTS subaward appears alongside three active CAGE facilities
under a common parent in Arkansas, Vermont, and Maine. That footprint may
provide geographic redundancy, but the subaward row has no facility identifier,
so it cannot establish where the work occurred, how much capacity each site
holds, or whether another supplier could replace it.

The Poland record adds a foreign procurement response, but the agreement's
planned delivery window cannot be read as delivered capability. The official
Polish pages also preserve a schedule-vintage conflict: the May 2024 agreement
page states 2026–2030, while a December 2024 modernization briefing states
2028–2030 in its JASSM discussion. That may reflect different lots, planning
stages, or wording rather than a formal amendment. It is a reason to seek the
contract and acceptance trail, not evidence of delay or delivery. The Romania
and Malaysia cases show the same distinction in digital infrastructure: a
public-cloud project can expand government capacity, while a private campus
can expand regional capacity through external finance and ownership. Neither
case yet shows who can inspect, replace, pause, or renegotiate the underlying
system.

Domestic large-load governance supplies the distributional and political
bridge. A regulator may assign costs to a large load, require reliability
conditions, or disclose risk. That is an institutional response; it is not yet
evidence that households were protected, a firm changed plans, or residents
accepted the arrangement.

## Cultural and political meaning

“Strategic autonomy” is often narrated through assets, spending, factories, or
model ownership. The evidence suggests a more practical test: who can say no,
what happens if they do, and who bears the interruption while alternatives are
found? A physically domestic asset may still depend on foreign software,
finance, components, data, maintenance, or customers. An imported system may
still expand practical autonomy if it is portable, inspectable, and replaceable.

This turns state capacity into an everyday question. Households encounter it
through electricity bills, service reliability, public response, defense
spending, or local tax and employment promises. Those experiences may shape
legitimacy, but the current records do not infer public meaning from the asset
or expenditure alone.

## Counterinterpretations and limits

- High spending can buy insurance, modernization, or political signaling without
  increasing usable capacity.
- External procurement can diversify a supplier base rather than create
  dependence.
- Local ownership can be less capable or less replaceable than a well-governed
  external provider.
- A completed infrastructure project may improve service even if its operator
  is externally controlled.
- A regulatory tariff can prevent cross-subsidy or can simply reallocate costs;
  actual incidence must be measured.
- A test milestone can expose failure and delay rather than increase readiness.
- A defense boom can raise short-run activity or deterrence while also raising
  debt; the IMF average does not determine whether a specific country crowds
  out social spending.
- Higher public debt can reflect broader macro conditions, tax policy, or
  recession rather than the defense program under study.

## What would change the finding

It would weaken if the cases showed that capacity or ownership consistently
changed external terms without a credible alternative, or that resources alone
predicted observed concessions and behavior changes. It would strengthen if
states with similar assets differed according to supplier diversity,
portability, inspection, and absorption capacity, and if negotiation records
showed those conditions producing refusal, switching, concessions, or changed
external behavior.

## Next test

For one system, collect contracts, supplier concentration, data/control rights,
maintenance dependencies, termination costs, inventories, alternative suppliers,
outage records, regulatory decisions, and a dated negotiation or switching
event. Pair that event with domestic distribution: prices, jobs, service
continuity, household burden, local revenue, and public attribution. Use a
counterexample with similar scale but different replaceability. Do not label
the result leverage until another actor's terms or behavior actually changes.

**Evidence status:** capacity/commitment/ownership comparison; realized
leverage, public legitimacy, and external behavioral response remain open.
