# From formal worker voice to practical control

**Status:** thirteenth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** AI and algorithmic management, worker requests, formal labor administration, platform-work rights, remedy, and practical exit

## The question

When workers can request a change, file a charge, bargain, or receive a legal
right to explanation, does that change how work is actually organized?

The stages are:

```text
AI or algorithmic exposure
  -> worker notices a burden or loss of control
  -> request, consultation, complaint, bargaining, or legal right
  -> employer/agency/platform response
  -> change granted, denied, delayed, or partly implemented
  -> workload, schedule, pay, data, safety, or task-control result
  -> appeal, enforcement, retention, mobility, or exit
```

The current evidence shows activity at each early stage. It does not yet
support a general worker-power score or a same-worker causal path.

## What the evidence shows together

### 1. Voice is a conversion point, not an outcome

Workers are requesting schedule, equipment, task, telework, and training
changes. Workers and unions are filing charges and petitions. International
standards are creating rights to explanation and review. These are meaningful
signals of demand and institutional design.

They do not establish that the worker gained control. A request can be denied,
partly granted, delayed, or granted in a form that creates new workload or
stigma. A charge can settle without restoring the worker’s job, income, data,
or discretion.

### 2. Formal protection can outrun practical remedy

ILO Convention No. 193 is unusually explicit about algorithmic transparency,
human review, data access and correction, deactivation, and effective remedies.
Its own legal conditions make the limit clear: entry into force depends on
ratification, and domestic enforcement remains open.

The NLRB record shows a parallel institutional problem. A formal route can
process many charges and petitions while agency capacity, quorum, settlement,
and case timing shape what workers actually receive.

### 3. Control has several meanings

The Federal Reserve’s AI-use comparison suggests that workers with more control
over task completion report more AI use. That could mean autonomy enables useful
adoption. It could also mean higher-skill tasks are more AI-compatible, or that
employers permit AI where the work is already less constrained.

BLS’s pause and self-paced measures describe job structure, not lived power. A
self-paced job can have an impossible workload; a job with a pause option can
still penalize stopping. Control must therefore be measured as permission,
actual use, adequacy, consequence, and ability to refuse.

### 4. The missing worker-level join is decisive

The program still lacks a same-worker path joining AI exposure, request or
complaint, employer response, maintained change, workload/pay/health result,
and later retention or exit. Without that join, the safest claim is that worker
voice is a possible conversion mechanism whose success varies by institution,
job, bargaining position, and remedy access.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- AI use may be higher among workers with more control because of occupation,
  education, task fit, or employer policy—not because control causes adoption.
- A fully granted schedule change may still be inadequate for care, health, or
  transport needs.
- A partial change may be enough for one worker and harmful for another.
- A high settlement rate may represent efficient resolution, weak adjudication,
  or agreements that do not change the underlying workplace.
- Formal transparency can explain a decision without allowing a worker to
  contest or reverse it.
- Algorithmic management may reduce some arbitrary manager decisions while
  increasing pace, surveillance, or opacity elsewhere.
- A legal right may improve conditions for organized workers but remain
  unreachable for migrants, contractors, informal workers, or deactivated
  platform workers.

## Bounded social discussion

The available social discussion is limited but useful:

- [OECD algorithmic-management discussion](../../social-discussions/records/social-oecd-algorithmic-management-linkedin-001.json)
  frames algorithmic work allocation and monitoring as accountability,
  privacy, bias, and wellbeing questions. It is a secondary relay, not worker
  telemetry.
- [Worker-change discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0042-worker-changes-linkedin-001.json)
  surfaces the practical meaning of workplace changes and accommodation, but
  does not measure whether requests were granted or maintained.
- [Union discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0041-union-linkedin-001.json)
  helps identify collective voice and bargaining as possible control routes;
  public advocacy is not proof of a negotiated result.
- [Public-health worker discussion](../../social-discussions/records/atlas-batch-0049-public-health-worker-linkedin-001.json)
  links political hostility and institutional strain to retention risk. It
  adds worker meaning, not a representative retention estimate.

## Stronger next test

Build one workplace or platform event ledger with the following fields:

```text
worker/job/platform
  -> AI or algorithmic task, monitoring, schedule, pay, or deactivation exposure
  -> request, consultation, complaint, bargaining, or legal claim
  -> decision and explanation
  -> change granted, partial, denied, delayed, or reversed
  -> workload, schedule, pay, data, safety, or discretion result
  -> appeal, enforcement, retention, mobility, or exit
```

Record worker identity only under an approved privacy design. Preserve request
and grant denominators, response time, decision-maker, actual persistence, and
the worker’s own assessment of adequacy. A legal rule or agreement enters the
ledger as a design stage until implementation and receipt are observed.

## Current answer

**Answer state: partly answered.** Worker voice is a plausible conversion point
between AI exposure and control. The evidence shows requests, formal charges,
collective cases, and international rights, but not a general result for
worker discretion, pay, safety, or exit. The next decisive evidence is a
same-workplace or same-platform sequence from exposure through maintained
change and later worker outcome.
