# Domestic capacity, dependence, and state leverage cross-source bridge v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-14
**Scope:** US domestic affordability, firm/sector capacity, infrastructure, and external dependence  
**Status:** cross-level geopolitical synthesis; state leverage and household-to-state links remain open

## The broad question

How do domestic conditions become geopolitical constraints or bargaining power?
The program needs to connect what people and firms experience at home with the
sector, infrastructure, ownership, trade, finance, and alliance structures
that determine a state's room to act.

```text
external shock, trade rule, finance, technology, or supply constraint
  -> domestic price, access, jobs, capacity, or adjustment burden
  -> consumer, worker, firm, and public-system adaptation
  -> productive capacity, ownership, supplier choice, and resilience change
  -> state bargaining room, alliance dependence, policy choice, or leverage
```

The connection is not a direct household-to-state causal claim. It is a set of
linked units: consumer, worker, firm, sector, infrastructure, country, and
state decision.

## What the combined program supports

### 1. Domestic affordability is part of strategic capacity

Price pass-through and household adaptation show how external or policy shocks
can become substitution, delayed purchases, borrowing, lost savings, or extra
work. Those are domestic adjustment signals. They should not automatically be
called national weakness: the same adjustment may coexist with substitution,
new suppliers, investment, or improved capacity.

### 2. Sector dynamism is not the same as strategic resilience

Applications, openings, exits, jobs, and employer stocks reveal whether a
sector is renewing or contracting. They do not by themselves reveal whether
the sector produces critical inputs, has domestic ownership, can scale during
a shock, or depends on a foreign supplier. Strategic interpretation requires
sector-specific input, inventory, ownership, and substitution records.

### 3. Ownership and replaceability determine leverage

Infrastructure or productive capacity can be physically located in a country
while control, finance, software, customers, or critical inputs remain outside
it. The relevant state variables are provider diversity, data and contract
control, local technical skills, alternative suppliers, inspection rights,
termination cost, and the ability to continue service during a dispute.

The new realization records make this distinction operational across two
systems. In AI infrastructure, Romania's public-cloud program and Malaysia's
private hyperscale campus both have dated construction or migration milestones,
but their public-service outcomes, local incidence, and portability remain
different open questions. In defense procurement, the JASSM/LRASM record now
separates a large obligation, subaward structure, corporate/facility ownership
context, production or integration milestones, and Poland's planned delivery
window. None of those stages alone proves delivered capability or bargaining
leverage.

The Poland reciprocal-production record adds a distinct implementation stage:
official 2026 accounts describe potential buyer/seller access in a US-linked
defense relationship, planned regional production with the US, Estonia, and
Latvia, assembly followed by production at PGZ/WZL-2, and technology/know-how
transfer language. The later BBN checkpoint and Ministry of State Assets
production-plan account make implementation governance and planned scale more
visible, but they do not establish a commissioned line, completed quantity,
delivery, measured local-content share, or practical replaceability. The
September 2026 Poland-US FMF agreement adds a broader financing signal—reported
$4 billion and acceleration of existing contracts—but does not identify a
Barracuda allocation and therefore cannot be treated as production or delivery
evidence. The
World Bank AI capability frame supplies a parallel vocabulary—adopt, adapt,
advance—in which access and local adaptation can increase capability while
concentration and missing complements preserve dependence. These are related
mechanisms, not evidence that defense production or AI infrastructure produced
the same kind of state power.

### 4. Distribution affects legitimacy and policy room

The domestic burden of dependence may land on consumers through prices, workers
through job or schedule changes, firms through financing/input costs, places
through energy or environmental burdens, or public systems through procurement
and renewal. Unequal incidence can affect trust and political demand, but that
meaning must be measured rather than inferred from trade exposure alone.

### 5. State leverage is relational

A country has more room to act when it can substitute suppliers, finance,
technology, energy, labor, or markets; inspect and regulate key systems; and
absorb adjustment without unacceptable domestic loss. A large asset or trade
volume is not automatically leverage. Leverage appears when another actor
changes behavior because exit, refusal, retaliation, or continued operation is
credible.

SIPRI spending totals provide the resource-allocation context, while the
procurement and infrastructure cases provide narrower realization paths. The
combined evidence therefore supports a staged state-capacity proposition:
resources and commitments can precede realized capability, and realized
capability can still coexist with external ownership or limited
replaceability. An external response, refusal, switch, or negotiated
concession is still required before calling the result leverage.

## Concrete comparison design

Choose one critical system—cloud/AI, energy, a medical input, food, transport,
or a financial payment rail—and compare two sectors, firms, or countries with
different supplier concentration or domestic control. Record:

1. external exposure and the exact input or service at risk;
2. domestic firms, ownership, capacity, inventories, skills, and alternatives;
3. price, quality, availability, wages, work, and household adjustment;
4. who bears the cost across consumers, workers, firms, places, and the state;
5. contracts, data/control rights, inspection, portability, and termination;
6. public investment, regulation, subsidy, or emergency response;
7. actual switching, substitution, resilience, or failure during a shock;
8. attribution, trust, public demand, diplomatic action, retaliation, or
   alliance response.

Use a counterexample with similar external exposure but greater supplier
diversity, local ownership, public control, or a successful switch. Keep a
domestic price result, a firm-capacity result, and a geopolitical result as
separate claims unless a shared unit and time order support the connection.

## What this changes in the broad program

The geopolitical theme is not an abstract layer added after social research.
It asks how everyday affordability, work, firm survival, local services, and
infrastructure ownership accumulate into national capability or dependence.
But the direction cannot be assumed. A state may accept external ownership to
gain capacity, use that capacity to bargain, or discover that it cannot inspect
or replace the system. The end-to-end test is therefore: who absorbs the
adjustment, who controls the alternatives, and does any external actor actually
change behavior as a result?
