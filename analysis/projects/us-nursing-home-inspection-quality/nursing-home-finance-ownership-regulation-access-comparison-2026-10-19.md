# Nursing-home finance, ownership, regulation, and resident outcomes

**Checked:** 2026-10-19  
**Status:** bounded cross-study care-governance map; estimates are not pooled

The nursing-home corpus now exposes a chain in which financing and ownership
change firm behavior, regulation changes measured compliance and access, and
placement changes resident outcomes. The studies use different settings,
units, and estimands.

| Stage | Evidence | Observed signal | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment design | Medicaid financing / Indiana administrative evidence | A 12% increase in Medicaid dementia volume concentrated in the lowest-quality facilities and was followed by lower one-year survival in the studied setting | Indiana and federal-audit evidence; worker conditions, resident voice, and universal effects are not measured |
| Ownership and finance | Detailed related-party accounts | 68% of profits estimated as tunneled to related real-estate or management entities; implied typical IRR rises from 4.83% to 13.11% after adjustment | Accounting classification is not proof of illegality, care harm, or staffing response |
| Merger response | Facility-level acquisition evidence, 1999–2019 | Health deficiency citations fell 8% and persisted up to five years, while direct-care hours fell and resident-health outcomes did not meaningfully improve | Citations, labor input, and health are distinct measures; fewer citations are not automatically better care |
| Inspection and measurement | US inspection and admission evidence | Facility responses are strong during inspections and mostly fall after completion; higher ratings still predict relative mortality | Ratings are not absolute quality, and the abstract does not identify the causal care input |
| Regulation and access | California 100-bed staffing threshold | Facilities bunch and manipulate size to avoid staffing requirements; access is limited especially for disadvantaged patients | California working-paper evidence; clinical outcomes, prices, and persistence remain open |
| Resident placement | 2.6 million stays across 7,200 nursing homes | Assignment to a roommate with dementia raises 90-day mortality 2.1 percentage points for patients without dementia; effects reverse for some dementia patients | Mortality is not privacy, preference, quality of life, or worker experience; the 0.8-point assignment-rule reduction is modeled, not implemented |

## Bounded synthesis

The combined evidence says that “quality” is not one observable. Payment can
change admissions; ownership can change where surplus appears; mergers can
lower citations while reducing direct-care hours; inspections can produce
temporary compliance; staffing thresholds can change facility size and access;
and roommate assignment can alter mortality through peer effects. None of
these stages alone identifies the full resident experience.

The most important conversion boundary is between institutional metrics and
lived care. A lower citation count, a higher rating, a subsidy, or a modeled
assignment rule is not equivalent to safe care, adequate staffing, resident
choice, privacy, or durable wellbeing. Conversely, a financing or ownership
signal should not be treated as proof of harm without the corresponding care
and outcome measure.

## Next bounded test

The next useful comparison is one facility-level episode joining ownership or
payment change to staffing composition, inspection timing, resident choice,
care experience, and a later outcome. Until that exists, retain the stages as
connected mechanisms rather than a single causal chain.
