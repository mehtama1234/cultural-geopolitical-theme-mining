# Project: nursing-home inspection timing and care quality

## Question

When inspectors usually arrive on a predictable schedule, do nursing homes
raise care effort only near the inspection—and what does that mean for
residents?

## Decision rule

Keep inspection timing, staffing effort, measured care inputs, patient survival,
regulator information, resident wellbeing, and facility economics separate.
More inspections or more randomness are not automatically better on every
dimension.

## Current bounded finding

The [inspection-timing finding](findings/us-nber-nursing-home-inspection-predictability-care-quality-2026-001.md)
reports that care effort and patient survival follow the inspection cycle, while
modeling suggests less predictable timing could improve survival with a modest
information tradeoff.

The [threshold-regulation record](../../records/us-nber-nursing-home-threshold-regulation-staffing-access-2026.json)
adds the firm-response side: California nursing homes bunch around the 100-bed
threshold, and higher staffing above it is nearly entirely selection rather than
a causal staffing increase. Low-staffing facilities manipulate size to avoid the
rule, limiting access especially for disadvantaged patients. The next test is to
join threshold response to staffing composition, resident outcomes, worker
conditions, payer mix, and local capacity.

The [inspection-response record](../../records/us-nber-nursing-home-inspection-strategic-response-mortality-2025.json)
adds the persistence test: facilities respond strongly across several core
dimensions during inspections, but nearly all responses drop immediately after
the inspection. Ratings may not measure absolute quality cleanly, yet higher
ratings still predict lower patient mortality. The next test is to separate
temporary compliance, persistent facility quality, patient sorting, and resident
experience.

The [merger-quality record](../../records/us-nber-nursing-home-mergers-compliance-direct-care-2025.json)
adds the ownership test: acquired facilities show an 8% reduction in health
deficiency citations over three years, persisting at least five years, but direct
care hours decline and resident health does not meaningfully improve. The citation
change is concentrated in low-severity categories, so the next test is whether
ownership changes alter clinical outcomes, staffing quality, resident voice, and
access rather than only compliance measures.

The [related-party finance record](../../records/us-nber-nursing-home-related-party-tunneling-2024.json)
adds the financial-governance layer: the paper estimates that 68% of nursing-home
profits are tunneled to related real-estate and management companies, raising the
implied typical investment return from 4.83% to 13.11% after adjustment. This is
not by itself proof of illegality or care harm; the next test is whether financial
extraction changes staffing, reimbursements, liability, resident outcomes, or
worker conditions.

The [Medicaid financing record](../../records/us-nber-medicaid-nursing-home-volume-quality-moral-hazard-2025.json)
adds the public-payment layer: federal matching grants can reward verifiable
volume rather than quality, and Indiana evidence reports a 12% rise in Medicaid
dementia volume concentrated in the lowest-quality facilities, with lower
one-year survival. The next test is whether payment redesign improves provider
selection, staffing, resident outcomes, and state accountability beyond the
studied setting.
