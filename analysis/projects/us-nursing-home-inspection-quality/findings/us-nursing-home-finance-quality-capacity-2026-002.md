# Finding 002: Care capacity can be weakened by the way ownership, payment, and oversight measure quality

**Status:** provisional U.S. care-finance and public-capacity synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-10-19

## The bounded finding

The current nursing-home evidence describes a chain in which financial
structure, public payment, ownership change, and oversight can move different
care inputs in different directions. Related-party payments account for an
estimated 68% of nursing-home profits in one detailed financial study, raising
the implied typical investment return from 4.83% to 13.11% after adjustment.
Separately, Medicaid financing evidence finds a 12% increase in dementia
volume concentrated in lower-quality facilities in Indiana, with lower
one-year survival. Merger evidence reports 8% fewer deficiency citations for
at least three years, but fewer direct-care hours and no meaningful resident-
health improvement. Inspection evidence finds that facilities respond strongly
around inspections and that nearly all responses fall immediately afterward,
while higher ratings still predict relative mortality.

These findings are not one pooled estimate. Together they support a
measurement-and-capacity gap:

```text
ownership and financing structure
  -> provider incentives and resource allocation
  -> staffing, admissions, inspection response, and recorded deficiencies
  -> resident exposure and survival
  -> [open] sustained quality, experience, voice, remedy, and exit
```

## Evidence comparison without pooling

| Evidence surface | Result | Unit and boundary | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Related-party finance | 68% of profits estimated to be routed to commonly owned real-estate or management entities; adjusted investment return is higher than reported return | U.S. nursing-home providers and related accounts; working-paper accounting analysis | Legality, staffing, resident harm, reimbursement changes, prices, and causal quality effects |
| Medicaid payment design | Federal matching incentives can reward verifiable volume over measured quality; Indiana dementia volume rises 12% and shifts toward lower-quality facilities with lower one-year survival | 24 federal audits plus Indiana administrative and structural evidence | Generalization across states, worker conditions, resident voice, patient selection, and full fiscal mechanism |
| Chain acquisition | Deficiency citations fall 8% and persist at least three years, while direct-care hours fall and resident health does not meaningfully improve | Facility-level staggered difference-in-differences, 1999–2019 | Whether citations reflect clinical quality, which workers lose hours, resident experience, access, and ownership economics |
| Inspection response | Facility responses are strong during inspections and nearly all fall immediately afterward; higher ratings still predict relative mortality | U.S. nursing homes and patient admissions; exact effect sizes not disclosed in the abstract | Absolute quality, lasting resident wellbeing, complaints, staffing composition, and whether redesign improves outcomes |

The measures must remain separate. Profit routing is a financial structure;
volume is a payment-response measure; citations are an oversight output; direct-
care hours are an input; and mortality is a resident outcome. A favorable move
in one does not certify the others.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

The care system can appear productive or compliant while shifting risk into
less visible places. Ownership can improve a recorded compliance measure while
reducing direct-care time. Payment can expand access to beds while reallocating
patients toward lower-quality providers. Inspection can reveal relative quality
and induce real effort while also rewarding preparation around a predictable
event. Financial extraction may coexist with legitimate related-party services,
so the evidence does not justify calling every payment misconduct.

The central distinction is:

```text
profit transparency       != resident protection
more admissions            != better access
fewer citations            != better health
more direct-care minutes   != complete quality
higher rating              != absolute quality
formal oversight           != durable remedy
```

This is a public-capacity and household-room issue as well as a firm-power
issue. Residents and families often cannot observe ownership accounting,
facility selection incentives, inspection timing, or the difference between
temporary compliance and durable care. Their practical alternatives, switching
costs, and ability to contest a placement remain central missing links.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Merger-related citation reductions may reflect genuine compliance gains even
  when direct-care hours fall; the evidence does not identify one universal
  direction of quality.
- Related-party payments may represent legitimate services, financing, tax, or
  risk allocation; adjusted returns depend on classification and valuation.
- Indiana Medicaid results should not be generalized to every state or payment
  design without a comparable test.
- Higher ratings predict relative mortality, but ratings may still reflect
  patient sorting, persistent facility differences, or unmeasured inputs.
- None of these sources follows one resident or family from placement through
  payment, care experience, complaint, remedy, transfer, and later wellbeing.

## Next decisive test

Build a facility–resident episode ledger that joins, where lawful and possible:

```text
facility ownership and payment exposure
  -> staffing and direct-care allocation
  -> inspection timing, citations, complaints, and admissions
  -> resident acuity, experience, transfer, survival, and discharge
  -> family choice, appeal, remedy, switching, and later security
```

The design must include facilities with improved citations but no health gain,
facilities with high ratings and poor observed experience, residents who can
switch, and residents who remain because alternatives are unavailable. Do not
turn the finance, compliance, staffing, or mortality measures into a single
care-quality index before the units and clocks are aligned.
