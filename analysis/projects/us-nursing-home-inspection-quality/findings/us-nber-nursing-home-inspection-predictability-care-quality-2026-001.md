# Finding 001: Predictable inspections can create predictable care effort

**Status:** bounded U.S. nursing-home oversight and care-quality finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study uses U.S. nursing-home inspection records from 2006–2019 and
patient outcomes from 2013–2019. Inspections are officially unannounced, but
they often follow a predictable cycle: 74% occurred 40–60 weeks after the prior
inspection, with an average gap of about 53 weeks.

Facility care effort was highest around inspection and declined afterward before
rising again as the next inspection approached. The effort measure combines
payroll staffing variables and care inputs such as antipsychotic use and
physical restraint.

Four-week patient survival followed a similar pattern. The study reports that a
one-standard-deviation increase in effort was associated with a 0.2 percentage-
point, or 5.4%, lower patient mortality rate.

The route is:

```text
inspection timing and predictability
  -> facility staffing and care effort
  -> patient survival and regulator information
  -> [open] sustained care quality, resident wellbeing, and facility behavior
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds an oversight version of a recurring pattern:

> A formal check can improve real performance while its predictable timing lets
> organizations concentrate effort around the check.

The model estimates that the current inspection system saves 886 lives per year
compared with no inspections. Making inspections less predictable is estimated
to save about 92 additional lives per year, roughly the gain from increasing
inspection frequency by 10%. But less predictability also reduces some of the
regulator's immediately available information.

## Limits and next test

- The effort-mortality relationship and lives-saved figures combine empirical
  estimates with a model; they are not all directly observed outcomes.
- The composite effort index does not capture every aspect of resident care or
  quality of life.
- Facility ownership, staffing, resident acuity, selection, complaints, and
  local conditions may change the pattern.
- The study does not show the effect of an actually implemented new inspection
  regime.

The next decisive test is a protected facility-resident ledger joining inspection
timing, staffing, deficiencies, care inputs, complaints, resident acuity,
survival, discharge, quality of life, and later facility response. Until then,
the safe claim is that inspection design changes provider effort and patient-risk
patterns, but the full care and wellbeing effect remains open.
