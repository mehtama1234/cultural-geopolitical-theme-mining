# Finding 001: Cybersecurity rules can consume capacity through overlap

**Status:** bounded firm/public-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

GAO's March 2026 report summarizes a September 2025 panel with seven industry
representatives from different critical-infrastructure sectors. Participants
said that operators can face multiple cybersecurity frameworks with overlapping
or slightly different controls, definitions, and reporting requirements.

The reported burden is operational rather than merely legal. Participants
described duplicative work, conflicting guidance, confusion over similar
requirements, and incident-reporting rules that differ in detail, deadlines,
and thresholds. Short reporting windows can make it technically difficult to
collect and submit the required information while responding to an incident.

GAO also reports partial progress: of 12 earlier recommendations to improve
federal coordination, agencies had implemented 11 and partially addressed one.
Participants identified possible next tools including standardized terminology,
shared effectiveness metrics, deconflicted rules, confidential information
sharing, a federal working group, and streamlined incident reporting.

The atlas-relevant insight is:

> Governance can protect critical infrastructure while also consuming the
> organizational capacity needed to operate and secure it. The distributional
> question is who has enough legal, technical, and staffing capacity to absorb
> overlapping rules—and whose time is diverted from prevention, repair, or
> service continuity.

The route is:

```text
private critical infrastructure
  -> multiple regulatory frameworks
  -> duplicated or conflicting controls and reporting
  -> compliance and incident-response capacity
  -> security practice and public-service continuity [not measured here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a firm-facing governance mechanism distinct from the water-sector
cyber record. The water record follows technical exposure and uneven system
capacity; this one follows how the regulatory environment itself can allocate
time, reporting power, and implementation burden across firms and agencies.

The next decisive artifact is a named sector or operator followed through
requirements, staff hours, reporting route, security investment, incident or
near miss, agency feedback, and service or consumer outcome.

## Limits and counterevidence

- Seven panel participants are not a national prevalence sample.
- Reported overlap does not prove that rules reduced security or caused an
  incident.
- The record does not quantify compliance cost, staff time, reporting quality,
  breach reduction, consumer loss, or service continuity.
- Sector-specific rules may reflect genuinely different risks; harmonization can
  reduce duplication without weakening safeguards.
- The 11-of-12 recommendation status describes federal coordination, not private
  operator compliance or realized cybersecurity.
