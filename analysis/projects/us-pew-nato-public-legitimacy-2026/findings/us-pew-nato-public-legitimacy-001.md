# Finding 001: NATO can remain popular while permission for U.S. leadership is divided

**Status:** bounded geopolitical legitimacy and political-meaning finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

Pew Research Center surveyed 45,658 adults in 37 countries from February 8 to
May 13, 2026. Across the 13 NATO member states included in the comparison, a
median of 65% viewed NATO favorably and 30% unfavorably. Majorities in the
United States, the Netherlands, and Italy were favorable, while 59% in both
Greece and Turkey viewed NATO unfavorably.

The U.S. institutional average concealed a large partisan split: 75% of
Democrats and Democratic leaners viewed NATO favorably, compared with 42% of
Republicans and Republican leaners. The same survey reported 69% of Democrats
and 34% of Republicans expressing confidence in Ukrainian President Volodymyr
Zelenskyy, while confidence in Russian President Vladimir Putin was low in both
groups.

The route is:

```text
formal alliance membership
  -> institutional favorability
  -> partisan and leader-specific meaning
  -> willingness to fund, host, risk, or sustain commitments [open]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This gives the geopolitical legitimacy theme a current public-opinion layer:

> Support for an alliance is not the same thing as trust in a particular
> leader, agreement with a particular policy, or willingness to bear a future
> cost.

That distinction matters for U.S. power. NATO can retain broad institutional
support while the political permission for U.S. leadership is uneven across
parties and countries. Formal alignment and relational trust may therefore move
on different clocks.

## Limits and next test

- The survey measures attitudes, not troop deployments, host-country access,
  defense spending, burden sharing, operational cooperation, or treaty
  durability.
- Favorable NATO opinion does not establish support for a specific war,
  administration, leader, budget, base, or risk.
- The 13-country median is not a U.S. estimate, and the country samples cannot
  be treated as one shared population.
- The U.S. partisan comparison is descriptive. It does not identify whether
  party identity, leader evaluation, ideology, war preferences, or material
  interests produced the gap.
- The source does not follow respondents from an attitude to a later vote,
  protest, military decision, tax preference, or alliance action.

The next decisive artifact is a repeated public-opinion or event-linked record
that connects alliance attitudes to a specific commitment, burden-sharing
decision, host-country permission, or later political action. Until then, the
safe claim is that institutional alliance favorability and political permission
for U.S. leadership are related but separate resources.
