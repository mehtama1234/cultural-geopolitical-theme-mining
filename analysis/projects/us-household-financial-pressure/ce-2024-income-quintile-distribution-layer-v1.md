# Consumer spending by income quintile v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Question:** Did the 2024 consumer spending adjustment look the same across income groups?

## Result in plain words

No. BLS reports that average income before taxes increased 6.8% for the lowest income quintile in 2024, while average expenditures increased 3.8%. For the highest income quintile, income was essentially unchanged and expenditures increased 0.4%. The lowest-quintile expenditure increase was concentrated in housing, with food, apparel and services, education, and miscellaneous spending also contributing.

This is not a claim that the lowest-income households became secure or that the highest-income households became insecure. It is a population-average distributional result: the same national price environment was absorbed by income groups with different spending and income changes.

## Source and method

- [BLS Consumer Expenditures in 2024](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/consumer-expenditures/2024/home.htm), including Table 5 and the income-quintile discussion.
- [BLS Consumer Expenditure tables](https://www.bls.gov/cex/tables.htm), which provide means, shares, and standard errors by income quintile and other demographic groups.
- Income quintiles rank consumer units by before-tax income; they are not individual people, and they do not measure wealth or liquid cash.

The values below are published BLS percentage changes. The direct 2024 XLSX table endpoint was not used for additional calculations in this pass; no unreported spreadsheet values are inferred.

## 2024 distributional comparison

| Income group | Income before taxes, change from 2023 | Average expenditures, change from 2023 | Reported spending interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Lowest quintile | +6.8% | +3.8% | Increase concentrated in housing, with food and other categories contributing |
| Second quintile | +5.3% | +2.3% | Spending rose less than income |
| Third quintile | +4.8% | +2.3% | Spending rose less than income |
| Fourth quintile | +4.1% | +2.4% | Housing was the largest dollar contributor to the increase |
| Highest quintile | 0.0% | +0.4% | Spending rose slightly despite essentially flat reported income |

## What this contributes to the broad societal program

1. **A national average can hide different room to adapt.** Income and spending moved differently by quintile, so one aggregate spending change cannot represent consumer experience.
2. **Housing remains a budget pressure across the distribution.** The lowest-quintile increase was especially concentrated in housing, connecting this layer to rent, insurance, energy, transport, and mobility rather than treating “consumption” as discretionary shopping only.
3. **Spending change is not welfare change.** More spending can mean more quantity, higher prices, unavoidable bills, or a changed mix of goods. Less spending can mean relief, delay, substitution, or unmet need.
4. **The political meaning remains a separate arrow.** Income-group differences may shape judgments about fairness, government, or firms, but BLS expenditure tables do not measure attribution, trust, identity, or political action.

## Boundaries and next test

These are averages across consumer units. They do not identify the same household's bill, price, debt, quality tradeoff, benefit receipt, or recovery. The next test is to combine quintile spending shares and standard errors with SHED/SIPP material-condition measures and ANES/Pew interpretation and action measures, keeping the units separate until a valid panel or matched design is available.
