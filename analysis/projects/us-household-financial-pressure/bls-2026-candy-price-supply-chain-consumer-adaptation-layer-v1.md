# BLS 2026 candy prices, supply chains, and consumer adaptation layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Monthly Labor Review
**Publication:** September 8, 2026
**Status:** product-specific consumer-price and supply-chain layer; not a general household-welfare estimate

## Why this update matters

The consumer lane needs examples where a global or upstream shock reaches an
ordinary purchase and changes not only the price but also package size,
holiday practice, substitution, and perceived value. Candy is a useful bounded
case because it is both an everyday luxury and a culturally concentrated
holiday purchase: Halloween, Christmas, Valentine’s Day, and Easter account for
about two-thirds of annual confectionery sales.

## The consumer adaptation route

The article describes multiple adaptation channels rather than one response:

1. **Price pass-through:** input and import costs move toward end-product and
   consumer prices.
2. **Quantity change:** smaller packages can preserve a visible price point
   while reducing product quantity.
3. **Occasion management:** holiday-heavy demand makes the change salient at
   culturally meaningful times.
4. **Channel change:** pandemic-era consumers shifted some purchasing toward
   online sales, while total domestic candy dollar sales fell only 0.2% from
   2019 to 2020.
5. **Supply-chain exposure:** cocoa and sugar shortages, weather, logistics,
   regulation, and traceability requirements shape the available product and
   price.

This is a compact example of why nominal spending cannot be read as unchanged
consumption or welfare. A consumer can spend more, receive less quantity,
switch products, delay a holiday purchase, or preserve a ritual by accepting a
higher price. The article does not identify which response dominates for which
household.

## What this adds to the broad goal

The layer connects a global input and climate/logistics route to a specific US
consumer category while keeping the stages distinct:

**supply shock → input price → producer price → package quantity/retail price
→ holiday or everyday purchase → substitution/ritual adaptation → household
meaning and later trust.**

The first four stages are described in the BLS article. Household budgets,
income groups, retailer margins, actual substitution, nutrition, and cultural
meaning remain open.

## Open arrows and next test

The next stronger design would match product-level price and package-size data
to household income, food insecurity, retailer/channel, holiday timing, and
observed purchase or non-purchase. It should distinguish shrinkflation from
price inflation, preserve quantity and quality, and compare necessities with
discretionary treats. Do not turn one product category into a general food or
consumer-confidence result.
