# Material pressure, attribution, and political action bridge

Status: forty-fourth active depth artifact; checked 2026-10-18.

## Scope

This bridge links medical affordability, public-benefit access, guaranteed
income, financial worry, trust, attribution, voting, protest, civic contact,
and institutional response. It asks whether material experience becomes public
action, and what intervening route makes that conversion possible.

## What we found

Material conditions can become political, but there is no automatic pressure →
action path. Oregon Medicaid coverage increased turnout in one presidential
election, while a three-year guaranteed-income experiment found no detectable
broad change in participation, political preferences, trust, or polarization.
SNAP office closures reduced nearby participation, showing that administrative
access can suppress use before a person ever reaches political action. Medical
expense crises were associated with lower turnout but, in exploratory CCES
screens, more official contact and protest. Financial worry also tracked lower
trust and different reported vote choice in a 2024 panel. These are distinct
routes, populations, estimands, and limits—not one pooled causal finding.

## Evidence ledger

| Evidence | Signal | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Oregon Medicaid lottery | Coverage increased one presidential-election turnout estimate by 2.5 percentage points; no later-election increase through November 2010 | Local low-income previously uninsured Oregon adults; mechanism not observed |
| NBER guaranteed-income experiment | $1,000/month treatment versus $50 control showed no detectable broad effect on participation, preferences, trust, or polarization | 3,000-person, three-year experiment; bounded context and precision |
| NBER SNAP office closures | Nearby participation fell 7–9% over two years after closures; openings were suggestive in the other direction | Tract-level administrative participation; no individual access attempt, food security, attribution, or political response |
| CCES medical-affordability modules | Crisis was associated with lower validated turnout and, in exploratory screens, more official contact/protest; pooled contact OR 1.831 | Weighted exploratory cross-sections; hardship timing and causal direction are unresolved |
| 2024 ANES panel | Extreme financial worry co-occurred with lower federal trust and different reported vote choice | Panel selection, prior identity, beliefs, and retrospective judgment remain confounders |
| NBER trust, crisis, and turnover | Lower generalized trust was associated with greater political turnover after economic crises across country, subnational, and individual panels | Long-run observational construction; no complete individual crisis→trust→vote→turnover chain |

## What this connects

- Material condition: affordability, income, health cost, or benefit access.
- Route access: office closure, information, travel, administrative burden, and time.
- Interpretation: who or what people blame, and whether action seems effective.
- Action: voting, official contact, protest, organizing, non-use, or exit.
- Response: whether institutions acknowledge, remedy, or deepen the problem.
- Feedback: later trust, security, and willingness to act again.

## Deeper question

When does material pressure become effective political action rather than
private adaptation, distrust, or non-use?

This is a high-priority missing link because the atlas has strong evidence on
material hardship, administrative access, trust, and participation separately,
but rarely observes the full conversion chain in one person or place. The
bounded next test is one dated episode with exposure, access, attribution,
efficacy, action, response, and later trust or repeated action.

## What could change the answer

- A benefit can raise turnout without producing broad trust or participation
  changes.
- Low participation can reflect route closure, time cost, distrust, or private
  coping rather than political satisfaction.
- Contact or protest can occur without institutional resolution.
- Attribution, organization, and perceived efficacy may matter more than
  hardship alone.
- Lower protest is not automatically lower legitimacy; it may reflect remedy,
  exclusion, exhaustion, or lack of access.

## Challenge checks

Do not infer hardship=action or non-turnout=trust. Keep causal estimates apart
from exploratory cross-tabs, preserve each study's population and period, test
counterexamples, identify affected groups, and treat public discussion as a
bounded lead rather than representative evidence.

## Current answer

Partly answered. Material pressure changes the opportunity and motivation for
action, but conversion depends on access, attribution, efficacy, organization,
and institutional response. The full chain remains open.

## Stopping rule

Do not call hardship mobilizing, demobilizing, trust-building, or politically
effective from turnout, protest, cross-tabs, participation, or funding alone.
Stop the next pass only after one source-confirmed same-person/place episode is
fully linked—or the source record explicitly shows which link cannot be
observed.
