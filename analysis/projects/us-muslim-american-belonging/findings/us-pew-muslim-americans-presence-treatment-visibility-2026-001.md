# Finding 001: Institutional presence can grow while legitimacy and safety remain unsettled

**Status:** bounded cultural-identity and political-visibility finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in plain language

Pew's 2026 review shows several things changing at once for Muslim Americans. The estimated Muslim population grew from about 2.4 million in 2007 to 5.5 million in 2026; the number of U.S. mosques rose from roughly 1,200 in 2000 to nearly 2,800 in 2020; and four Muslim members of Congress were reported in 2026 compared with none in 2001.

But greater presence did not automatically produce broad legitimacy or equal treatment. In 2026, 42% of U.S. adults said Muslim Americans had a negative impact on the country and 43% said Muslims were less patriotic than other Americans. Among Muslim adults, 59% said there was a lot of discrimination against Muslims and 59% said media portrayals were unfair. Pew also reports that the share saying Islam is more likely than other religions to encourage violence rose from 25% in 2002 to 51% in 2026, with a much wider partisan gap in 2026.

These are different measures. Population, mosques, officeholding, public judgment, personal experience, media treatment, and reported hate-crime incidents should not be collapsed into one “integration” number.

```text
population and institutions grow
  -> public visibility increases
  -> representation may increase
  -> public judgment and experienced treatment can still remain hostile
  -> [open] safety, service access, civic power, policy response, and belonging
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This gives the atlas a clear cultural-political pattern:

> Visibility and capacity can grow faster than legitimacy, safety, or equal treatment.

That pattern connects to the broader findings on religious communities, immigration, public trust, media legitimacy, and political representation. A community can build institutions and gain officeholders while still facing suspicion or unfair treatment. Conversely, negative public judgments do not tell us whether the community lacks internal capacity or whether every member experiences hostility.

The partisan widening in views of Islam also shows that the same population can become a site of national political meaning. The source does not establish whether parties, media, security events, migration, or other causes produced the shift.

## What the source does not show

- Population estimates, mosque counts, officeholding, survey attitudes, reported experiences, and FBI incidents use different units and dates.
- Reported hate crimes are not total discrimination or victimization prevalence.
- Public suspicion does not prove actual conduct, and representation does not prove equal influence or policy success.
- The long-run comparison crosses survey modes and wording changes.
- The source does not follow people or places from discrimination through avoidance, organizing, migration, service use, voting, or policy change.

## Next decisive test

Join local institutional presence or representation to a dated place- or person-level outcome: perceived safety, public-system use, civic participation, organizing, or policy response. Keep public judgment, experienced treatment, and actual institutional power separate.
