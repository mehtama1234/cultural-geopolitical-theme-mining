# Finding 001: K–12 AI adoption is not the same as equal AI capacity

**Status:** bounded education-institution and inequality finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35347 uses a national survey of K–12 school principals to
measure AI integration through policies, teacher training, student-use guidance,
leadership engagement, and access to AI-enabled tools. AI use has spread across
schools: students mainly use it for homework help and writing, while educators
primarily use it for lesson planning and administrative work.

Institutional support has lagged adoption. The paper reports that training,
guidance, and school policies have not kept pace with use. It also finds
diffusion gaps: a one-standard-deviation increase in student disadvantage is
associated with a 0.07–0.11 standard-deviation lower AI-integration index, and
private and charter schools score 0.23–0.44 standard deviations lower than
traditional public schools. District size explains roughly one-third of the
disadvantage gap between public schools.

The atlas-relevant insight is:

> Technology diffusion can widen institutional differences even when the tool
> itself is widely available. The scarce resource may be the capacity to set
> rules, train people, guide use, and turn adoption into safe and meaningful
> practice.

The route is:

```text
AI availability and early use
  -> school policy, training, guidance, and leadership capacity
  -> uneven institutional integration
  -> student/teacher use and educational consequence [not observed here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a K–12 institutional-capacity and unequal-exposure layer distinct from
firm AI adoption, coding activity, and forecasts of future labor effects. It
shows why adoption counts alone cannot answer whether technology changes real
options for students or educators.

The next decisive test is a school or district panel joining AI integration to
training, policy, student and teacher use, accessibility, discipline or privacy
incidents, learning, workload, and later educational or labor outcomes.

## Limits and counterevidence

- The public abstract does not disclose the full sample count, response rate, or
  survey instrument details.
- Associations do not establish that disadvantage causes lower integration or
  that integration improves outcomes.
- Lower integration may reflect deliberate caution or appropriate limits rather
  than deprivation.
- The integration index does not measure learning, safety, privacy, teacher
  workload, student wellbeing, or household advantage.
- More AI use is not automatically better; governance quality may matter more
  than adoption volume.
