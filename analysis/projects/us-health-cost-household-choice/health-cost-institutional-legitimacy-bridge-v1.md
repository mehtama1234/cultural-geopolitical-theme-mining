# Health cost, institutional response, and legitimacy bridge v1

## Purpose and boundary

This bridge asks how the health-cost chain could reach institutional legitimacy:
care foregoing and household sacrifice, credit-system exposure and remedy, then
confidence or political judgment. The evidence is intentionally layered. The
SHED, CFPB, and Census units are not pooled, and none of the links below proves
that one person’s medical bill caused a later trust judgment or political act.

## Four observed surfaces

| Chain stage | Direct evidence | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Household choice and residual burden | SHED 2024→2025 panel | Persistent care foregoing coincides with 41.78% medical debt, 65.33% reduced savings, 36.39% increased borrowing, 76.44% delayed purchases, and 30.62% fair/poor health in 2025; exit still carries residual strain |
| Institutional translation and response | CFPB Consumer Credit Panel and Regulation V record | Medical collections become credit-visible; reporting changes remove or delay some records; the 2025 federal medical-debt rule was later vacated |
| Material difficulty and confidence | Census HTOPS July 2026 cross-tab | Respondents reporting expense difficulty had 15.12 percentage points lower high confidence in federal statistical agencies and 6.94 points lower high confidence in Congress than respondents reporting no difficulty; same-round descriptive contrast |
| Medical hardship and targeted action | CES 2018/2020 personal-crisis module extracts | Same-respondent medical-expense hardship is observed alongside turnout and six political acts; responsibility attribution is available, and the exploratory adjusted screen shows higher official-contact odds in both years |

## Interpretation status

The defensible inference is a conversion hypothesis:

```text
health-cost pressure
  -> care sacrifice and household adaptation
  -> debt becomes visible to credit institutions
  -> remedy changes visibility or timing but may leave the underlying burden
  -> attribution can route hardship into targeted political action or judgment
  -> repeated exposure can become a judgment about institutional fairness
```

The first two stages are directly reported in their respective source systems.
The final conversion into legitimacy is inferred across sources and remains
open. Confidence differences can reflect prior identity, party, information,
general financial stress, or institutional experience unrelated to health care.
Credit-report removal can improve a financial opportunity without restoring
care, savings, health, or dignity. A person can therefore experience material
repair, administrative repair, or neither—and those are distinct legitimacy
routes.

CES adds a direct action benchmark but not a completed legitimacy result. The
medical-expense item is measured in the same respondent as contact and protest,
and responsibility attribution distinguishes government-targeted pressure from
bad-luck interpretation. The module extracts do not show whether the action
concerned health care, received a response, changed trust, or restored material
room. ANES supplies trust and policy judgment but does not supply the dated
medical episode. The two surveys are therefore complementary endpoint
benchmarks, not a joined causal chain.

## Smallest decisive next test

The strongest next design would link, for the same person or household:

1. a dated health need, bill, denial, or collection entry;
2. coverage, amount owed, payment timing, and available alternatives;
3. received, delayed, substituted, or forgone care;
4. debt, credit, housing, work, health, or family-security consequence;
5. dispute, appeal, charity, insurer, provider, regulator, or court response;
6. later trust, switching, complaint, voting, organizing, or exit measure.

Until that design exists, the atlas should describe legitimacy as a plausible
but unproven downstream consequence, not as an effect of medical cost.
