# Health costs travel through care choice, household adaptation, institutional response, and contested legitimacy

**Status:** provisional layered end-to-end finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-15

## The finding

The current evidence supports a connected mechanism, but not one completed
same-person causal chain. Cost-related care foregoing is associated with debt,
reduced savings, borrowing, delayed purchases, outside help, and a different
health surface. Coverage access can expand care and reduce financial exposure;
medical-debt relief can improve credit access without repairing average health
or financial wellness. Medical debt can also become an institutional credit
signal, while expense difficulty and confidence in public institutions move in
opposite directions across separate population surveys. The final conversion
from burden to trust, political action, switching, or exit therefore remains a
bounded inference and an open acquisition target.

## Evidence chain

| Link in the chain | What the evidence shows | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Need/cost → care choice | In SHED 2025, 26% reported skipping needed care because of cost; the share was higher at lower incomes and among uninsured respondents. | Reported / compared |
| Care choice → adaptation | Respondents reporting any care skipped had higher same-respondent medical debt, unexpected medical expense, borrowing, reduced savings, delayed purchases, work adaptation, and outside-household help. | Compared association |
| Care choice → persistence | In the 2024–2025 SHED recontact panel, persistent foregoing had 41.783% medical debt, 65.334% reduced savings, 36.389% increased borrowing, and 76.442% delayed major purchases; entry and exit retained distinct strain surfaces. | Longitudinal descriptive |
| Payment → practical room and care choice | In MEPS 2024 first-event records, ER zero-payment respondents were less confident paying an unexpected expense than the $2,000+ band, while medical debt and cost-related care-delay shares were higher in the high-payment band. | Compared; selection-sensitive |
| Practical room/debt → care delay | In same-respondent MEPS 2024 fields, cost-related medical-care delay was 14.15% among respondents not confident paying an unexpected expense versus 5.36% among confident respondents; it was 16.98% with medical debt versus 4.57% without, with a non-monotonic amount-band pattern. | Compared association |
| Coverage → care-delay distribution | In MEPS 2024, cost-related medical-care delay was 17.63% among uninsured respondents versus 5.87% with any private coverage and 5.39% with public-only coverage. | Compared association; selection-sensitive |
| Coverage → care and financial exposure | The Oregon Medicaid lottery increased coverage and care use while reducing out-of-pocket exposure and unpaid bills sent to collections. | Randomized estimate |
| Institutional visibility → credit constraint | CFPB analysis found medical collections concentrated in lower-income communities; small-balance tradelines were common, and a collection was associated with a credit-score decline. | Official reported / compared |
| Remedy → downstream outcome | Randomized medical-debt relief produced modest credit-access improvements where counterfactual reporting was active, but no detected average health, utilization, or financial-wellness repair. | Randomized estimate |
| Burden → legitimacy | A Census expense-difficulty/confidence contrast is directionally consistent with a legitimacy concern, but it does not follow the same person, bill, remedy, or political act. | Inferred across sources / open |

## Arrow-by-arrow boundary

The first three arrows are now supported by population and panel evidence, but
the panel does not identify a dated bill or the alternative that was rejected.
The Oregon result identifies a coverage opportunity effect, not the response to
one person's episode. The CFPB and debt-relief studies establish an institutional
route and a remedy response, but neither observes the complete care-choice and
trust pathway. The evidence should therefore be read as a layered mechanism
map, not as a single effect estimate.

```text
cost / coverage
  -> care delayed, changed, or refused                 reported / compared
  -> debt, savings, borrowing, purchase, work tradeoff  compared / longitudinal
  -> health and recovery                                longitudinal descriptive
  -> credit visibility or coverage remedy               official / randomized
  -> trust, action, switching, or exit                  inferred / open
```

## What is established

- Care foregoing is an observable household choice, distinct from recorded
  service use and from total health expenditure.
- The choice is accompanied by multiple adaptations rather than one universal
  “financial burden” response; money, time, work, and health outcomes must stay
  separate.
- Coverage and debt relief are not interchangeable interventions. Coverage
  changes access and financial exposure; debt relief changes credit visibility
  and access in narrower conditions.
- Institutional response can redistribute burden without proving that the
  original care need was resolved.

## What is not established

This evidence does not show that a particular bill caused a particular care
decision, debt balance, health change, job outcome, distrust judgment, vote,
provider switch, or political movement. It also cannot identify whether a
reported exit from care foregoing means recovery, changed need, improved access,
recall error, or another unmeasured route.

## Decisive next acquisition

The next source or matched design should follow the same person or household
from a dated need or bill through plan/network status, amount owed, due date,
available alternatives, care continuation or foregoing, money/time/work/family
substitution, collections or correction, remedy effort, and a later health,
security, trust, action, switching, or exit measure. It must preserve weights,
missingness, uncertainty, counterexamples, and a clear follow-up window.
