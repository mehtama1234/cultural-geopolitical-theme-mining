# Finding 015: Medicaid coverage can increase voting briefly without creating durable political participation

**Status:** bounded randomized coverage-to-turnout finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The finding

The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment linked random Medicaid-lottery selection,
Medicaid coverage, and official statewide voter records. Medicaid coverage
increased turnout in the November 2008 presidential election by about 2.5
percentage points, roughly 7% relative to the control-group mean. The effect
was larger among men (about 5.4 points, or 18%) and residents of Democratic
counties (about 3.6 points, or 10%). The evidence suggests some of the increase
may have come through new registration rather than only higher turnout among
already-registered voters.

The effect did not persist: the study found no evidence of increased turnout in
later elections through the November 2010 midterm. This is a causal public-
benefit-to-political-participation result, but it is not a general law that
coverage creates durable civic engagement.

## The chain

```text
randomized Medicaid access
  -> coverage and lower medical/financial exposure
  -> short-run voter registration/turnout response
  -> no detected persistence through 2010
  -> attribution, policy demand, trust, remedy, or recovery [open]
```

## Why it matters to the broad atlas

This closes a political-action stage that the other health-cost layers leave
open. It also adds a temporal boundary: a public benefit can change political
participation at one election without producing durable participation. The
turnout result does not identify whether the mechanism was financial relief,
health, administrative contact, registration, or another channel.

## Do not overread

- The experiment is a historical Oregon lottery among low-income uninsured
  adults, not a current national Medicaid estimate.
- Turnout is observed in official voter records; party attribution, issue
  choice, campaign contact, policy demand, trust, and later household recovery
  are not observed in the result.
- The subgroup differences are bounded estimates, not proof that county party
  context or gender caused the response.
- No claim is made that the observed short-run turnout change persists after
  the experiment's later elections.
