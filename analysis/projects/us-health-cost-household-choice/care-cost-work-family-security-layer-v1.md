# Care cost, work, family time, and security layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Status:** cross-source social-reproduction synthesis; not a same-family causal estimate

## The broad question

When health or care is needed, who absorbs the cost? The answer may be a
provider bill, an insurer, an employer, a caregiver, a child, a missed shift,
a delayed treatment, a credit record, or another household need.

```text
care need or health event
  -> price, coverage, travel, wait, paperwork, or approval
  -> treatment, delay, substitution, paid help, or unpaid family labor
  -> work hours, income, food, housing, transport, time, and debt change
  -> health, job mobility, family roles, dignity, and future security change
  -> employers, insurers, providers, public programs, and families respond
```

This is a societal pattern about social reproduction—the work required to keep
people healthy, fed, housed, and able to participate—not a one-household story.

## Source layers

| Source layer | Unit | What it contributes | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal Reserve household survey | US adult and household report | Cost-related care skipping and its strong income gradient | Does not identify the exact bill, illness, coverage rule, or later outcome |
| Hospital-admission research | Person/event linked to earnings and employment | A serious health event can affect both health and work/financial outcomes | Effects vary by condition, insurance, job, age, and family support |
| Medicaid expansion research | State/policy exposure and financial outcomes | Coverage can reduce medical collections and protect parts of household finances | Financial protection is not equivalent to timely, complete, or good care |

| USDA food-security layer | Household and household-with-children | Food is a downstream security outcome; families may shield children while adults absorb hardship | Annual status does not identify the exact care event or price path |

## Provisional societal patterns

### 1. Care is paid in several currencies

Money is only one cost. A household can pay through time, travel, missed work,
job lock, reduced food or housing room, borrowing, family labor, or delayed
care. A policy that lowers a bill may leave the unpaid-care or scheduling cost
untouched.

### 2. Financial protection and health protection can diverge

Coverage and debt relief may improve collections, credit, or payment capacity
without proving that treatment was timely or health recovered. Conversely, a
household may receive care while losing wages, savings, or job flexibility. The
outcome must be named before claiming that a reform “worked.”

### 3. Families redistribute harm internally

Food-security evidence shows that adults and children can experience different
levels of hardship. Caregiving evidence shows that another family member may
provide the missing hours. Aggregate household stability can therefore hide an
unequal distribution of lost sleep, work, food quality, autonomy, or health.

### 4. Institutions shape labor freedom through care dependence

Employer-linked coverage can protect a family and constrain a worker’s ability
to leave. Rigid schedules can make care duties look like low commitment when
the actual issue is an incompatible work rule. The relevant measure is not only
whether employment continued, but whether the worker had a real alternative.

## Keep the outcomes separate

| Outcome | Do not substitute it for |
|---|---|
| Medical bill | Payment trouble, credit record, or delayed care |
| Coverage | Timely access, treatment quality, or health recovery |
| Debt relief | Returned work time, restored health, or family security |
| Employment retained | Good job, free choice, or absence of job lock |
| Household food security | Equal food access for every family member |
| Paid care | Total care burden, including unpaid hours |
| Care delay | Choice, preference, or non-need |
| Household stability | Individual dignity, health, time, or exit options |

## Evidence status by arrow

| Arrow | Status | Safe current conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Care need → price/coverage/access burden | Reported/compared | Cost, coverage, travel, waiting, and paperwork can constrain care. |
| Burden → care choice | Reported | A substantial minority report skipping care because of cost, with higher rates at lower incomes. |
| Health event → work/financial outcome | Compared in event studies | Hospitalization can affect earnings and employment, with heterogeneous effects. |
| Coverage/debt relief → financial record | Compared/causal in selected studies | Some interventions protect collections or credit-related outcomes. |
| Care burden → family time/work/food | Inferred from separate layers | The mechanism is plausible but needs same-event longitudinal measurement. |
| Care and institutional dependence → trust/political meaning | Open | Dignity, blame, trust, and action must be measured directly rather than inferred from hardship. |

## Next bounded empirical comparison

Use one dated health or caregiving event as the unit and follow the same person
or family for twelve months. Record:

1. need, diagnosis, care role, and event date;
2. quoted and final price, coverage, deductible, approval, travel, waiting, and
   paperwork;
3. care received, delayed, changed, or refused;
4. paid and unpaid care hours, including who in the family supplied them;
5. work hours, leave, schedule control, job change, benefits, and lost pay;
6. food, housing, transport, saving, borrowing, collections, and debt changes;
7. health, work, family-time, dignity, trust, and exit outcomes at one, six,
   and twelve months.

Compare similar events across portable/public versus employer-linked coverage,
high versus low job flexibility, and strong versus weak family support. Include
a counterexample where a similar event did not delay care or cause work loss.

## What would change the interpretation

The working picture would weaken if care costs rarely changed care, work, debt,
food, or family time after illness severity and resources were accounted for;
if coverage portability did not affect job choice where it should bind; if debt
relief reliably restored health and work without remaining loss; or if family
care burdens were evenly distributed and did not vary with schedule control or
institutional design.

## Relationship to the broad program

This layer connects household room, time, work control, care, inequality,
housing and food security, consumer/insurance power, public systems, trust, and
political meaning. Its central societal question is: when care is necessary,
which institution or family member absorbs the cost, and what future choice is
lost as a result?
