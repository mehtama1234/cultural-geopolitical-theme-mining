# Finding 001: Cultural distance is widening in some U.S. dimensions, not all

**Status:** bounded U.S. cultural-distance finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

Bertrand and Kamenica measure how accurately a person's group can be inferred
from media consumption, consumer behavior, time use, social attitudes, or a
newborn's name. They compare groups defined by income, education, gender, race,
and political ideology.

The study finds that differences in social attitudes by political ideology—and
somewhat by income—increased over the last four decades. White and non-White
groups became more different in consumer behavior while converging somewhat on
attitudes. Gender differences in time use narrowed through 1995 and then
stabilized. Many other group-by-dimension combinations remained broadly stable.

In simple terms:

```text
group differences
  -> show up differently in media, shopping, time, and attitudes
  -> some dimensions widen
  -> others converge or stay stable
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This prevents “America is culturally splitting apart” from becoming one blanket
claim. Cultural distance has multiple surfaces. Political-attitude distance
can rise while racial attitude distance narrows, or consumer behavior diverges
without proving hostility or social isolation.

## Limits and next test

- The study combines different datasets, periods, and group definitions.
- Being able to infer a group is not the same as conflict, exclusion, or loss
  of trust.
- The results do not identify the causes of widening or narrowing distance.
- Cultural distance does not establish political action or policy change.

The next test would compare the same people or places over time on cultural
signals, contact, trust, belonging, political judgment, and collective action.
