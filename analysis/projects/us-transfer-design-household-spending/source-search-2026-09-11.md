# Source search: US public aid and the shape of a dollar

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

When two families receive aid of the same value, does the form and timing of the aid change what happens next?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NBER-W35698 | [NBER: Is a Dollar a Dollar? How Transfer Design Shapes Household Spending](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35698) | A new study compares one-time food vouchers, monthly food benefits, one-time cash payments and monthly cash payments for low-income families with children. It finds larger short-run food-store spending responses for in-kind and recurring aid than for cash and one-time aid: 0.38 and 0.18 for food benefits versus 0.20 and 0.06 for cash. | Working paper using high-frequency household scanner data | The study examines pandemic-era programs and short-run food-store spending. A larger spending response is not by itself proof of better food security, higher well-being or lasting financial improvement. |
| NBER-W32784 | [NBER: The Impact of Unconditional Cash Transfers on Consumption and Household Balance Sheets](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32784) | A US randomized study of monthly unconditional cash transfers finds higher spending, including on housing, food and cars, with improved self-reported financial health but no measured effect on several debt and hardship outcomes. | Randomized study | The transfer design, sample and outcomes differ from the new study; it is a comparison, not a direct test of the same program. |

## First pattern to test

```text
public need
  -> aid amount, form and timing
  -> what the household can buy now
  -> food, housing, debt or care choice
  -> short-run relief or faster spending
  -> trust in the program and demand for the next policy
```

The NBER evidence supports the first spending difference. It does not show that food vouchers are always better than cash, or that a short-run purchase response becomes lasting security.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Cash gives families room to address needs a program designer cannot see. Food-labeled aid may protect food spending but restrict a family facing rent, medicine or transport costs. Recurring aid may help planning, while a one-time payment may fit a sudden repair or debt.

## Main gaps

- current US results outside pandemic-era programs;
- spending on food versus skipped bills, care, debt and savings;
- effects by income, family size, race, place and access to stores;
- administrative cost and take-up under each design;
- whether households value choice more than labeling;
- whether aid form changes political trust or support for a program.

## Decision rule

Pair the NBER spending result with food-security, debt, health and program-access measures. Do not treat what a family buys in the next few weeks as the whole effect of public help.
