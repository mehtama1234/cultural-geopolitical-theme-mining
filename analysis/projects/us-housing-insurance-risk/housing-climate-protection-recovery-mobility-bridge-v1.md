# Housing climate risk, insurance protection, recovery, and mobility bridge

**Status:** thirty-sixth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** housing affordability, insurance, hazard exposure, claims, repair, care disruption, relocation, recovery, and place trust

## Question

When climate or disaster risk reaches a household, does insurance and public
response preserve the ability to remain and recover—or convert the home into an
unaffordable, underinsured, or forced-mobility exposure?

```text
hazard / income / place
  -> premium, nonrenewal, rent or mortgage pressure
  -> coverage, backstop, aid, or claim
  -> payment, repair, care, work, and safe-return capacity
  -> stay, move, displacement, or selective reoccupation
  -> trust, neighborhood change, and durable recovery
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage or link | Comparable observation | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing tenure and payment room are unequal | The Federal Reserve reports homeownership at 63% of U.S. adults overall, 33% below $50,000 family income, and 86% at $100,000+; median 2025 mortgage payment was $1,600 and median rent $1,200. | Household economic well-being survey, cross-sectional national estimates. | Tenure and median payment are not affordability, wealth, quality, or long-run mobility outcomes. |
| Rent pressure can become forced mobility | In 2025, 23% of renters were behind on rent at some point in the prior year, including 33% below $25,000 income and 5% at $100,000+; 2% moved because of eviction or its threat. | Federal Reserve survey with income-conditioned renter estimates. | Self-reported arrears are not administrative eviction records or a causal income effect. |
| Insurance can turn ownership into exposure | Six percent of homeowners lacked insurance; the share was 12% in low/moderate-income neighborhoods. Forty-three percent of uninsured homeowners cited inability to afford insurance, and 20% of insured homeowners wanted more coverage but could not afford it. | Federal Reserve household survey. | Does not measure claims, property damage, insurer causation, or whether underinsurance caused a move. |
| Climate-risk premiums and nonrenewal are geographically uneven | Treasury/FIO and FEMA-linked ZIP analysis found mean premiums of $2,321 in the highest climate-risk quintile, 82% above the lowest-risk quintile, and approximate nonrenewal differences of 80%. | Matched ZIP and policy context, 2018–22; unweighted/descriptive comparisons. | ZIP-level exposure is not a household panel; risk definitions, construction, reinsurance, and state rules remain separate. |

| A last-resort market can expand while protection remains incomplete | California had 8.30 million private homeowner policies excluding FAIR Plan policies and 668,609 FAIR Plan homeowner/commercial policies in December 2025; 4,794 FAIR Plan claims from the Palisades and Eaton fires had produced $914 million paid against an estimated $4 billion loss by February 2025. | Official market and claims snapshots. | Aggregate payments do not show timing, adequacy, repair, safe return, or household-level recovery. |

| Relocation can redistribute welfare and place | An NBER HOPE VI analysis reports large displacement losses from demolition/redevelopment, with vouchers and counseling only partly offsetting them while neighborhood prices and gains shifted toward higher-income homeowners. | Named urban redevelopment episodes with modeled welfare and policy-offset analysis. | Does not establish universal redevelopment effects, individual stability, or complete wellbeing. |

## Cross-source finding

The central pattern is a **protection-to-mobility conversion gap**. A home can
remain physically present while insurance, rent, care, repair, and financing
conditions make it harder to remain, borrow, rebuild, or move safely. Public
backstops and regulator action can reduce losses without restoring the prior
place, household income, or social network.

The chain needs six separate gates:

1. **Exposure:** hazard, property, tenure, income, care, and place conditions.
2. **Protection:** affordable insurance, mortgage/rent room, mitigation, and
   feasible alternatives.
3. **Event:** damage, nonrenewal, school closure, claim, eviction threat, or
   other dated shock.
4. **Response:** notice, aid, claim handling, complaint, payment, repair, and
   human support.
5. **Mobility:** stay, return, safe move, displacement, reoccupation, or exit.
6. **Durability:** income, care, work, neighborhood composition, trust, and
   future insurability.

The strongest counter-signal is that hazard exposure does not produce one
uniform migration response: block-level and parcel-level movement differ, and
some households can remain protected while low-income uninsured households bear
the sharpest risk. A regulator can also produce monetary recovery without
proving that a household repaired or safely returned.

## Bounded public discussion

An NBER relay links wildfire smoke and school disruption to mothers' employment;
a comment suggests temporary staffing as a possible buffer but does not test it.
A wildfire research relay frames damage as a multi-year income and community-
composition shock with selective reoccupation. A public housing post describes
relocation costs as insurance, transport, utilities, neighborhood fit, and
adaptation—not rent alone.

These discussions identify care, reoccupation, and transition-capacity
mechanisms. They are research relays or individual accounts, not representative
mobility or recovery evidence.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- High-risk households may remain because of attachment, wealth, jobs, family,
  or lack of alternatives; staying is not proof of safety or preference.
- Nonrenewal can reflect insurer pricing, regulation, reinsurance, construction,
  or underwriting changes rather than realized property risk alone.
- A FAIR Plan claim payment can enable successful repair, even when aggregate
  data cannot prove the share or speed of safe return.
- Floodplain migration may reflect amenity, housing supply, or property type,
  not hazard denial or irrational choice.
- School disruption can reduce maternal employment through care loss, but labor
  demand, remote work, health, and household substitution may moderate it.
- Redevelopment can improve some residents' housing or safety while producing
  displacement losses for others; average neighborhood improvement is not equal
  resident welfare.

## Stronger next test

Build a property-household-year episode ledger that records:

1. hazard exposure, tenure, income, care, work, property, mortgage/rent, and
   insurance terms;
2. nonrenewal, premium, disaster, school or service closure, claim, notice, and
   aid timing;
3. complaint, payment, coverage, repair, contractor, mitigation, and verified
   safe-return status;
4. stay, move, temporary displacement, reoccupation, neighborhood composition,
   work, care, and school outcomes; and
5. later premium, renewal, borrowing, trust, civic response, and exit.

Compare a household that repairs and remains, one that moves with support, and
one that remains underinsured or cannot return. Preserve a matched low-risk
place and a case where public correction improves protection without relocation.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The corpus shows how hazard, insurance, rent, care, work,
claims, public correction, and relocation can interact, but it does not yet
follow the same household from exposure through verified payment, repair, safe
return or move, and durable place security.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call insurance protective, disaster aid restorative, or relocation
successful from policy count, aggregate payments, claims, or reoccupation alone.
Require a dated property-household episode with coverage, payment, repair,
mobility, later security, and a counterexample.
