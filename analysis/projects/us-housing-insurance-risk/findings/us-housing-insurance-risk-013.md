# Finding 013: Affordable housing on paper can be unavailable or physically inadequate

**Status:** bounded housing-supply and usability finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in simple words

HUD's 2025 report separates three questions: is a rental affordable, is it
available to the household that needs it, and is it physically adequate?

For every 100 extremely low-income renter households in 2023, the report found
about 66 affordable units. But only about 38 were affordable and available, and
only about 34 were affordable, available, and physically adequate.

The route is:

```text
very low household income
  -> nominally affordable units
  -> vacancy and higher-income occupancy constraints
  -> adequate, available unit
  -> [open] search, landlord acceptance, lease-up, safety, stay, or move
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This clarifies why a housing shortage is not just a price problem. A unit can
fall under an affordability threshold and still be occupied, unavailable in
the needed place, or physically inadequate.

It also explains why a voucher or other housing benefit may still require a
search through the private market. The benefit can be real while the usable
unit remains scarce.

## What it does not show

- These are national and regional stock ratios, not household outcomes.
- They do not show search time, applications, landlord acceptance, or lease-up.
- They do not measure accessibility, transportation, safety, repair, or later
  housing stability for a specific household.
- A low ratio does not mean every household fails, and a higher ratio does not
  guarantee a successful move.

## Next decisive test

Join a place or household record to the stock measure: search contacts,
screening, landlord response, lease-up, moving cost, inspection, repair,
stay/move, and later stability. Keep assistance issuance separate from usable
housing.
