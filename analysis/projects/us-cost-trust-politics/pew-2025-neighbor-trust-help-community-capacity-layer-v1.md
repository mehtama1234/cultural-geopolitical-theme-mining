# Pew 2025 neighbor trust, mutual aid, and community capacity layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** Pew Research Center, *How connected do Americans feel to their neighbors?*
**Survey field dates:** March 10–16, 2025; 9,482 U.S. adults in the American Trends Panel
**Status:** community-trust and informal-capacity layer; not a measure of actual assistance delivered, generalized social cohesion, or causal neighborhood effects

## Why this update matters

The atlas has formal institutions, public programs, religious affiliation, and
political participation. This layer adds the neighborhood as an operating
social institution: who is known, trusted, similar, expected to help, and
available in an emergency. It is especially useful for the broad goal because
it distinguishes generalized trust from local trust and stated willingness from
reciprocal capacity.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Careful interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Know all or most neighbors | 26%; 62% know some; 12% know none | Local familiarity is limited and uneven |
| Trust all or most neighbors | 44%; 46% trust some; 9% trust none | Neighborhood trust exceeds generalized trust |
| Trust all/most neighbors in earlier comparison | 52% in 2015, versus 44% in 2025 | Cross-sectional trend comparison, not individual decline |
| Know all/most neighbors in earlier comparison | 31% in 2018, versus 26% in 2025 | Comparable question timing should be checked carefully |
| Neighbors same race/ethnicity | 50% say all or most are the same | Perceived similarity, not objective segregation |
| Neighbors same political views | 23% say all or most are the same | Political homogeneity is less common than racial similarity |
| Comfortable asking neighbor to hold emergency keys | 59% | A practical trust threshold, not an observed emergency plan |
| Would bring mail or water plants for neighbor | 76% extremely/very likely | Stated willingness to help |
| Believe neighbor would do the same | 52% extremely/very likely | Reciprocity expectation is substantially lower |
| Would conserve water/electricity if public official asked in emergency | 72% | Local compliance willingness under a public request |
| Believe neighbors would do so | 42% | Collective-capacity expectation is lower than personal willingness |
| Would bring meal to sick neighbor/friend | 67% | Informal care willingness |
| Believe neighbor/friend would bring meal | 42% | Perceived available support is lower than intended giving |
| Would loan money to friend in emergency | 50% | Financial mutual aid willingness |
| Believe friend would loan money | 45% | Smaller giver/receiver expectation gap for money |

Pew finds that older, White, more affluent, more educated, and rural or
suburban adults are generally more likely to know and trust neighbors and to
report helping behavior. People who attend in-person religious services at
least monthly are also more likely to know and trust neighbors. Pew notes that
party differences in neighbor trust are related to race, education, and other
factors, so the raw partisan contrast should not be read as a party mechanism.

## What this adds to the broad goal

1. **Local trust is not generalized trust.** Forty-four percent trust most or
   all neighbors, compared with 34% saying most people can be trusted. A person
   can be locally embedded while remaining skeptical of society in general.
2. **Willingness and available support diverge.** People are more likely to say
   they would help than that neighbors would help them. This creates a
   practical-capacity question: social resilience may depend on reciprocity,
   not goodwill alone.
3. **Informal infrastructure can substitute for formal capacity—but is not
   free.** Keys, meals, errands, money, and emergency conservation can protect
   households or public systems, yet they transfer time, risk, and obligation
   to friends and neighbors. The survey does not measure the cost or adequacy
   of that substitution.
4. **Similarity and trust are related but not identical.** Half report racial
   similarity among neighbors, only 23% political similarity, and perceived
   similarity correlates with trust and mutual-help expectations. This does not
   establish that homogeneity causes trust or that diversity prevents help.
5. **Place and resources shape community capacity.** Rural/suburban residence,
   income, education, age, and religion condition reported connection. These
   are possible structural pathways, not proof that one neighborhood form or
   demographic group causes stronger reciprocity.

## End-to-end position

The source supports this bounded chain:

```text
place and social proximity
  -> familiarity and local trust
  -> willingness and expectation of mutual aid
  -> potential informal protection or public-request compliance
```

It does not observe a dated household shock, who actually provided help,
duration, adequacy, burden, failed reciprocity, formal-service interaction,
material recovery, later trust, or political action. It cannot establish that
neighbor trust prevents hardship, replaces public programs, increases civic
participation, or causes social cohesion.

## Next test

Use a compact place-linked or repeated household design to follow a dated
crisis, care need, heat/energy emergency, migration, or service disruption
through neighbor contact, help requested, help delivered, time/money burden,
formal assistance, recovery, and later trust or movement. Preserve distinct
measures for knowing, trusting, willingness, expectation, actual help,
reciprocity, adequacy, and cost.

## Boundaries and acquisition note

This is a weighted cross-sectional ATP survey with self-reported expectations.
The 2015/2018 comparisons are aggregate trend comparisons, not recontact-panel
estimates. Neighbor, friend, local-community, and public-official request items
refer to different social relationships and should not be collapsed. This pass
retained official HTML only and downloaded no topline PDF, microdata, or large
archive.
