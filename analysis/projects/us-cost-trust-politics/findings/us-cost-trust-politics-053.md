# Finding 053: Alliance legitimacy is real but not the same as operational support

**Status:** bounded cross-national alliance-legitimacy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The bounded finding

Pew's 2026 survey found a median of 65% favorable views of NATO across 13
member states, versus 30% unfavorable. Support was not uniform: majorities in
Greece and Turkey viewed NATO unfavorably, at 59% in each country. In the
United States, 75% of Democrats and 42% of Republicans expressed a favorable
view of the alliance.

## Why it matters for the broader atlas

This adds a public-legitimacy stage to the geopolitical lane. An alliance can
have formal commitments, exercises, procurement, and civilian coordination
while its social permission varies by country and political identity.

```text
alliance capability or commitment
  -> public interpretation and legitimacy
  -> willingness to sustain cost, risk, or deployment
  -> government room to act and alliance durability
  -> crisis behavior and external response [open]
```

The key insight is not simply “NATO is popular” or “NATO is unpopular.” It is
that aggregate support can coexist with sharp national and partisan divides.
Those divides may matter when a capability requires money, troop deployment,
host-country cooperation, or sustained risk—but this survey does not test that
conversion.

## Evidence boundary

Favorability is an attitude measure. It does not show support for a particular
war, base, budget, casualty risk, or crisis response. It does not measure
government policy, military readiness, alliance burden sharing, deterrence, or
adversary adaptation.

## Next test

Link country- and subgroup-specific alliance attitudes to a dated policy,
exercise, deployment, budget, or crisis response. Measure public communication,
opposition, government action, host-country burden, and whether support or
behavior persists afterward.
