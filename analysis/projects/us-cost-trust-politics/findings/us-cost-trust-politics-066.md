# Finding 066: Arts can be a shared cultural resource without being equally reachable

**Status:** bounded cultural-infrastructure and social-connection finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in simple words

An NEA analysis of Census Household Pulse data found that 25.2% of U.S. adults
attended a live performance or art exhibit in the previous month during April–July
2024, and 16.3% created, practiced, or performed art. About 62.2% agreed that
their neighborhood or community had plenty of arts opportunities.

Access was sharply unequal by income. Attendance was 16.2% among adults earning
under $25,000 and 55.2% among adults earning $200,000 or more. Arts attendees
also reported needed social and emotional support more often than non-attendees
in the same survey.

The safe route is:

```text
money, time, place, health, transport, and cultural access
  -> attendance or creation
  -> reported support and social contact [associated]
  -> [open] belonging, organizational continuity, civic capacity, and action
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This makes culture more concrete. Arts are not only private taste or an economic
sector; they can also be places where people meet and feel supported. But the
same data show that the opportunity is uneven. A community can have arts
institutions while lower-income adults have much less practical access to them.

The association must stay modest. People with more money, time, health,
transportation, education, or existing social ties may be more likely both to
attend arts events and to report stronger support. The source does not tell us
which direction the relationship runs.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The HPS asks about the previous month, not long-term participation or durable
  cultural membership.
- Attendance is not the same as meaningful participation, belonging, or civic
  action.
- The results are descriptive associations, not a randomized arts intervention.
- The survey cannot show whether arts access caused support, whether support
  enabled attendance, or whether either changed political behavior.

## Next decisive test

Use repeated or place-linked data that preserve arts access, cost, transport,
attendance or creation, meaning, social contact, organizational continuity, and
a later civic or wellbeing outcome. Test whether changes in access precede the
later outcome.
