# Finding 034: Availability is not use—the conversion cost sits at the interface

**Status:** provisional cross-theme conversion synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

Retained evidence across five domains points to one of the atlas’s most
important recurring patterns: formal availability is a weak proxy for usable
opportunity. A rail connection can exist while only a small share of airport
passengers or workers use transit. A health-care benefit or appointment can
exist while equipment, communication, sensory, digital, or bias barriers make
the encounter inaccessible. A workforce program can enroll participants while
disclosure, interpretation, technology, or outside-training barriers weaken
completion. Medicaid or SNAP can coexist with work while wages, schedules,
household need, and administrative conditions determine whether employment
produces security. A smartphone can provide information and connection while
users report excess time, sleep costs, and incomplete self-regulation.

These are not one pooled rate and do not describe the same people. They are
separate evidence surfaces that converge on a useful mechanism:

```text
nominal capacity or access
  -> interface fit: time, price, information, physical usability,
     schedule, disclosure, reliability, and alternatives
  -> attempted and completed use
  -> protected or sacrificed money, time, health, work, connection, or agency
  -> adaptation, complaint, redesign, switching, non-use, or dependence
  -> public, firm, and geopolitical consequences
```

The main empirical contribution is a conversion rule. Do not treat a route,
program, regulation, device, benefit, or facility as an outcome merely because
it exists. The relevant unit is whether a person or organization can use it
reliably, safely, affordably, and with a workable alternative when it fails.

## Evidence comparison

| Domain | Nominal availability or capacity | Observed use, friction, or outcome | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airport transit | All but two reviewed small airports had some bus/rail service; 23 of 31 large airports had rail, with 18 connecting on airport grounds or by dedicated air-train | Passenger transit use ranged from 4% to 19% at 12 airports with usable reports; about 4% of airport and airline employees nationwide used transit to commute; two visited airports reported 17% and 19% employee use | Route-level headway, fare, transfer, shift fit, completed trips, missed work, and causal effect of demand-management interventions |
| Disability health-care access | One in four U.S. adults reports a disability; federal health systems and accessibility rules provide formal coverage and oversight routes | GAO identified physical, sensory, cognitive, communication, digital, and bias barriers; none of 12 HHS population-health surveys measured most accessibility barriers nationally | Barrier prevalence, failed/avoided encounters, accommodation delivery, continuity, correction, and provider exit |
| Disability workforce programs | DOL workforce programs provide job centers, training, guidance, and accommodation obligations | 7% of participants in reviewed programs reported disabilities; 43% of those reporters did not provide a disability type; selected site visits found interpreter, outside-training, technology, monitoring, and guidance-awareness problems; participants reporting disabilities had worse employment outcomes | Accommodation request and receipt, service completion, placement, retention, wage, and whether access cost shifted to the job seeker |
| Work plus public benefits | Medicaid and SNAP provide public health/food protection alongside labor-market participation | About 13.8 million Medicaid-enrolled adults and 10.6 million adults in SNAP households worked at some point in 2024; about 88% in each group worked in the private sector; selected-state data found repeated large-employer concentration | Wage adequacy, predictable hours, employer benefits, benefit churn/cliffs, household security, worker alternatives, and employer responsibility |
| Smartphone self-regulation | 73% said smartphones help them stay informed and 51% said they help them feel connected | 53% said they spend too much time; 45% tried to cut back; only 25% of those trying reported being extremely or very successful; 41% said smartphones hurt sleep | Logged behavior, notifications, obligation, replacement options, platform design, durable change, and later well-being or civic action |

The units differ: airport and health-care evidence is institution/place or
stakeholder-based; workforce and benefits evidence combines selected program
participants with national population estimates; smartphone evidence is a
self-reported American Trends Panel survey. The comparison is therefore a
mechanism synthesis, not a meta-analysis, index, or estimate of a single
national conversion failure.

## The conversion stages

### 1. Presence is the easiest stage to measure

Administrative and infrastructure records are often strongest at the input
stage. A map can show a rail station, a provider can report equipment, a
program can count participants, a government can count enrollees, and a device
can supply an information function. These records are valuable, but they
answer only whether a capability is nominally present.

The airport review makes the distinction unusually visible. Service existed at
nearly every reviewed airport, but passenger use was available for only 12
airports and ranged from 4% to 19%. The national airport/airline employee share
was about 4%, while two visited airports reported 17% and 19%. Those values are
not contradictory: they have different universes, designs, and local service
conditions. They demonstrate why availability and use must not be collapsed.

The same logic applies to health-care accessibility. “Covered,” “open,” or
“offered” does not tell us whether a person can get onto the equipment,
communicate with staff, tolerate the environment, use the check-in interface,
receive an interpreter, or complete a safe encounter. The institution may
possess capacity while the patient bears the conversion cost.

### 2. Interface fit determines practical value

The second stage is where unequal optionality appears. The conversion cost can
be monetary, temporal, physical, cognitive, informational, social, or
administrative:

- a transit route may not match an early or overnight shift;
- a transfer or fare may erase the nominal transportation benefit;
- a lift, exam table, kiosk, app, interpreter, or communication practice may
  fail a disability-related need;
- a job center may have assistive technology but not an accessible external
  training site;
- a worker may have a job but insufficient hours, employer coverage, or
  predictable scheduling;
- a smartphone may be useful precisely because it carries information and
  relationships, making cutback harder even when use feels excessive.

The interface is not a neutral implementation detail. It determines who can
convert formal access into a completed activity and who must supply the missing
time, money, transport, family assistance, disclosure, or self-control.

### 3. Attempted use and completed use are different outcomes

The smartphone evidence provides a clean stage distinction. Concern is not an
attempt, an attempt is not success, and success is not durable behavioral
change. Among people who tried to cut back, only one-quarter reported being
extremely or very successful, while most reported partial success. The device
also supplies information and connection, so non-use is not costless. This is
not proof of addiction or a particular platform effect; it is evidence that
utility and burden can coexist and complicate exit.

The same distinction is missing in other domains. A complaint is not a
correction, an enrollment is not a usable service, an accommodation offer is
not accommodation receipt, a transit route is not a completed trip, and a
benefit-eligible worker is not a worker with durable household security.

### 4. Unmeasured conversion costs become private adaptation

When a system does not record the failed or abandoned conversion, the person
or household often absorbs it privately. They may drive instead of ride,
bring a family member, delay or avoid care, disclose less, abandon training,
work around an unstable schedule, remain in a constrained job, or continue a
device habit because the alternative is socially or informationally costly.

The GAO disability-workforce review’s 43% missing disability-type share must be
read carefully here. Non-disclosure may be a legitimate privacy choice, not
simply a data failure. But without enough respectful information, institutions
cannot see where needs cluster or whether service completion differs by need.
The correct response is better voluntary measurement and protection, not
assuming that missingness means no barrier.

### 5. The downstream consequence can move from person to firm to state

A conversion failure is not only a service-quality issue. It can alter labor
supply, household spending, public-benefit dependence, congestion, emissions,
health continuity, firm retention, local legitimacy, and political judgment.
The working-benefit evidence shows why employment alone cannot stand in for
security: millions of working adults are connected to Medicaid or SNAP, and
selected states show repeated large-employer concentration. The evidence does
not prove employer intent or wage shifting, but it identifies a place where
firm labor models and public capacity interact.

At infrastructure scale, the same pattern can become state dependence. A
country or region may possess a technology, facility, or supply route while
actual throughput, reliability, replaceability, workforce, and public
acceptance remain weak. The atlas should therefore carry the conversion rule
from household interfaces up through firms, infrastructure, and geopolitical
capability rather than infer power from announced capacity.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This finding connects five themes that are often analyzed separately:

1. household room and consumer choice;
2. health, disability, and care access;
3. labor-market entry and public-benefit dependence;
4. infrastructure, mobility, and local place capacity; and
5. digital attention, self-regulation, and cultural agency.

It also provides a common vocabulary for the end-to-end map:

```text
input or entitlement
  != usable option
usable option
  != attempted use
attempted use
  != completed use
completed use
  != protected outcome
protected outcome
  != recovery, trust, action, or exit
```

This vocabulary prevents a common analytical error: interpreting institutional
activity as lived benefit. The error can appear as a rail binary, a coverage
count, a participant count, an enrollment total, a device adoption rate, a
complaint response, or a policy announcement. Each is a real stage; none is
the whole route.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Low transit use can reflect preference, residential geography, parking,
  shift timing, or poor service; it is not proof that transit supply failed.
- High use can reflect lack of alternatives rather than a high-quality or
  freely chosen route.
- A reported disability does not specify functional need, accommodation, or
  access failure; missing disability type may reflect privacy choice.
- Worse workforce outcomes among disability reporters do not identify whether
  access barriers, health, prior work, local demand, or service mix caused the
  difference.
- Working while receiving Medicaid or living in a SNAP household does not
  prove low wages, employer shifting, or inadequate benefits; the national and
  selected-state employer universes differ.
- Smartphone overuse is self-assessed, not logged behavior or a clinical
  diagnosis; failed cutback does not establish platform coercion.
- These sources do not share respondents, cases, places, or time windows and
  must not be joined into one causal societal episode.

## Next decisive test

The smallest cross-domain measurement improvement is an interface-to-outcome
ledger with a stable, privacy-preserving unit:

```text
need or entitlement
  -> offered route and responsible actor
  -> price, time, information, accessibility, schedule, and alternative
  -> attempted / completed / delayed / abandoned use
  -> protected or sacrificed outcome
  -> complaint, correction, payment, redesign, or recovery
  -> continued use, switching, non-use, trust, collective action, or exit
```

For a first implementation, choose one domain rather than build a universal
index. A route-day-shift airport ledger, an encounter-level disability-care
ledger, or a workforce-program service-to-retention ledger could test the
conversion hypothesis directly. The decisive fields are the offered option,
the usable alternative, the effort or trade-off, the completed outcome, and
the response after failure. The strongest result is not more capacity on paper;
it is lower conversion cost and a protected outcome for people who previously
could not use the nominal option.
