# Finding 050: Family can be a wider social infrastructure than the household

**Status:** bounded U.S. cultural and social-network finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The bounded finding

Pew's June 2025 survey of 6,871 U.S. adults, including 4,271 Black adults,
asked who people consider family and how close they feel to different family
members. Among Black adults who had the relevant relationship, 73% reported
being extremely or very close to a non-relative they considered family, 77% to
a spouse or partner, 66% to a parent, and 63% to a sibling. Black adults were
also more likely than non-Black adults to report closeness to a grandparent
(48% versus 33%), cousin (42% versus 20%), or aunt/uncle (36% versus 19%).

## What it means for the broader atlas

This is not just a family-values statistic. It changes how we should look for
support, identity, care, and consumer behavior. A person may rely on a cousin,
close friend, church/community tie, or extended family member for advice,
transport, childcare, money, emotional support, or help navigating an
institution—even when that support does not appear as a formal benefit or a
co-resident household member.

```text
family definition and belonging
  -> trusted relationship and possible support
  -> care, money, information, or navigation
  -> protection or obligation
  -> hidden labor, adequacy, reciprocity, and durability [open]
```

The cultural insight is that “family” is not one fixed household form. The
consumer and public-system insight is that informal networks may be part of the
actual access route. The inequality insight is that wider networks can protect
people while also distributing unpaid work and obligation unevenly.

## Do not overread it

Reported closeness is not delivered aid. The survey does not show who provided
care, money, transportation, translation, or institutional help; whether the
support was adequate; or whether the helper became strained. The relationship
questions use different denominators, and the evidence is cross-sectional.

## Next test

For a dated care, benefit, health, housing, or consumer episode, record both
formal and informal routes:

1. who the person considered part of the support network;
2. what money, time, care, information, or transport was actually supplied;
3. who performed the work and what alternative existed;
4. whether the formal system became usable because of that support;
5. whether the support was adequate, reciprocal, and sustainable; and
6. what happened to trust, family meaning, work, or later security.
