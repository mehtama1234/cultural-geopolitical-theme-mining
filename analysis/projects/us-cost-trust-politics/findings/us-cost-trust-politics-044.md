# Finding 044: Local goodwill is larger than expected reciprocal support

**Status:** bounded cultural/social-capacity finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-19

## The bounded finding

Pew's March 2025 American Trends Panel shows a repeated gap between what
people say they would do for others and what they expect others to do for them.
Seventy-six percent were extremely or very likely to bring a neighbor's mail or
water plants, while 52% thought neighbors would do the same. Sixty-seven
percent would bring a meal to a sick neighbor or friend, while 42% expected
that help. Fifty percent would loan money to a friend in an emergency, compared
with 45% who expected a friend would loan money.

This is a useful cultural insight: social resilience is not just goodwill. It
also depends on whether people believe help is available, reciprocal, and
usable when a real shock occurs.

## The chain

```text
local proximity and trust
  -> willingness to help
  -> lower expectation of reciprocal help
  -> potential support capacity with uncertain access
  -> actual aid, adequacy, burden, recovery, and later action [open]
```

The gap is not proof that neighbors fail one another. The questions involve
different relationships and hypothetical behavior. But it identifies a
measurable middle stage that ordinary trust scores hide: people may be willing
to give while not feeling protected themselves.

## Limits and next field

- This is a weighted cross-sectional survey of 9,482 U.S. adults, not a dated
  emergency or delivered-aid record.
- Stated willingness is not actual help, and expected help is not a verified
  failure of reciprocity.
- No helper burden, duration, adequacy, formal-service interaction, recovery,
  or later civic action is observed.
- The next decisive source is a place-linked or repeated household episode
  recording help requested, help delivered, cost to the helper, adequacy, and
  later trust or movement.
