# Finding 051: More families are combining paid work, but the trade is not one-dimensional

**Status:** bounded U.S. work-family arrangement finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The bounded finding

Pew's analysis of Census data reports that in 2025, 52% of different-sex
couples with children under 18 had two full-time working parents, up from 31%
in 1975. The share where the father worked full time and the mother was not
employed fell from 42% in 1975 to 23% in 2025.

In a separate survey of 2,242 working parents, 83% of parents in two-full-time-
parent families said the arrangement had a positive financial impact. But only
49% said it had a positive impact on children's well-being, while 22% said it
had a negative impact. Among employed parents in families where the father
worked full time and the mother was not employed, 85% saw a positive impact on
children's well-being, but only 19% saw a positive financial impact and 41% a
negative financial impact.

## What this means

The work-family bargain has multiple currencies. Two incomes may increase
financial room while reducing schedule slack, increasing care coordination, or
changing how parents judge family life. A single label such as “dual-income,”
“stay-at-home,” or “working family” hides those trade-offs.

```text
family work arrangement
  -> income and schedule capacity
  -> childcare, household labor, and relationship coordination
  -> financial, child-wellbeing, career, and time judgments
  -> later security, care continuity, or exit [open]
```

The pattern varies by education and race/ethnicity, so the same arrangement is
not equally available or meaningful to all families. It also does not tell us
whether a parent chose the arrangement, was constrained into it, or had a real
alternative.

## Evidence boundary

The source is cross-sectional for parent perceptions and descriptive for work
arrangements. It does not measure actual childcare quality, unpaid hours,
income adequacy, work flexibility, job security, health, or later family
outcomes. Some reported perceptions reflect employed parents' views and are
not symmetric across all family arrangements.

## Next test

Follow a same-family episode with work hours, paid and unpaid care, schedule
control, childcare alternatives, income, commuting, household tasks, child
well-being, and later job or care changes. Keep financial benefit, time burden,
family meaning, and career control as separate outcomes.
