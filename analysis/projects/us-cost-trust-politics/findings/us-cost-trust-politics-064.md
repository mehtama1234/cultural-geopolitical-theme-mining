# Finding 064: Dissatisfaction with democracy can coexist with belief that it once worked and uncertainty about reform

**Status:** bounded democracy-evaluation and reform-confidence finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

Pew's March 2026 survey finds that 68% of Americans say the United States used
to be a good example of democracy for other countries but has not been in recent
years. Only 24% say it is currently a good example. Separately, 69% say they
are dissatisfied with how democracy is working in the United States, compared
with 30% who are satisfied.

The partisan pattern is sharp: 86% of Democrats and Democratic leaners are
dissatisfied, compared with 51% of Republicans and Republican leaners. That
difference describes current political evaluation, not a permanent party
psychology or a measure of whether people support democracy itself.

```text
democratic ideal or past reputation
  -> judgment that current performance has fallen
  -> demand for reform, defense, participation, or withdrawal [open]
```

## Why it matters

This adds a middle layer between national identity and political action. People
can regard democracy as an important ideal or former strength while judging its
current operation badly. That is different from saying they have abandoned
democracy, and different again from showing that any particular reform will be
supported, feasible, or durable.

## Limits and counterexamples

- These are cross-sectional public judgments, not an audit of democratic
  performance or rights protection.
- Dissatisfaction does not establish nonparticipation; people may vote,
  organize, protest, litigate, or seek institutional reform.
- Saying the U.S. was once a good example does not identify when performance
  changed or which institution caused the judgment.
- Partisan differences may reflect current control of government, issue
  priorities, identity, and survey timing.
- The record does not observe reform demand, reform feasibility, policy change,
  or later civic action.

## Next test

Follow a respondent or dated political episode from a concrete institutional
experience through democratic evaluation, reform preference, information use,
participation, and later trust or action. Preserve people who are dissatisfied
but active, and people who are satisfied but politically inactive.
