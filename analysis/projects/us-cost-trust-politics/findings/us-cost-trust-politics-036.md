# Finding 036: Perceived intergenerational progress can coexist with doubt that the American Dream is attainable

**Status:** provisional cultural-mobility meaning finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

Pew's October 2025 National Survey of Latinos finds that 20% of Latino adults
said they had achieved the American Dream, 33% said they were on the way, 18%
said it was out of reach, and 27% said it does not exist. In the same survey,
57% said their standard of living was better than their parents' at the same
age, while 23% said it was worse; 53% expected their children to do better
than they had.

The nativity comparison is not monotonic: 70% of Latino immigrants said their
standard of living was better than their parents' versus 49% of US-born Latino
adults, while immigrants were less likely to say the American Dream is harder
to achieve (35% versus 48%). Open-ended responses most often defined the dream
through financial security, economic opportunity, or homeownership (about 60%),
but also through a good life for self/family/community (40%), freedom (18%),
hard work (13%), and equal treatment (11%); categories overlap.

The safe interpretation is:

> Reported family progress and cultural confidence do not collapse into one
> measure of current attainability. Mobility meaning, reference-group
> comparison, nativity, material opportunity, and future expectation are
> separate surfaces.

## Evidence chain

```text
family history, migration, work, prices, housing, and treatment
  -> perceived standard-of-living change
  -> American-Dream attainability judgment and definition
  -> future expectation, belonging, attribution, or action
  -> [open] observed income/wealth, housing, consumer, civic, and political outcomes
```

## What the public record supplies

| Surface | Reported result | Still open |
|---|---|---|
| Dream position | 20% achieved, 33% on the way, 18% out of reach, 27% says it does not exist | Standardized mobility threshold, income/wealth trajectory, and causal explanation |
| Intergenerational comparison | 57% report a better standard of living than their parents; 53% expect children to do better | Linked parent/child records, assets, housing, debt, and realized future outcomes |
| Nativity comparison | Immigrants report more parent-to-self and self-to-child progress, while US-born adults more often say the dream is harder | Age, arrival cohort, legal status, language, place, resources, and selection |
| Meaning | Financial/economic security or homeownership is the largest coded theme; relational, freedom, effort, and equal-treatment meanings coexist | Intensity, attribution, policy knowledge, trust, civic action, and later behavior |

These are weighted survey judgments, not an income-mobility panel or a causal
nativity comparison.

## Why it matters to the broader atlas

This adds a cultural-meaning layer to migration and household mobility. A
person can report improvement relative to parents while believing that the
social promise is unavailable to people like them or to their children. That
combination may reflect reference groups, prices, housing, legal status,
discrimination, family support, or changing expectations; the survey does not
identify which mechanism dominates.

Keep these states separate:

```text
better than parents != observed income mobility
American-Dream belief != current affordability
nativity contrast  != migration effect
hope for children  != realized child outcome
meaning            != political action
```

## Next decisive test

Follow the same respondent or family across earnings, wealth, housing,
immigration/legal context, discrimination or belonging, perceived mobility,
future expectations, trust, consumer adaptation, civic participation, and
political action. Preserve people whose material progress coexists with a
negative dream judgment and people who report attainability without observed
material advancement.
