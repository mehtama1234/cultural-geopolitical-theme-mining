# Finding 062: Shared American principles can remain important while their safety and promise are disputed

**Status:** bounded national-identity and political-meaning finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-21

## The bounded finding

AP-NORC's America 250 survey finds broad agreement that freedom of speech
(87%), the right to vote (86%), and freedom of religion (80%) matter to the
country's identity. But almost half of adults (46%) also said freedom of speech
faces a major threat, with another 33% saying it faces a minor threat. The
shared principle and the perceived danger therefore coexist.

The American Dream is more contested. One-third said the belief that hard work
leads to getting ahead still holds, half said it once held but no longer does,
and 15% said it has never been true. Belief varied sharply by party and age:
57% of Republicans, 24% of independents, 17% of Democrats, 46% of adults 60+
and 22% of adults ages 18–29 said it still holds.

The flag supplies a symbolic counterexample: 47% saw it as more unifying than
divisive, but 36% saw it as neither and 16% as more divisive. Half never
displayed it at home. A common symbol can therefore exist without common
ritual, pride, or political agreement.

```text
shared principle or national symbol
  -> perceived threat, promise, belonging, or division
  -> identity and political interpretation
  -> civic action, legitimacy, or withdrawal [open]
```

## Why it matters

This adds a cultural layer between abstract national ideals and political
behavior. Americans can agree that a right is central while disagreeing over
who threatens it, what protection requires, or whether the national promise is
still credible. It is a counterweight to treating polarization as simple
disagreement over values: some values are shared at the level of principle but
contested at the level of present reality.

## Limits and counterexamples

- The survey is cross-sectional and does not observe later action, turnout,
  protest, service, or institutional change.
- Perceived threat is not a legal or content audit of rights.
- American Dream belief is not a direct measure of mobility, income, wealth, or
  opportunity.
- Symbolic display is not equivalent to patriotism, civic participation, or
  agreement about history.
- Party and age differences are descriptive; they do not identify causal
  polarization or cohort mechanisms.

## Next test

Follow a person or civic episode from a concrete rights, economic, or symbolic
experience through interpretation, discussion, participation, and later trust
or action. Preserve people who hold the principle but reject the current
institutional performance, and people who feel the promise has failed while
still participating in civic life.
