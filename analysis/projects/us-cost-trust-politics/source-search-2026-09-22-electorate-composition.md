# Source search: electorate composition, eligibility, and political voice

**Search date:** 2026-09-22  
**Geography:** United States eligible electorate  
**Status:** bounded pass complete; demographic eligibility is not the same as political participation or influence

## Working question

How is the racial and ethnic makeup of the eligible electorate changing, and
what does the source still leave unknown about usable political voice?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |

| US-PEW-ELECTORATE-COMPOSITION-2026 | [The U.S. Electorate's Changing Racial and Ethnic Makeup](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2026/09/10/the-u-s-electorates-changing-racial-and-ethnic-makeup/) | Uses ACS/IPUMS history and Census-based projections to describe eligible-voter composition, growth, and citizenship/age eligibility differences. | Pew analysis of official survey and population data | It measures eligibility, not registration, turnout, representation, party preference, issue positions, or policy influence; 2026 national figures are projections. |

## First pattern

```text
population change, adulthood, and naturalization
  -> eligible-voter composition
  -> registration and ballot access [open]
  -> voting, representation, and policy influence [open]
  -> political belonging and legitimacy [open]
```

Pew projects roughly 249.75 million eligible voters in 2026. White eligible
voters are projected at 61%, down from 74% in 2006; Hispanic eligible voters
are 16%, up from 9%; Asian eligible voters are 5%, up from 3%; and Black
eligible voters are 12%, about the same share as in 2006. Between 2022 and
2026, the Hispanic eligible-voter population grew by about 5.25 million and
the Asian eligible-voter population by about 2.25 million.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

The eligible electorate is already narrower than the total population. In
2024, Pew estimated that 52% of Hispanic adults and 60% of Asian adults were
eligible voters, compared with 81% of White adults and 74% of Black adults.
Those differences reflect age and citizenship composition; they do not show
political commitment or exclusion by themselves.

## Decision rule

Use this source for the distinction between demographic presence, formal
electoral eligibility, and usable political voice. Do not treat population or
eligible-voter growth as turnout, representation, persuasion, policy change, or
legitimacy without a linked registration, voting, officeholding, or policy
record.
