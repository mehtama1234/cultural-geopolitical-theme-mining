# Worker representation and political availability bridge v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Scope:** US work institutions, time constraints, and political participation  
**Unit:** separate population layers: CPS worker/nonvoter and BLS worker
representation estimates  
**Status:** cross-source societal synthesis; not a same-person or causal join

## The broader question

Political voice depends on more than preference. People need time, health,
transport, registration access, information, and some capacity to contest or
coordinate. Work institutions may add a collective voice channel, but formal
representation is not the same as free time or direct political action.

```text
work rule / schedule / representation
  -> pay, time, control, security, and collective voice
  -> ability to follow information, register, vote, organize, or abstain
  -> employer, union, party, agency, or policy response
```

The existing sources measure different stages and populations. Their alignment
identifies a societal mechanism to test; it does not establish that union
status caused a voting barrier or that a schedule conflict reflects a specific
employer.

## What the source layers show

### Formal representation is uneven

The BLS 2025 annual averages report 10.0% of wage and salary workers as union
members and 11.2% represented by a union or employee association. The
comparable membership rate was 20.1% in 1983. The 2025 rate varies by age,
occupation, industry, and state; membership was 4.7% among workers age 16–24
and 10.8% among workers age 25 and over.

These figures measure institutional reach, not practical control. A represented
worker may still lack schedule flexibility, and a nonrepresented worker may
have meaningful individual bargaining power.

### Political nonparticipation has distinct time and access routes

The 2024 CPS Voting and Registration Supplement reports these reasons among
adult nonvoters:

| Reported barrier | Share of nonvoters |
|---|---:|
| Not interested | 19.7% |
| Too busy/conflicting schedule | 17.8% |
| Did not like candidates/issues | 14.7% |
| Illness/disability | 12.4% |
| Registration problems | 3.6% |
| Transportation problems | 2.2% |

The categories should remain separate. A schedule conflict is not apathy, an
illness barrier is not a political belief, and candidate dissatisfaction is not
an access failure.

## Societal interpretation

1. **Collective voice and political availability are separate resources.** A
   worker can have representation but no time to participate beyond work, or
   have time but no collective workplace channel.
2. **The decline of formal representation changes the institutional landscape,**
   but does not by itself prove declining political participation or autonomy.
3. **Time is a political resource.** The 17.8% schedule-conflict category gives
   a population endpoint for the time/control lane, while the BLS layer shows
   where one formal bargaining institution is present or absent.
4. **The cultural meaning remains open.** Security, dignity, solidarity,
   dependence, distrust, and political identity require direct respondent or
   worker-event measures.

## Arrow status

| Arrow | Status | Safe conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Work population → representation | Observed | Formal coverage is uneven across worker groups |
| Representation → practical control | Open | Membership/coverage is not schedule autonomy or appeal power |
| Work/time conditions → political availability | Compared across sources | Schedule, illness, transport, and registration are reported barriers |
| Representation → turnout/action | Open | BLS does not measure voting; CPS does not measure union status |
| Work experience → attribution/trust/identity | Open | Requires same-event meaning and action measurement |

## Next valid test

Build a worker-event or matched-workplace design with:

1. schedule, tool, monitoring, benefit, or safety change;
2. union/contract status and actual grievance or appeal route;
3. pay, hours, time control, health, commute, care, and missed-work effects;
4. information, trust, solidarity, workplace action, civic action, and vote;
5. a comparable workplace where similar technology or workload left control
   stable or improved; and
6. follow-up long enough to distinguish a temporary schedule conflict from
   durable political withdrawal or collective organization.

Until then, the valid broad finding is that formal worker representation and
political availability are both distributed institutional resources, not that
one determines the other.
