# Gallup 2026 moral values, government role, and cultural-polarization layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** Gallup, *Americans’ Rating of Moral Values Hits New Low*; *Moral Acceptability Falls for Several Behaviors*; *Americans Still Say More Religiosity Would Benefit U.S.*
**Field dates:** May 1–17, 2026 Values and Beliefs poll; the moral-values article reports a 1,001-adult national telephone survey
**Status:** current cultural-meaning and public-role layer; not a measure of objective morality, policy effects, or individual moral change

## Why this update matters

The atlas already records religious affiliation, switching, spirituality, and
identity. This Gallup layer adds the public evaluation of the moral climate:
whether Americans think values are good or worsening, whether government is
seen as influential, whether it should promote values, and which behaviors are
considered acceptable. It reveals an important political pattern: shared
pessimism does not produce a shared solution.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Careful interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| U.S. moral values rated poor | 56%, a Gallup record; up 12 points in one year | National evaluation, not an objective index |
| Moral values getting worse | 80%; 15% getting better | Broad pessimism, but causes and meaning are unspecified |
| Government policies significantly affect values | 69%; 76% Republicans, 70% independents, 62% Democrats | Perceived influence is not support for government action |
| Government should promote moral values | 45%; 50% should not | Public is divided on the remedy |
| Republican support for government role | 62% | Party and religious-attendance differences are substantial |
| Democratic opposition to government role | 67% | Opposition can coexist with believing policy affects values |
| More religious people would benefit society | 65%, down from 75% in 2013 | Support for greater religiosity has weakened overall |
| Nonreligious adults saying more religiosity is negative | 55% negative, 27% positive | Religious identity conditions cultural interpretation |
| Religion increasing its influence | 39% overall; 48% Republicans, 35% Democrats | Perceived influence is politically situated |
| Moral acceptability of abortion | 49% acceptable, 41% wrong | A near-divided issue, not a settled consensus |
| Moral acceptability of changing one’s gender | Among the largest party gaps; Democrats 55 points more accepting than Republicans | Issue meaning is sharply partisan |
| Moral acceptability of gay or lesbian relations | 62% acceptable | Still majority-acceptable, with a 46-point party gap |

Gallup also reports significant year-over-year declines in acceptance of birth
control, having a baby outside marriage, gambling, teenage sex, and cloning
animals. At the same time, most Americans still view birth control, divorce,
sex between unmarried people, gay or lesbian relations, and several other
behaviors as morally acceptable. The pattern is selective retrenchment, not a
uniform return to one prior moral order.

## What this adds to the broad goal

1. **Shared decline narratives can hide political disagreement.** Democrats,
   Republicans, and independents all became more likely to rate moral values as
   poor, but the parties disagree about what government should do.
2. **Influence and legitimacy are different.** Sixty-nine percent say policy
   affects moral values, while the public divides over whether government
   should promote them. Recognizing state influence is not the same as granting
   the state a legitimate cultural mandate.
3. **Religious resurgence is not a simple majority trend.** Most adults say
   greater religiosity would help society, yet that share is down from 2013 and
   nonreligious adults are more likely to view it negatively.
4. **Moral change is issue-specific.** Long-run liberalization has plateaued or
   pulled back on selected behaviors, while broad majorities remain permissive
   on others. “America is becoming more moral” or “less moral” is too coarse a
   description.
5. **Party labels are partly institutional and partly cultural.** The same
   partisan groups that differ on government’s role also differ sharply on
   abortion, gender, sexuality, religion, and family norms. These are related
   cultural dimensions, not one latent attitude that can be assumed from party
   identity.

## End-to-end position

The evidence supports this bounded chain:

```text
perceived cultural/moral conditions
  -> attribution of government influence
  -> judgment about legitimate public role
  -> issue-specific moral acceptance or rejection
```

It does not observe a respondent’s personal conduct, policy exposure, local
institution, religious practice, media environment, vote, protest, workplace
encounter, or changed behavior. It cannot establish that government policy
caused moral change, that moral concern caused a vote, or that issue attitudes
translate into social cohesion or political realignment.

## Next test

Use a repeated or event-linked survey to connect a dated policy or institutional
encounter to prior religious and political identity, perceived moral threat or
benefit, attribution, issue position, public action, and later trust or policy
support. Preserve separate measures for national mood, government influence,
government legitimacy, religious influence, moral acceptability, and observed
behavior.

## Boundaries and acquisition note

Gallup’s measures are survey judgments and trend comparisons. The values poll
asks about selected behaviors whose wording and availability differ across the
long series; issue acceptability is not legal status or observed conduct. The
religiosity and government-role results are cross-sectional comparisons with
older readings, not individual panel change. This pass retained official HTML
only and downloaded no topline PDF, microdata, or large archive.
