# Institutional legitimacy, power attribution, and practical dependence v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17  
**Scope:** current US survey evidence on institutions, news, AI, technology,
and political meaning  
**Status:** cross-source synthesis; no pooled trust index or causal political
effect claimed

## The question

What does it mean when people distrust or worry about powerful institutions
while continuing to use them, depend on them, or judge them differently by
partisan identity?

The shortcut is:

```text
institution is powerful → people distrust it → people exit or act
```

The current evidence supports a more precise route:

```text
institutional exposure or perceived power
  → attribution and normative judgment
  → confidence or concern
  → continued use, switching, non-use, complaint, or political action
```

The last arrow is not yet observed as one same-person chain in these current
releases.

## Three current surfaces

| Surface | Current evidence | What it establishes | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Press–government conflict | Pew: 71% describe the relationship as bad; 55% say news organizations have too much political power | Shared perception of conflict plus disagreement over power attribution | Accuracy, censorship, source exposure, correction, subscription change, action |
| AI meaning and anticipated labor change | Pew: 52% more concerned than excited about AI in daily life; 75% of Democrats and 68% of Republicans expect fewer jobs over 20 years | AI concern and job-loss expectations have become politically differentiated | Personal exposure, implementation, control, realized displacement, regulation, voting |
| Institutional confidence | Gallup: 27% average confidence across 14 institutions; Big Tech 20%, small business 67% | Confidence is low on average but sharply institution-specific; party gaps vary by institution | Performance, dependence, practical alternatives, switching, remedy, collective action |

These are not interchangeable measures. Pew’s media items concern power and
acceptable criticism; Pew’s AI items concern anticipation and emotional
orientation; Gallup’s items concern confidence. A low score in one surface is
not evidence of a low score in all three.

## The interesting pattern: legitimacy can diverge from dependence

The strongest provisional pattern is not simply “trust is declining.” It is
that legitimacy and practical dependence can move apart:

- Large technology companies receive only 20% confidence in the Gallup
  measure, but the survey does not show reduced use or viable alternatives.
- People can describe a government–media relationship as bad while still
  accepting journalist criticism of politicians and, separately, politician
  criticism of journalists.
- AI can be more concerning than exciting while expected job effects are
  interpreted through partisan identity rather than a verified workplace
  event.

This is a useful broad-goal proposition because it protects against treating
continued use as approval, low confidence as exit, or concern as action.
Practical optionality is the missing mediator: can a person stop using,
switch, contest, correct, or materially influence the institution?

## What can be compared safely

The sources support a bounded comparison of three kinds of judgment:

1. **Power judgment:** whether news organizations are seen as too powerful.
2. **Normative judgment:** whether criticism by journalists or politicians is
   acceptable.
3. **Legitimacy judgment:** whether an institution deserves confidence.

They do not support a pooled “institutional trust” score. The samples,
question wording, institution lists, time frames, response options, and
political contexts differ. The focus groups in the Pew press study are
illustrative, not representative, and are not combined with the survey
percentages.

## Open arrows

The strongest next acquisition is a repeated-respondent or event-compatible
study with the following fields in one route:

1. institution or system encountered;
2. source, product, employer, or public-service exposure;
3. perceived power and responsible actor;
4. concrete performance, harm, correction, or remedy;
5. confidence, concern, fairness, or identity judgment;
6. continued use, switching, non-use, complaint, organizing, or vote/contact;
7. later recovery, dependence, or changed legitimacy.

Until those fields are joined, the atlas should describe institutional meaning
as a structured set of judgments rather than a causal explanation of political
behavior.

## Boundaries

This synthesis does not claim that media conflict caused polarization, that AI
concern caused partisan realignment, that low Big Tech confidence caused exit,
or that institutional confidence predicts voting. It preserves the distinction
between perception, attribution, norm, confidence, practical dependence, and
action. The underlying Pew and Gallup records remain the authoritative units.
