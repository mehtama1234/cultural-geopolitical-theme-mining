# Finding 001: Disaster response can be prepared while information and recovery remain uneven

**Status:** bounded disaster, public-systems, and place-recovery finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

GAO reviewed 16 selected state, local, and Tribal governments and found that
six had advance contracts for debris removal. Advance contracting can let
cleanup begin sooner after a disaster, but FEMA offices were not always clear
about who should encourage governments to use it.

Public visibility also differed. After the Los Angeles wildfires, the Army
Corps shared an interactive debris-removal map within about six weeks. After
Hurricane Helene, county-specific infographics were not shared until about four
months later. FEMA also lacked a fully leading-practice process for assessing
evolving fraud, waste, and abuse risks in the debris-removal program.

```text
disaster damage
  -> advance contract and contractor capacity
  -> debris removal and public information
  -> reimbursement and oversight
  -> [open] safe return, household protection, local recovery, and trust
```

The broader insight is that recovery has two clocks: the physical cleanup clock
and the public legibility clock. Both can matter for whether residents know what
is happening, can contest omissions, and can plan a return.

## Limits

- The selected governments and grants are not a national sample.
- An advance contract does not guarantee contractor capacity, timely work,
  cost control, or equitable service.
- Reporting delays do not prove field-work delays, and a public map does not
  prove a household is safe.
- Fraud risk is not observed fraud, and the report does not follow households,
  businesses, or long-run recovery.

## Next test

Follow a named disaster and affected place through contract activation, debris
removal, public reporting, reimbursement, household access, business reopening,
and later recovery or displacement. Until then, the safe claim is about
preparedness and visibility, not completed recovery.
