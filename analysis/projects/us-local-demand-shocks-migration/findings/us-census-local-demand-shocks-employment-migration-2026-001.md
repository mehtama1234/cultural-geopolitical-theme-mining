# Finding 001: The measured shock changes the measured story of local jobs and migration

**Status:** bounded place, labor, mobility, and measurement finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An official Census working paper creates a harmonized set of annual commuting-
zone labor-demand shocks using several approaches from the literature. The
shock measures have different geographic and time patterns and are generally
weakly correlated.

The reported responses also differ. Trade-related shocks show little response
in employment or migration, while more general Bartik-style shocks are
associated with economically meaningful changes in both.

The practical route is:

```text
local demand change
  -> shock definition and place exposure
  -> employment response and/or migration response
  -> commuting, household, housing, and service adaptation
  -> [open] durable place change, security, and political response
```

The first lesson is methodological and social at once: there is no single
“local shock” story until the shock being measured is clear.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a place-and-measurement boundary to the program's labor and housing
themes. A place may show little migration after one kind of shock while another
shock measure shows employment and movement. That difference can reflect real
events, different industries, anticipation, timing, or what households do
without moving.

It also cautions against treating migration as the only adjustment route. A
household may commute farther, reduce participation, switch sectors, draw on
benefits, change housing, or absorb losses without appearing as a migrant.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is a working paper; the public abstract does not give the full formulas,
  estimates, time windows, or migration definitions.
- Shock measures may capture different events, not merely noisy versions of one
  event.
- Little migration does not prove little household harm or easy adaptation.
- A meaningful response to one shock measure does not establish an individual
  migration motive or a universal local-shock effect.

## Next decisive test

The strongest follow-up is a protected commuting-zone household panel linking
shock definition, industry exposure, jobs, wages, commuting, moves, housing,
family structure, benefits, local services, and later outcomes. Until then, the
safe claim is that local adjustment depends on how the shock is defined and can
run through employment, migration, and less visible household adaptations.
