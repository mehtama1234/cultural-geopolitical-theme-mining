# Finding 001: AI exposure is not the same as workers' ability to adapt

**Status:** bounded work-and-technology finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in plain language

Some jobs are more exposed to AI than others. But exposure alone does not tell us who is most vulnerable. The NBER study adds a second question: how much practical ability do workers in those jobs appear to have to move into other work if their tasks change?

In the January 2026 working-paper version, 37.1 million workers were in the highest-exposure group. Most of them—26.5 million—were in occupations with above-median adaptive capacity. A smaller group, 6.1 million workers or 4.2% of the study workforce, was both highly exposed and in occupations with low expected adaptive capacity. The published September revision uses different coverage and reports 34.7 million highly exposed workers, 23.9 million with above-median capacity, and 3.3 million—2.5%—in the high-exposure, low-capacity group.

The important point is not which headline count is “the” number. The source changed its coverage between versions, so the figures should be kept separate. The stable insight is that a broad technological threat can contain both relatively well-positioned workers and a smaller, concentrated pocket of higher vulnerability. The low-capacity group is concentrated in clerical, administrative support, and assistance roles.

```text
AI may change tasks
  -> exposure differs by occupation
  -> ability to move or retrain also differs
  -> some workers have options; others face a narrower exit
  -> actual displacement, earnings, and household effects remain unmeasured
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a new version of the recurring pattern:

> Pressure is not the same as outcome. The outcome depends partly on whether people have usable options afterward.

That connects the technology lane to the atlas's existing findings about housing, health, public systems, and military readiness. A benefit, capability, or new technology can exist while the practical ability to convert it into security remains uneven.

It also changes how a cultural or political reaction should be read. People may be responding not only to whether AI is present, but to whether they believe they could control the transition, retrain, move, bargain, or find another acceptable route. This is a testable interpretation, not a result measured by this study.

## What the source does not show

- AI exposure is a measure of possible task change, not proof that jobs will disappear.
- Adaptive capacity is an occupation-level index, not a direct measure of each worker's money, health, care duties, transportation, discrimination, employer support, or local job openings.
- The study does not follow workers through layoffs, training, earnings, job quality, or household stability.
- The revised published counts should not be combined with the earlier working-paper counts.

## Next decisive test

The next useful source would link exposure and adaptive capacity to observed training participation, job changes, earnings, and local labor demand. That would test whether the apparent “capacity” translates into a real exit route.
