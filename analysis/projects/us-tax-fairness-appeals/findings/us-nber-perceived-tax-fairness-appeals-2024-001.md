# Finding 001: Comparative fairness information changes tax-appeal behavior

**Status:** bounded U.S. property-tax field-experiment finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 32588 uses a natural field experiment that disclosed
information about the average tax rate paid by other households. Higher
perceived average rates increased perceived fairness and reduced the likelihood
of filing a tax appeal. The authors estimate that for every additional dollar
paid by the average household, an individual taxpayer was willing to contribute
an additional $0.43.

```text
comparative tax information
  -> perceived fairness and peer contribution
  -> tax appeal or non-appeal
  -> [open] payment, compliance, public-service trust, legitimacy, and politics
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a concrete information-to-institutional-action result. It shows that a
person's response to a public charge depends partly on comparative fairness,
not only on the nominal amount owed. It also gives the material-to-meaning
ledger a useful distinction: filing or not filing an administrative appeal is
an action, but it is not automatically political participation, compliance, or
trust in government.

## Limits and next test

- The setting is a local property-tax process and should not be generalized to
  all taxes, fees, benefits, or political actions.
- The treatment disclosed average tax rates but not why households' rates
  differed; perceived fairness may not equal distributive fairness.
- Reduced appeals do not prove payment, compliance, acceptance, or improved
  household welfare.
- The study does not establish durable belief change, service-quality response,
  or later political participation.

The next decisive test is a linked tax-household episode joining assessed bill,
comparative information, ability to pay, appeal/administrative effort, payment
or delinquency, public-service perception, trust, and later civic action.
