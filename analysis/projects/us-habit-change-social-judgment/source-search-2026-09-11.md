# Source search: US habit change and social judgment

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States-facing consumer question; opening study is not a national US sample  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

Why might people avoid a useful aid when the aid could make their struggle visible to other people?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HBS-ZLATEV-2024 | [HBS Working Knowledge: We Have Better Ways to Break Habits Than Willpower](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/we-have-better-ways-to-break-habits-than-willpower-why-dont-we-use-them) | The article reports research by Julian Zlatev and Ariella Kristal on commitment tools. In one trust task, 69% chose the person described as using willpower over the person using a blocking app; later studies reported lower integrity ratings and less tool choice when others might find out. | Research summary; article checked 2026-09-11 | The opening page does not give a national US adoption rate or follow the same people from tool choice to long-run behavior. |

## First pattern to test

```text
habit or unwanted use
  -> visible aid could help
  -> aid signals a weakness to other people
  -> person chooses private willpower or no aid
  -> harder change, repeated use, or delayed care
  -> firms and public programs decide whether to hide, normalize or expose the aid
```

The HBS account supports the social-judgment part of this path. It does not show that willpower fails in every case, that every aid is effective, or that judgment is the main reason people do not use one.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Some people may prefer private self-control for good reasons: cost, privacy, side effects, ease, or a belief that the aid does not fit the problem. A tool can also reduce freedom if an employer or platform imposes it.

## Main gaps

- US consumer data on who uses blocking tools, patches or other commitment aids;
- whether use changes later attention, health or spending behavior;
- effects by age, income, work setting and social group;
- whether product makers make help look normal or make failure more visible;
- whether a private tool works differently from a shared promise or rule.

## Decision rule

Find one US behavioral or health data source that measures both use of an aid and later behavior. Do not turn the trust experiment into a broad claim about self-control.
