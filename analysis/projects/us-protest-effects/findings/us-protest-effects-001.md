# Finding 001: Protest can create attention without creating broad political change

**Status:** bounded U.S. protest-effect finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

An NBER study examined 14 U.S. protest waves from 2017 through 2022 and linked
them to online activity, surveys, elections, and politician behavior. Protests
usually created substantial internet attention but had limited effects on the
other measured outcomes. The protests after George Floyd's death were an
important exception: they increased Democratic support and legislative activity
on racial issues. The study also reports little general political backlash
unless protests became violent.

In simple terms:

```text
visible protest
  -> online attention
  -> sometimes opinion or legislative movement
  -> usually no broad measured shift
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is an empirical counterweight to the cultural-signaling model. Cultural
messages and protest visibility can enter public attention without necessarily
changing opinion, votes, or policy. Political action therefore has several
separate stages: being seen, changing interpretation, building support, moving
official behavior, and producing implementation.

## Limits and next test

- The study is about protest waves and local exposure, not a same-person life
  history.
- Minimal average effects may hide large effects for specific communities,
  organizers, or institutions.
- The George Floyd/BLM result is an exception, not a general protest rule.
- Digital attention is not the same as persuasion, representation, or material
  policy delivery.

The next test would link a defined protest episode to organizer and community
exposure, message interpretation, coalition growth, official response,
implementation, and later trust or political action.
