# Aging, care supply, and social capacity bridge v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13
**Status:** cross-source societal synthesis; same-family end-to-end outcome remains open

## The broad question

When an aging or ill person needs help, where does the work go? It may be
provided by family, bought from a worker, absorbed by an employer, delayed,
covered by a public program, or left unmet. The social trend is not simply
“more older people”; it is how care capacity is financed, scheduled, staffed,
and distributed.

```text
care need and task
  -> family time, paid care, public support, or unmet need
  -> work, earnings, health, housing, transport, and household security change
  -> employers, providers, families, and agencies adapt or transfer the burden
  -> political meaning: private duty, shared infrastructure, deservingness,
     dignity, and responsibility
```

## What the current sources establish

| Layer | Reported evidence | What it contributes | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| BLS eldercare | 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24; providers spent 3.88 hours on care days, while employed providers spent 2.84 hours on care days; about 51.6% reported care daily or several times a week | Scale, work overlap, frequency, and time intensity of unpaid care | Two-year population averages do not show lost earnings, health, or recipient outcome |
| BLS/ATUS | Care, work, household labor, and social-time records are available for weighted microdata analysis | Time as a scarce social resource | A diary day is not a longitudinal care spell or a price of replacement care |
| NBER linked earnings | Caregiving is associated with later earnings and employment changes, with different patterns by gender in the studied records | A work and income channel exists | It does not observe every task, family division, health outcome, or forced/voluntary choice |
| HBS/work accounts | Workers describe rigid schedules and care coordination as work constraints | Employer and schedule-control mechanism | Accounts are not a representative causal estimate |
| HHS policy review | States have begun introducing and enacting caregiver tax-credit programs | Policy recognition and institutional response | A bill or enactment is not take-up, respite, time, or better care |
| HRSA/provider capacity | Formal shortage designations can coexist with visible health-sector establishments | Presence and adequacy are different | Primary-care shortage is not complete long-term-care supply or a family outcome |

The layers support a societal proposition: unpaid care is a large, unevenly
distributed input into the care system, and its cost can appear as lost work,
time, health, housing flexibility, or future security rather than a care bill.
They do not yet balance paid care avoided, earnings lost, recipient safety, and
the cost of public or employer relief for the same family. The new published
table layer strengthens the scale and time-intensity part of that proposition;
the same-respondent displacement and recovery test remains an acquisition
question.

## Keep the units separate

| Unit | Measure | Societal question |
|---|---|---|
| Older person/recipient | need, task, safety, independence, care received | Is the person getting the needed support and choice? |
| Caregiver | hours, task, distance, sleep, work, earnings, health | Who supplies the hidden labor and what option is lost? |
| Household/family network | money, housing, transport, sibling division, paid replacement | Can the family create a reliable second option? |
| Employer | leave, schedule, remote work, absence, retention, promotion | Does the workplace absorb or shift care risk? |
| Provider/workforce | staffing, pay, hours, availability, travel, continuity | Can paid care cover the actual task at the needed time? |
| Agency/state | eligibility, notice, funding, waiting, uptake, enforcement | Is care treated as shared infrastructure or private duty? |
| Public meaning/action | dignity, deservingness, trust, advocacy, vote, policy demand | How does care become a cultural and political issue? |

## Competing system patterns

1. **Family substitution:** families protect the recipient by transferring
   time, earnings, sleep, and career risk to caregivers.
2. **Market substitution:** paid care buys back time for some families while
   low pay, unstable schedules, and workforce shortage transfer risk to care
   workers and leave gaps uncovered.
3. **Employer accommodation:** predictable leave and schedule control preserve
   employment, but may not provide daily care or prevent promotion loss.
4. **Public infrastructure:** respite, transport, home services, and credits
   can spread cost, but eligibility and administrative burden may exclude the
   people with the least room.
5. **Unmet-care failure:** each option is inadequate, so care is delayed,
   reduced, or carried by one exhausted person until a crisis forces a costly
   response.

These can coexist by income, gender, race, disability, family structure, place,
and job. “Family care” does not mean the same thing across those contexts.

## End-to-end test

Follow a recurring care need for the same recipient and caregiver for two years:

```text
first recurring task and date
  -> task, hours, distance, price, coverage, and replacement options
  -> leave, schedule, work, earnings, sleep, health, housing, and savings
  -> recipient safety, independence, care continuity, and emergency use
  -> employer/provider/public response
  -> later trust, policy demand, or changed family arrangement
```

Compare similar care needs with different schedule control, paid-care access,
transport, family distance, and public support. Include a counterexample where
care need remains high but paid work, health, or recipient safety does not
deteriorate. Measure the option that prevented the loss, not only the hardship
that occurred.

## Reading rules

- Do not call unpaid care free or treat a caregiver count as a cost estimate.
- Do not call employment retention proof that care caused no burden; hours,
  pay, promotion, leave, and schedule control matter.
- Do not treat a tax credit as equivalent to respite, transport, or a trained
  worker.
- Do not treat provider presence as adequate care.
- Do not infer dignity, trust, deservingness, or political responsibility from
  age structure or care hours; measure those meanings directly.
