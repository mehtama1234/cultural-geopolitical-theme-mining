# Finding 017: Grandparent ties can function as social infrastructure before they become formal care

**Status:** bounded U.S. cultural and family-connection finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-20

## The bounded finding

Pew's 2026 synthesis reports that 70% of grandparents ages 65 and older were in touch with grandchildren at least a few times a month, while 21% reported at least 100 hours of grandchild or great-grandchild care over the prior two years. Separately, 8% of children lived with a grandparent who headed the household in 2024. Among adults with a living grandparent, 36% reported being extremely or very close; the share was 49% among Black adults, compared with roughly one-third among Hispanic, Asian, and White adults.

**Why it matters:** this adds an intergenerational-meaning and communication layer to the atlas. Grandparent relationships can provide belonging, advice, care, transportation, childcare, or housing without appearing as a formal benefit or crisis-placement record. The same relationship can be supportive, burdened, distant, or unequal, so routine contact, care work, and shared housing must remain separate stages.

**Do not overread:** the evidence does not establish causal effects on health, child outcomes, work, consumption, trust, or recovery. Contact includes seeing, phone, or texting; care hours overlap a pandemic period; and closeness is a self-report, not a measure of cultural identity or household welfare.
