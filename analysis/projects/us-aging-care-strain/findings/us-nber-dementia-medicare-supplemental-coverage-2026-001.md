# Finding 021: Coverage can become less complete before probable dementia onset

**Status:** bounded aging, insurance, and care-access finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study uses 2000–2016 Health and Retirement Study data to compare 3,001
Medicare-enrolled adults with probable dementia with 14,055 matched controls.

Four years before probable dementia onset, the probable-dementia group was 2.97
percentage points more likely to have Medicare without supplemental coverage.
Two years before onset, Medicaid eligibility was 4.95 percentage points higher,
but Medicaid take-up was comparable between groups.

The route is:

```text
emerging cognitive impairment or changing household circumstances
  -> Medicare and supplemental-coverage changes
  -> eligibility or enrollment route
  -> [open] premiums, out-of-pocket costs, care use, and caregiver burden
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a care-system version of a recurring pattern:

> Being eligible for help is not the same as having complete coverage or
> successfully enrolling in it.

The timing matters. Coverage differences appear before the study's probable
dementia onset, so the practical problem may involve a transition period when
older adults and families are already dealing with changing needs but have not
yet reached a stable care arrangement.

## Limits and next test

- The data are historical and the result is about probable dementia, not a
  confirmed clinical diagnosis for every person.
- The public abstract does not show claims, premiums, plan generosity,
  out-of-pocket costs, care quality, or caregiver time.
- Coverage differences may reflect income, assets, living arrangements,
  eligibility rules, or changing health needs rather than dementia alone.
- Comparable Medicaid take-up does not prove that enrollment assistance would
  improve coverage or care.

The next decisive test is a protected older-adult episode ledger joining early
symptoms, caregiver help, plan choice, Medicaid eligibility and application,
premiums, claims, out-of-pocket costs, care use, and later caregiver or
household outcomes. Until then, the safe claim is that coverage completeness
and enrollment can diverge around a major care transition.

## Source

- [NBER Working Paper 35606: Supplemental Medicare Insurance Coverage for Older Adults with Probable Dementia](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35606)
