# Finding 003: Care is organized through multigenerational living, unpaid substitution, and gendered work tradeoffs

**Status:** provisional 2025 SHED care/work finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey shows care as a social and
economic institution rather than a single household burden. Thirty-three
percent of adults lived with at least two adult generations; 49% of adults
under 30 lived with a parent, up 12 percentage points from 2019. Among parents
of children under 13, paid childcare was used by 25%, while 44% used some
unpaid childcare. Median monthly childcare payments among families paying for
both childcare and housing were $1,083, compared with $1,900 in housing
payments.

Care is also distributed unevenly through gender and work. Among partnered
parents of young children, 58% of mothers versus 12% of fathers said they were
usually the primary caretaker; among dual-earner parents the comparison was
49% versus 8%. Thirty-seven percent of mothers versus 18% of fathers living
with children under 13 were not working for pay. For adult care, 18% of adults
regularly provided unpaid care to an adult relative or friend; 32% of prime-age
caregivers had no paid job, rising to 44% among daily caregivers.

The safe societal trend is therefore narrower than “families are collapsing”
or “care causes women to leave work”:

> Households are adapting to care needs through co-residence, paid and unpaid
> substitution, and unequal caretaker roles; the same adaptation can provide
> support and belonging while also narrowing money, time, and paid-work options.

## What the source reports

| Stage | 2025 published measure | Safe interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Living arrangement | 33% of adults lived with two or more adult generations; 49% of adults under 30 lived with a parent | Co-residence is a major resource and exposure surface, not a welfare score |
| Paid care | 25% of parents of young children used paid childcare in the prior week; 35% among the $100,000+ income group versus 15% below $50,000 | Paid-care reach varies by resources; low use does not distinguish affordability, preference, availability, or nonemployment |
| Care cost | Median monthly childcare payment was $1,083 versus $1,900 median housing payment among families paying both | Childcare is a material budget line comparable to housing for the selected payer group |
| Unpaid substitution | 44% of parents of children under 13 used some unpaid childcare; 58% of working single parents did so | Family and social networks carry care capacity, but hours and displaced activities are unknown |
| Gendered role | 58% of mothers versus 12% of fathers were usually the primary caretaker in partnered families with young children; 49% versus 8% among dual-earner couples | Care roles are strongly gender-patterned; role assignment is not proof of coercion or welfare loss |
| Adult care/work | 18% regularly cared for an adult; 32% of prime-age caregivers had no paid job, versus 23% without that responsibility; 44% of daily caregivers had no paid job | Care and paid-work status co-occur, with intensity conditioning the association; timing and causality remain open |

## Mechanism map

```text
child / adult care need
  -> paid care, unpaid network, co-residence, or direct time
  -> money, work, schedule, housing, and mobility tradeoff
  -> family meaning, dignity, support, strain, or conflict
  -> employer, provider, and public-system response
  -> later security, trust, political demand, or exit
```

The source directly measures living arrangements, care use, selected costs,
caretaker roles, and employment associations. It does not follow a family
through a dated care shock, alternative search, schedule change, health or
income consequence, employer response, or later political meaning.

## Boundaries and counterexamples

- Multigenerational living can be chosen and protective, can pool housing and
  care, or can reflect constrained affordability; the cross-section cannot
  distinguish these pathways.
- Paid childcare use is not care quality or access. Higher-income use can reflect
  employment and affordability, while lower use can reflect unpaid support,
  nonemployment, unavailable providers, or preference.
- Unpaid care is not automatically sacrificed time. It can be reciprocal,
  meaningful, and flexible, but the survey does not measure hours or what it
  displaced.
- Mothers' and fathers' caretaker reports differ partly in how “shared” roles
  are reported; the role gap is descriptive, not a causal estimate of gender
  norms, wages, or employment.
- Adult-care questions focus on regular care and may omit irregular or very
  low-frequency caregivers. Caregiver nonemployment may reflect prior health,
  labor-market selection, household resources, or preference as well as care.

## Next test

The strongest next design is a same-family or person panel that records the
dated care need; paid, unpaid, and co-resident alternatives; price, travel,
waiting, and schedule control; work and health response; employer or public
support; and later return, persistence, recovery, trust, or political action.
The current SHED result should be paired with ATUS diary evidence and a lawful
longitudinal file, not treated as a causal time-displacement estimate.
