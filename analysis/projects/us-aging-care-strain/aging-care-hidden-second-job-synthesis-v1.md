# The hidden second job: where unpaid eldercare time goes

**Status:** reader-facing synthesis of US care and time-use evidence · **Checked:** 2026-09-15  
**Scope:** unpaid eldercare, work, household labor, travel, social time, paid-care alternatives, and public capacity

## The question

When an older person needs help, who supplies the time—and what happens to the
worker, family, employer, provider, or public system around them?

```text
care need → unpaid family time, paid care, public support, delay, or no care
         → work, money, travel, health, and social-time consequences
         → employer, provider, family, and agency response
         → dignity, fairness, responsibility, trust, and political demand
```

The current evidence measures the scale and intensity of the first two stages.
It does not yet follow the same caregiver and recipient through a longitudinal
outcome.

## The short answer

1. BLS estimates **38.2 million** people age 15 and older provided unpaid
   eldercare in 2023–2024.
2. About **23.6 million** providers were employed, including **18.4 million**
   full-time workers.
3. Providers spent **3.88 hours** on days when they gave care, compared with
   **1.07 hours** when averaged across all providers and days.
4. The provider rate was highest among people age 55–64 at **24.1%**; this is
   not only a retired-person issue.
5. Care frequency and relationship change the meaning of the burden: daily care
   is more household-centered and intense, while several-times-a-week care can
   extend across nonhousehold adults and wider kin networks.

These facts support “care as social infrastructure.” They do not prove a
care-induced earnings loss, health decline, recipient harm, or political shift.

## Scale: care is not a private anomaly

The 2023–2024 BLS Unpaid Eldercare release uses the American Time Use Survey.
The published eldercare provider measure covers people age 15+ who cared for
someone with an aging-related condition during the preceding three to four
months, including care for at least one person age 65 or older.

| Group | Population, thousands | Providers, thousands | Provider rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total, 15+ | 272,126 | 38,192 | 14.0% |
| Men | 132,989 | 17,201 | 12.9% |
| Women | 139,137 | 20,990 | 15.1% |
| Age 45–54 | 40,003 | 7,508 | 18.8% |
| Age 55–64 | 41,312 | 9,974 | 24.1% |
| Age 65+ | 58,995 | 9,919 | 16.8% |
| Employed | 174,244 | 23,569 | 13.5% |
| Full-time workers | 138,031 | 18,386 | 13.3% |

Many providers are in late-career years when paid work, health, retirement
planning, and care for parents or partners overlap. Care policy is therefore
also about schedule control, leave, commuting, retention, earnings, and
whether a job can absorb a recurring task.

## Average time and care-day time are different

Among all eldercare providers, 27.6% provided care on an average diary day.
Across all providers, average eldercare time was 1.07 hours per day; on days
when care occurred, it was 3.88 hours.

| Group | Care on average day | Hours/day, all providers | Hours on care days |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total providers | 27.6% | 1.07 | 3.88 |
| Employed providers | 21.3% | 0.61 | 2.84 |
| Full-time workers | 20.4% | 0.57 | 2.81 |
| Not employed | 37.8% | 1.82 | 4.83 |
| Age 55–64 | 30.4% | 1.25 | 4.11 |
| Age 65+ | 39.5% | 1.95 | 4.93 |

A 1.07-hour population average does not mean care is usually a small task for
the person providing it; care days can be long. A 3.88-hour care-day estimate
does not mean every provider gives care every day. Frequency and intensity must
remain separate.

## Care time is more than direct care

On eldercare days, reported activity time included household activities,
helping household and nonhousehold members, leisure, socializing, and travel.
Household activities accounted for 22.9% of care activity time; helping
household members 9.0%; helping nonhousehold members 8.8%; leisure and sports
33.7%; socializing and communicating 10.5%; and travel 5.2%.

These are not a complete “lost-time” budget and do not provide a
without-care counterfactual. They do show why a care bill misses coordination,
errands, travel, family presence, and social time.

Only 2.7% of providers on care days reported working or work-related activity.
That does not prove care caused people to stop working: care can occur on
non-work days, during breaks, or outside the reported diary window.

## Frequency changes the social meaning

Daily-care providers averaged 4.98 hours of eldercare on care days. Providers
who cared several times per week averaged 2.24 hours; those caring once a week
or less averaged 2.28 hours.

Daily-care providers were more likely to report helping household members:
32.3%, versus 4.4% among providers caring several times per week and 5.7%
among those caring once a week or less. For nonhousehold adults, the pattern
reversed: 29.3% of the several-times-per-week group reported helping
nonhousehold adults, versus 6.8% of the daily group.

This is a counterexample to treating “family care” as one universal experience.
Care can be concentrated in one household, distributed across kin, or spread
through a wider community network.

## What remains open

| Current evidence supports | Still open |
|---|---|
| scale of unpaid eldercare | same caregiver-recipient longitudinal panel |
| work overlap and care-day intensity | lost earnings, promotion, sleep, or health |
| frequency and household/nonhousehold context | recipient safety and continuity |
| direct care, household work, travel, and social time | paid-care price and replacement quality |
| care as shared social infrastructure | attribution, dignity, trust, advocacy, and policy response |

The safest societal interpretation is that unpaid care is a large, unevenly
distributed input into the US care system. Its cost may appear in time, work,
health, travel, housing flexibility, and future security rather than in a care
invoice.

## The next decisive test

Follow the same recurring care need for at least one year, ideally with both
recipient and caregiver records: task date and frequency; duration, distance,
and intensity; paid-care price and alternatives; work hours, leave, earnings,
sleep, health, and savings; recipient safety and continuity; and employer,
provider, agency, and family responses. Then measure attribution, dignity,
fairness, trust, organizing, and policy demand directly.

The key counterexample is a similar care exposure where work, health, and
recipient safety remain stable because paid support, flexible work, family
sharing, transport, or public services protect the household.

## Evidence boundaries

The BLS estimates are weighted population/time-use estimates from combined
2023–2024 ATUS data. They do not identify causality, displaced activities,
caregiver-recipient pairs, long-term outcomes, or political meaning. A provider
count is not a care-cost estimate, and employment retention is not proof that
care imposed no burden.
