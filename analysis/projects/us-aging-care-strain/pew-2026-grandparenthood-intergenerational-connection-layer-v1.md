# Pew 2026 grandparenthood and intergenerational-connection layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-20  
**Source:** Pew Research Center, “6 facts about grandparents in the U.S.”  
**Status:** bounded cultural, family, care, and communication layer; not a causal estimate of grandparent effects or household welfare

## Why this is a new layer

The kinship-care evidence already shows grandparents carrying formal and informal child-rearing responsibility. This source adds a different mechanism: grandparenthood is also a recurring social connection that can operate without a crisis placement or a shared home. It therefore links aging, family meaning, communication, care, race, and household structure.

## Recorded surfaces

| Surface | Result | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Life-course reach | Two-thirds of U.S. adults ages 65 and older were grandparents in 2024; the average grandparent was 68 | Grandparent status is not closeness, care, or material support |
| Routine connection | 70% of grandparents ages 65 and older were in touch with grandchildren at least a few times a month in a 2025 survey; 41% were in touch at least a few times a week | Contact includes seeing, phone, or texting; it does not measure quality, reciprocity, or support |
| Care work | 21% of grandparents reported that they or their spouse spent at least 100 hours caring for a grandchild or great-grandchild over the prior two years | The period overlapped the pandemic and does not identify current intensity, necessity, or burden |
| Shared housing | 8% of U.S. children lived with a grandparent who was the head of household in 2024 | This is not all multigenerational housing or all kinship care |
| Meaning and difference | 36% of adults with a living grandparent said they were extremely or very close; the figure was 49% among Black adults versus roughly one-third among Hispanic, Asian, and White adults | Reported closeness is not a measure of cultural identity, wellbeing, or causal racial difference |

## The central mechanism

```text
grandparent life stage
  -> contact, care, shared housing, or emotional closeness
  -> family continuity and intergenerational meaning
  -> possible childcare, social, practical, or financial support
  -> time, communication, housing, and care coordination demands [open]
  -> household adaptation, belonging, consumer choices, and later support [open]
```

This is deliberately separate from the kinship-care pathway. A grandparent can be a regular source of belonging or occasional care without heading a kinship household. Conversely, shared housing or care may be necessary and unequal rather than simply evidence of closeness. The cultural question is how families turn an intergenerational relationship into a usable support network, and who can sustain that network across distance, disability, work, money, and time.

## What this changes in the broader atlas

It adds a bridge between family structure and cultural meaning. “Family” is not only a fallback institution during parental absence; it can also be a routine communication and belonging infrastructure. That matters for consumer and social analysis because support may be delivered through visits, calls, texts, childcare, housing, transportation, or informal advice rather than a formal program. The route can preserve continuity while also creating hidden coordination costs.

## What remains unproven

The source does not establish whether contact or closeness improves health, child outcomes, social trust, spending, work capacity, or recovery. It does not show who initiates contact, whether support is wanted or affordable, how relationships vary by distance or immigration history, or how caregiving affects grandparent work, savings, housing, and health. The caregiving estimate also covers a pandemic-overlapping period.

## Next test

Build a multigenerational relationship episode ledger linking contact mode and frequency to care hours, childcare substitution, transportation, money or advice, distance, disability, work schedules, housing, conflict, cultural or racial identity, household stress, child and older-adult outcomes, and later support or withdrawal. Keep routine connection, crisis kinship care, and shared housing as separate routes before studying their interaction.
