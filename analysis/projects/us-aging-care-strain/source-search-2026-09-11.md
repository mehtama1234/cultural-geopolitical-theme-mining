# Source search: US aging, care, and the hidden second job

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** short discovery pass; no settled finding

## Working question

Does unpaid care change work and household security before it is counted as an economic cost?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-BLS-ELDERCARE-2023-24 | [BLS unpaid eldercare release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/elcare.htm) | 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24; 55% were women; care providers spent an average 3.9 hours on days they provided care | Official time-use survey | Two-year average; time use does not itself measure lost earnings or health |

## First pattern to test

```text
need for care
  -> unpaid family time or paid care
  -> work, earnings, health, savings, or housing change
  -> family and employer response
  -> public support or political demand
```

The first two links have direct evidence. The size and duration of the later effects need a focused study.

## Main gaps

- the cost of care by family income and local price;
- whether workers reduce hours, leave jobs, or lose promotions;
- health effects on the caregiver and the older person;
- who can buy paid help and who relies on family;
- employer leave, scheduling, and benefit rules;
- differences across race, gender, disability, household type, and rural or urban place;
- evidence that unpaid care does not materially change later work or savings.

## Decision rule

Read the NBER paper and one ATUS microdata table. If the data can connect care to a later work or money outcome, build a finding; if not, record the scale of unpaid care and move on.
