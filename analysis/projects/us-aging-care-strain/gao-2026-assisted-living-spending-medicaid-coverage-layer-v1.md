# GAO 2026 assisted living, Medicaid coverage, and care-finance layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-107884
**Reference window:** 2024 Medicaid and Medicare claims; state Medicaid coverage as of March 2025; report published June 2, 2026
**Status:** long-term-care financing and state-coverage finding; not a total-cost, facility-quality, or resident-outcome estimate

## Why this update matters

Assisted living sits between household housing and formal health care. Residents
may need help with eating, bathing, medications, or other daily activities, yet
the room, board, supportive services, therapy, hospice, and personal resources
that finance the arrangement are not covered by one uniform program. This
fragmentation makes assisted living an important societal and consumer trend:
families are choosing among home care, nursing homes, and residential settings
while states and federal programs pay different parts of the care bundle.

The layer adds a financing-and-availability mechanism to the aging-care map.
It also demonstrates why a large spending number can be simultaneously real and
an undercount: the provider category is not uniformly defined, federal programs
cover different services, and room-and-board support from other programs is
unknown.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Federal spending | Medicaid and traditional Medicare spending for services provided in assisted living facilities totaled **at least $12 billion** in 2024 | GAO says this is likely an undercount because assisted living is not uniformly defined or identified in data |
| Medicaid component | GAO identified at least **$3.5 billion** in federal Medicaid spending for assisted-living services in 2024 | This is federal Medicaid spending, not total federal/state Medicaid spending or all resident care costs |
| Medicare component | GAO identified **$8.5 billion** in traditional Medicare spending for health services delivered in assisted living in 2024 | Medicare generally does not cover assisted-living services themselves; the amount is for other covered health care such as hospice |
| State coverage | Medicaid programs in **44 states** covered assisted-living services for older adults and people with disabilities as of March 2025 | Coverage does not mean every eligible person receives a slot, the same service package, or room and board |
| Waiver structure | 29 of the 44 states used Medicaid home- and community-based-services waivers for assisted-living coverage | Waivers can target populations and limit enrollment, so formal coverage can coexist with waiting or exclusion |
| Other federal support | HUD and Veterans Affairs programs may help beneficiaries cover costs such as room and board | The amount of this support was unknown in GAO’s review |
| Resident financing | Most assisted-living residents pay for care using personal resources, while eligible people may receive Medicaid support for certain services | Personal payment, Medicaid service coverage, and total affordability are separate measures |
| Care setting | Assisted living is a residential alternative to in-home care and nursing homes for people needing help with daily activities | It is not one standardized level of care or a direct substitute for every home or nursing-home episode |
| Data structure | GAO analyzed 2024 Medicaid and Medicare claims and state program documents | Claims identify paid services, not all unmet needs, informal care, quality, resident choice, or later displacement |

## The central mechanism

```text
aging or disability-related daily-care need
  -> home, assisted living, nursing home, or family-care choice
  -> service coverage + room/board financing + available slot + provider capacity
  -> out-of-pocket burden, delayed move, informal care, or placement
  -> resident safety, family work/time, housing stability, and public spending
  -> state waiver design, federal payment, provider response, and household exit
```

The important unit is the care bundle, not the payer label. Medicaid may pay
for assistance with daily activities or therapy; Medicare may pay for a health
service or hospice; a household may pay room and board; HUD or VA may help with
housing-related costs; and family members may coordinate or supply care. A
resident can therefore be “covered” for one service and financially exposed for
another.

State variation creates a second mechanism. A waiver can make community-based
care possible for one eligible group while limiting enrollment for another.
Coverage in 44 states does not identify the number of authorized slots, waiting
lists, provider participation, reimbursement adequacy, geographic reach,
quality, or continuity when a resident's needs change. A national coverage
count can hide a state-level access and rationing system.

The data boundary is itself a trend signal. If assisted-living facilities are
not consistently identified, spending can be misclassified across home health,
outpatient, hospice, or institutional settings. That makes it difficult to
compare public cost, resident access, or substitution between settings. A
reported minimum is safer than a precise-looking total that the data structure
cannot support.

## What this changes in the broader trend map

This layer links aging, disability, housing, household wealth, family care,
Medicaid federalism, provider markets, and public spending. It shows long-term
care as a mixed social-reproduction and housing market rather than a single
medical program. The consumer choice is constrained by place, eligibility,
assets, family availability, provider supply, and the boundary between service
payment and room-and-board payment.

It also deepens the care-to-work chain. When a household cannot finance or find
a suitable residential setting, adult children or spouses may provide more
unpaid care, change work, or absorb coordination costs. That arrow is plausible
and important but is not measured by this GAO report; it must be tested with
caregiver and household records rather than inferred from spending.

Read this alongside the child-care workforce and health-accessibility layers.
The shared pattern is that formal capacity and nominal coverage do not equal a
dependable usable service. The populations, settings, payers, and outcomes are
different and must not be pooled.

## What remains unproven

GAO does not estimate total assisted-living spending, total resident out-of-
pocket cost, unmet need, wait time, facility occupancy, staffing, quality,
resident health, eviction or displacement, or family work consequences. It
does not show whether Medicaid coverage reduces nursing-home use, increases
home-to-community transitions, or improves resident welfare.

The $12B figure is a floor and combines different services and programs. The
44-state count is a policy-coverage measure, not a resident-level access rate.
Waiver authority, authorized services, provider participation, actual claims,
and successful care episodes are separate stages. People with similar needs may
face different options because of state, income, assets, disability, age,
family support, or facility availability.

## Next test

Build a state-year assisted-living episode ledger linking:

- resident need, age, disability, functional limitations, preferences, and
  household/family support;
- setting search, eligibility, waiver, application, authorization, waitlist,
  admission, move, discharge, and exit;
- services delivered, room/board cost, Medicaid, Medicare, HUD/VA support,
  private payment, unpaid care, and debt;
- facility capacity, staffing, ownership, quality, geography, reimbursement,
  and provider participation; and
- resident safety, continuity, health, family work/time, housing stability,
  complaint, and later setting choice.

Compare states and facilities with different waiver designs and provider
conditions, while keeping service types and payer responsibilities separate.
The strong result is not a larger covered-spending total; it is a traceable,
affordable, safe care episode in the setting that matches the resident's needs,
without silently transferring cost and labor to families.
