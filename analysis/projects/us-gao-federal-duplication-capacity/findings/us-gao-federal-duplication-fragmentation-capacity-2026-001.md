# Finding 001: Public capacity can be lost in the overlap between programs

**Status:** bounded federal-capacity and fiscal-coordination finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

GAO's 16th annual duplication, overlap, and fragmentation report identifies
610 remaining matters and recommendations for Congress and federal agencies,
including 97 new matters in the 2026 report. GAO reports approximately $774.3
billion in cumulative cost savings and revenue increases from action on earlier
matters, while estimating more than $100 billion in additional potential if
remaining suggestions are addressed.

The report is not simply a claim that government is wasteful. It describes a
recurring coordination problem: programs with overlapping goals, fragmented
responsibility, or inconsistent implementation can make it harder to determine
who owns a result, which data should be shared, where a person or firm should
go, and whether an apparent saving reduced cost without reducing service.

The atlas-relevant insight is:

> Administrative overlap is a distributional issue. It can consume agency and
> implementer time, shift navigation costs onto households and firms, and make
> public performance harder to observe—even when consolidation or coordination
> creates large aggregate savings.

The route is:

```text
overlapping or fragmented federal responsibility
  -> coordination, reporting, and navigation cost
  -> unclear ownership and uneven implementation
  -> service access, fiscal efficiency, and accountability
  -> public trust and political response [not measured here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a cross-government public-capacity layer distinct from individual
program eligibility errors, agency-specific backlogs, or one remedy route. It
provides a portfolio-level control point for asking whether formal efficiency
gains become actual service improvements and who bears the transition costs.

The next decisive test is a named duplication matter followed through agency
ownership, budget and staff changes, data or application route, service timing,
household/firm burden, implementation, and later outcome.

## Limits and counterevidence

- The 610 matters are a GAO portfolio, not a random sample or national rate of
  administrative failure.
- Reported cumulative savings and future potential do not establish household
  savings, service quality, or equitable distribution.
- Some overlap can provide redundancy, specialization, resilience, or multiple
  access points.
- Consolidation can create transition costs, local knowledge loss, or new single
  points of failure.
- The report does not identify a single causal effect of eliminating or merging
  any specific program.
