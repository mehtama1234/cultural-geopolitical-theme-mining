# Finding 001: Switching occupations does not erase the cost of occupational decline

**Status:** bounded labor-transition and life-course finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The finding in plain language

A worker can find another occupation and still lose earnings over the long run
after starting in work that later shrinks.

NBER Working Paper 35614 links the 2000 Decennial Census to U.S. administrative
employment and earnings records through 2020 for more than 2.4 million workers.
Workers whose initial occupation later contracted by at least 25% had about 5%
lower cumulative earnings, even though they worked slightly more quarters.
Occupational mobility was substantial but incomplete.

The practical chain is:

```text
initial occupation
  -> occupation declines
  -> switching, search, retraining, or staying
  -> quarters worked and earnings
  -> household room, identity, place, and later security [partly open]
```

The broader labor insight is:

> Mobility can be real without being a full recovery. A worker may remain
> employed and still carry a long-run earnings loss from the transition.

## What this adds to the atlas

This gives the work-and-technology lane a longer clock. It distinguishes
employment, occupational movement, and earnings recovery, and it prevents a
career switch from being counted automatically as successful adaptation.

## Limits and next test

- This is a working paper; the public abstract does not show every control,
  occupation definition, selection rule, or mechanism.
- The association does not prove that occupational decline caused each loss.
- Earnings and quarters worked do not measure job quality, health, identity,
  family security, or community continuity.
- Children's weaker occupational tie does not prove equal opportunity.

The next decisive artifact is a protected worker-year ledger joining occupation,
industry, employer, place, training, applications, switching, wages, hours,
health, household resources, identity, and children's later education and work.
