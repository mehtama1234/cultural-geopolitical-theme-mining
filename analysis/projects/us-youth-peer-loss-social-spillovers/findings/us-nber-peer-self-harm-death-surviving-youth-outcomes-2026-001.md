# Finding 001: A peer self-harm death can spread risk through a youth social network while care and offending responses diverge

**Status:** bounded youth, social-network, mental-health, and institutional-response finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study uses linked South Carolina administrative records and a matched
difference-in-differences design. It finds that exposure to a peer's self-harm
death increased the probability of a self-harm diagnosis by nearly 50% and
raised both the incidence and frequency of mental-health visits.

The response was not uniform. Care-use and criminal-behavior effects were
concentrated among white boys. Students without a prior mental-health diagnosis
increased felony offending rather than care-seeking. Assault and transportation
deaths did not produce a comparable rise in self-harm, which is consistent with
but does not by itself prove a contagion mechanism.

The route is:

```text
peer self-harm death
  -> school and social-network exposure
  -> distress, diagnosis, care-seeking, or offending
  -> [open] school continuity, family support, safety, and recovery
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a social-network version of unequal exposure and uneven institutional
conversion:

> The same community event can produce different paths—more care for some,
> more offending for others—depending on prior mental-health history and the
> support routes that are actually reachable.

It also expands the meaning of infrastructure. A school is not only a place
where students are counted; it is a social network through which risk, grief,
information, and help-seeking can travel.

## Limits and next test

- The evidence is from linked South Carolina administrative data, not a
  national student panel.
- Diagnoses and visits capture formal system contact, not all distress,
  informal support, grief, or recovery.
- The public abstract does not disclose exact sample counts, full follow-up,
  or the school, family, media, and friendship pathways.
- The comparison-death result is consistent with contagion but does not prove
  one mechanism or predict completed suicide for every exposed student.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected school-year ledger linking
peer exposure, school response, family and friend support, counseling access,
care use, offending, attendance, repeat self-harm, and later safety. Until
then, the safe claim is that peer loss can produce measurable and divergent
administrative responses among surviving students.
