# Finding 001: Physical proximity can change legislative behavior without changing the formal institution

**Status:** bounded working-paper finding on Senate peer exposure · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER working paper uses randomized Senate seating to study whether being
physically closer to particular peers changes legislative behavior. It reports an
11.9 percentage-point voting shift associated with randomized peer exposure. The
effect was concentrated among the closest peers, up to about 19.6 feet, and the
paper reports a much larger cross-aisle effect on final-bill votes.

The reported route is:

```text
randomized seating
  -> repeated opportunity for contact
  -> face-to-face interaction near a pending vote
  -> changed legislative vote
  -> [open] public trust, legitimacy, or durable coalition change
```

The paper also reports that, under its random-seating counterfactual across a
30-year sample, 59 consequential bills would have switched outcomes.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This supplies a measured political-culture mechanism that is easy to miss in
large opinion surveys:

> Political behavior can be shaped not only by stated ideology or formal rules,
> but also by who repeatedly shares physical space and has a chance to talk.

That does not mean proximity simply makes people agree. The result may operate
through access, timing, trust, information, coalition work, or pressure around a
vote. It shows that social contact can enter an institution through a very small
everyday channel and still appear in consequential decisions.

## Limits and next test

- The evidence concerns Senators in a special institutional setting, not ordinary
  voters, families, workplaces, or online communities.
- Randomized seating supports a proximity effect in the study design, but the
  video-observed interaction measure and the pathway from contact to vote remain
  less direct than the seating assignment.
- A changed vote does not prove changed private belief, public legitimacy, or
  lasting cross-party trust.
- The 59-bill counterfactual depends on the paper's sample and model; it is not a
  universal estimate for every legislature.

The next useful comparison is another setting where repeated contact is assigned
or plausibly exogenous—such as committee seating, workplace teams, or school
cohorts—where later trust, coalition behavior, or public action can also be
observed.
