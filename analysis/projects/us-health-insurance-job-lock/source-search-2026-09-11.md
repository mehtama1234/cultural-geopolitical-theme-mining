# Source search: US health coverage and staying in a job

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; coverage and job movement are linked in the evidence, broader household effects remain open

## Working question

How much of a job decision is really a health-coverage decision?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-DEPENDENT-LOCK | [Dependent Coverage and Parental Job Lock](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30200) | Using employer insurance claims and an Affordable Care Act eligibility change, the study finds that more time for dependent coverage increased dependent take-up and parental job retention. | NBER working paper | Job retention is not the same as job quality, satisfaction, pay or later family well-being. |
| US-NBER-EMPLOYMENT-LOCK | [Public Health Insurance, Labor Supply, and Employment Lock](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19220) | After a large loss of public health coverage in Tennessee, job search and employment rose, especially among people seeking employer coverage. | NBER working paper | The event and state are unusual; the result does not show how every worker responds to losing coverage. |
| US-NBER-MOBILITY | [Employment-Based Health Insurance and Job Mobility](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4476) | An early NBER study estimated lower voluntary turnover among workers with employer coverage and high medical expenses. | NBER working paper | It uses older data and pre-ACA insurance rules, so it is historical evidence about the mechanism. |
| US-HBS-EMPLOYER-COVERAGE | [Post-COVID Health Care: More Screens, Less Red Tape?](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/post-covid-health-care-more-screens-less-red-tape) | HBS notes that job loss can expose the weakness of tying health insurance to employment and can increase demand for coverage outside the job. | HBS Working Knowledge | This is expert analysis during the pandemic, not a new estimate of job lock. |

## First pattern to test

```text
job provides health coverage
  -> worker values keeping the plan or covering dependents
  -> job change, retraining, move, or business start becomes harder
  -> wages, work fit, family health access, or household risk changes
  -> policy demand grows for portable coverage
```

The NBER studies support coverage as one reason people stay or seek a job. They do not say that staying is harmful: keeping a job can protect care and income. The missing measure is the value of the coverage compared with the wage, work conditions and opportunity left behind.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Employer coverage may be a valuable part of pay and may help a worker stay in a good job. A job change can also bring better work and another plan. The analysis must compare the whole choice, not label coverage as a burden by itself.

## Main gaps

- premium, deductible and out-of-pocket cost by plan;
- dependent health needs and continuity of care;
- job quality, pay, hours and promotion forgone;
- movement into self-employment, training or a new place;
- differences by health, age, income, race and family structure;
- coverage options outside the employer after a job change.

## Decision rule

Compare the value of coverage with the job and opportunity it keeps or blocks. If only job retention is observed, report a coverage-related staying choice without calling it a loss of mobility or well-being.
