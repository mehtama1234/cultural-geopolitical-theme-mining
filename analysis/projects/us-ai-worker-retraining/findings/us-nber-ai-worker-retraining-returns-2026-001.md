# Finding 001: Training can improve earnings for AI-exposed workers, mainly through moving to less exposed work

**Status:** bounded work-and-technology finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in plain language

The previous AI finding asked whether exposed workers appear to have room to adapt. This study asks a more concrete question: what happens when people in exposed jobs receive occupational training?

Using U.S. Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act training records from 2012–2024, the NBER study compares trainees with similar people who sought workforce services but received job-search help only. For people starting in high-AI-exposure occupations, the estimated quarterly earnings return was about $1,000 before 2020 and about $3,000 in 2022–2024.

The reported gains came mostly from moving into occupations with less AI exposure, and to a smaller extent from training into AI-complementary work. The study also creates an AI Retrainability Index and finds that many occupations appear capable of supporting a worthwhile transition into higher-exposure work under the study's definition.

```text
AI exposure
  -> training or job-search route
  -> occupational transition
  -> estimated earnings change
  -> [open] job quality, stability, access, care/time cost, household security
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This gives the “capacity versus control” pattern a more concrete middle step. Adaptation is not just a trait attached to an occupation; it may depend on whether a person can reach a training system, complete it, move to a viable job, and keep the gain.

It also complicates a simple “AI replaces jobs” story. In this study, the clearest measured route is occupational movement away from higher exposure, not proof that workers stayed in place and simply became more productive. That may protect earnings for some workers while also shifting the time, financial, geographic, and identity costs of adaptation onto them.

That interpretation is a cross-source hypothesis. This study does not measure those costs or prove that the transition was voluntary.

## What the source does not show

- The public abstract does not disclose the full sample size or every matching detail.
- The comparison is within a WIOA workforce-program population, not all U.S. workers exposed to AI.
- Training participation and earnings gains do not prove training completion, job quality, stability, benefits, or worker control.
- A move to less AI-exposed work may be a useful protection, a constrained fallback, or both; the study does not identify the worker's preference.
- It does not follow household finances, care, health, political action, trust, or durable social meaning.

## Next decisive test

Link training entry, completion, time and care constraints, occupation change, earnings, and later retention for the same participant. The key question is whether the measured earnings return is a durable and usable exit route, and for whom.
