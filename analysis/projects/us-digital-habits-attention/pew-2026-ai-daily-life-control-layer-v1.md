# Pew 2026 AI daily-life, control, and public-concern layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-13  
**Source:** Pew Research Center survey of U.S. adults, February 17–23, 2026  
**Unit:** U.S. adult respondent; employed-adult and age-group universes are
preserved where applicable  
**Status:** reported population measures; not a causal effects estimate

## Why this layer matters

The platform and attention program needs to distinguish becoming available,
being used, being useful, becoming relational, and being trusted. Pew's 2026
survey supplies those distinctions in one current population source. It also
places everyday adoption beside concern about pace, privacy, societal impact,
and government regulation.

```text
availability and access
  -> purpose and frequency of use
  -> perceived benefit or concern
  -> control, trust, reliance, and practical exit
  -> household, work, cultural, and political consequence
```

The survey measures the first three stages. It does not observe the later
causal chain.

## What the survey reports

Pew surveyed 5,119 U.S. adults from February 17–23, 2026. Forty-nine percent
reported ever using AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini, or Copilot, and 24%
reported daily use. Frequency remains heterogeneous: 25% used them several
times a week or less, 8% about once a day, 12% several times a day, and 4%
almost constantly.

Purpose is not one thing. Forty-two percent reported using chatbots to search
for information, 38% of employed adults for work tasks, 20% for medical advice,
20% for diet and fitness information, 13% for news, 10% for emotional support
or advice, and 4% for companionship. These items permit multiple purposes, so
they must not be added into a single use index. The work item has an employed-
adult universe that differs from the all-adult items.

Pew also reports an age gradient in brand-specific ChatGPT use in 2026: 61%
among adults ages 18–29, 55% among ages 30–49, 37% among ages 50–64, and 19%
among adults 65 and older. This is an important exposure difference, but it is
not an age penalty or a capability measure. Access, awareness, work, education,
device use, and purpose may all contribute.

## The important split: ordinary use and weak perceived control can coexist

The evidence supports a specific cultural pattern: AI can become an ordinary
instrument while the public remains unsure that people, firms, or government
control its direction. Search and work are more common uses than companionship,
and users are more likely to say chatbots help productivity than hurt it. At the
same time, more adults expect a negative rather than positive societal effect,
most say AI is advancing too quickly, and a large majority expect their
personal information to become less secure.

This is not a contradiction. Instrumental usefulness answers “does this help me
with a task?” Control concern answers “who sets the terms, pace, data rules, and
limits?” A person can use a tool because it is useful while wanting more say
over what it learns, where it appears, or how an employer, platform, or state
uses it.

The emotional-support and companionship measures also show why the product
cannot be treated as only a productivity tool. Ten percent report emotional
support or advice and 4% companionship, but these are population self-reports,
not evidence that a relationship has formed. The next study must measure
frequency, substitution, disclosure, user understanding, ability to stop,
human alternatives, and what happens after an attempted exit.

## Connection to the broader atlas

This layer deepens four recurring arrows:

1. **Platform/data/attention:** AI enters search, news, work, health,
   entertainment, and household devices, expanding the number of ordinary
   encounters in which a platform mediates choice.
2. **Work/control:** work-task use is widespread among employed adults, but the
   survey does not say whether the benefit becomes worker time, employer pace,
   monitoring, or new task demand.
3. **Trust and cultural meaning:** perceived helpfulness coexists with concern
   about privacy, speed, and social direction; usefulness and legitimacy are
   separate outcomes.
4. **Unequal exposure:** age-specific use differs substantially, so a common
   AI environment is not a common exposure. Purpose, access, skills, and
   alternatives must be conditioned before claims about social inequality.

The result fits the program's unequal-optionality finding, but only at the
exposure and perception stages. It does not show who can refuse, switch, appeal,
delete data, or obtain remedy.

The [FTC's September 2025 AI-companion inquiry](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions)
adds a separate institutional stage. The FTC sought information from seven
companies about monetization, data use, safety testing, age limits, disclosures,
and possible effects on children and teens. This shows that governance is
responding to a risk question; it does not establish that the risk occurred or
that the inquiry produced a remedy.

## Counterinterpretations

- High reported use may reflect occasional information search rather than deep
  dependence or an altered social relationship.
- Concern about privacy may reflect general technology skepticism, not a known
  personal loss.
- A negative twenty-year expectation may reflect uncertainty or a desire for
  governance rather than opposition to current use.
- Age differences may reflect different tasks and device patterns rather than
  unequal capability or exclusion.
- Perceived productivity help may be accurate for the user's task but still
  leave employer evaluation and bargaining power unchanged.

## What remains open

The survey does not identify the product, session length, paid conversion, data
exchange, user retention after a stop attempt, error, correction, human
alternative, health outcome, work outcome, or political action. It does not
connect the same respondent's use to a later change in trust, spending,
relationships, employment, or civic participation.

The 2024 comparison is also not a clean trend without a question-universe and
wording audit: Pew notes that the 2024 chatbot question was asked among adults
who had heard at least a little about chatbots, while the 2026 report presents a
broader adult measure and includes multiple chatbot brands.

## Next test

Pair the Pew population baseline with product-level behavioral or complaint
records and a longitudinal respondent design. Follow information, work,
health, emotional-support, and companionship users separately. Measure the
initial purpose, frequency, disclosure, perceived control, actual ability to
stop or delete, human alternative, error or harm, remedy, and later trust or
action. Stratify by age, income, disability, education, employment, loneliness,
and digital access. A longer session or repeat use should not be interpreted as
benefit or manipulation until the user outcome and available alternatives are
observed.
