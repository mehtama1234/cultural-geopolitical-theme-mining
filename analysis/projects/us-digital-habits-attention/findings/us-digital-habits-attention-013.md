# Finding 013: AI trust can be learned through use while privacy control remains costly and incomplete

**Status:** bounded AI-choice, trust, and privacy-action finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-21

## The finding

A June 2026 study of 1,999 U.S. adult AI-assistant users found that platform
choice is task-specific: ChatGPT was the most common primary assistant, while
Claude had a much larger share for coding tasks, and about 43% reported routing
different tasks to more than one assistant. Trust also differed with
experience: Claude's top-three trust share was much higher among its users than
among people who knew of it but had not used it.

The same study found a gap between privacy concern and privacy action. More than
80% reported concern about conversation-data use, about 60% did not know whether
their assistant trained on conversations, and only 18% had paid for a more
private plan. In a hypothetical choice experiment, respondents placed the
highest value on avoiding human review, followed by sponsored answers and model
training use. Knowledge of the training policy was associated with using more
protective tools, but the cross-sectional design cannot establish causation.

```text
task need or default platform
  -> use and comparative experience
  -> learned trust and task-specific reliance
  -> privacy concern, knowledge, and affordability
  -> protective setting, paid plan, multihoming, or no action
  -> switching, data exposure, durable control, or remedy [open]
```

## Why it matters

This adds a cultural layer to the platform atlas. People are not simply
“pro-AI” or “anti-privacy.” They can use several systems for different jobs,
learn trust from direct experience, worry about data practices, and still take
little protective action because the relevant policy is unclear, the safeguard
costs money, or the default is convenient.

It also separates three kinds of power that are often collapsed: the ability to
choose a provider, the ability to understand what the provider does, and the
ability to pay for or activate a safer option. Reported multihoming is not the
same as practical independence from a dominant default.

## Do not overread

- The study is a weighted survey and choice experiment, not platform telemetry
  or a longitudinal panel.
- Reported task routing does not prove actual use, durable switching, or an
  available substitute for every task.
- Hypothetical willingness to pay is not a purchase, a provider change, or
  verified privacy protection.
- The association between policy knowledge and protective tools is not a
  causal estimate.

## Next decisive test

Follow the same user and task across assistant choice, disclosure exposure,
privacy-setting changes, actual paid-plan or provider changes, data retention or
deletion, and later trust. Preserve continued use despite concern as a distinct
outcome from informed acceptance, constrained dependence, and successful exit.
