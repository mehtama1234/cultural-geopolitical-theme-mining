# Finding 019: Americans can agree that the press-government relationship is bad while disagreeing about media power and criticism

**Status:** bounded political-media legitimacy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The finding in plain language

Pew's June 2026 survey found that 71% of U.S. adults viewed the relationship between the Trump administration and news media as very or somewhat bad, while 11% viewed it as good. That is a broad shared diagnosis.

But the diagnosis did not produce one shared interpretation. Fifty-five percent said news organizations have too much power in politics, including 69% of Republicans and Republican leaners compared with 43% of Democrats and Democratic leaners. At the same time, 68% said it is acceptable for journalists to criticize politicians, while 56% said it is acceptable for politicians to criticize journalists.

```text
shared judgment: the relationship is bad
  -> different views of who has power and who may criticize whom
  -> different legitimacy standards
  -> [open] trust, media use, retaliation, censorship, or political action
```

## What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a useful cultural-political distinction:

> Agreement that an institution is in conflict does not mean agreement about who is legitimate, who is too powerful, or what criticism should be allowed.

It connects the media lane to the broader findings on partisan meaning, public-system legitimacy, and cultural identity. A population can share a descriptive perception while splitting over responsibility and acceptable response. That split may matter more for political behavior than the shared diagnosis itself, but this source does not measure that next step.

## What the source does not show

- These are perceptions and norms, not independently coded measures of censorship, accuracy, ownership, or actual institutional power.
- Partisan differences may reflect political identity, media use, and whether a respondent's party is governing or opposing.
- Approval of criticism does not mean approval of every tactic, tone, retaliation, or legal constraint.
- The source does not measure trust as a general construct, voting, protest, media switching, or causal media influence.
- Focus groups are illustrative and not representative; they are not pooled with survey percentages.

## Next decisive test

Follow repeated respondents or dated events from a press-government conflict through media use, trust, attribution, retaliation or policy demand, and later political action. Keep shared diagnosis separate from divergent legitimacy judgments.
