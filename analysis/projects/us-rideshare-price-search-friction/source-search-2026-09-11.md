# Source search: US rideshare price search

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; matched prices and app search are measured in New York City, the broader household path remains open

## Working question

When two apps offer the same trip at different prices, why do many customers not check both?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-RIDESHARE-SEARCH | [Leaving Money on the Dashboard: Price Dispersion and Search Frictions on Uber and Lyft](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34441) | An audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute price gap of about $3.50, or roughly 14% of the average fare. Device data show that only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other, even though the two apps were not consistently cheaper or more expensive. | NBER working paper and digest | The study covers one city and a short price window. It does not show each rider's income, reason for choosing an app, service quality, final cost or later switching. |

## First pattern to test

```text
two platforms show different prices
  -> effort and habit of checking
  -> app chosen
  -> fare paid and money left
  -> customer power and platform competition
```

The source supports price gaps and low comparison. It does not establish why each rider chose one app or the full welfare effect.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

People may value wait time, reliability, discounts, account history or habit more than the lowest displayed fare. A higher price can buy a better service or save time.

## Main gaps

- rider income and time value;
- wait time, cancellation and service quality;
- discounts and account rewards;
- repeated trips and switching;
- platform rules that limit comparison tools;
- effects outside New York City.

## Decision rule

Keep posted price, comparison effort, service quality, chosen app and total customer value as separate steps.
