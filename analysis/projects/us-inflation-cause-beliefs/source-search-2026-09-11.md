# Source search: US inflation cause beliefs

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; beliefs and policy preferences are measured, later action remains open

## Working question

How does a household price become a story about responsibility and action?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-INFLATION-BELIEFS | [People's Understanding of Inflation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32497) | A US survey with information and choice experiments finds that people see inflation as broadly negative, name several different causes, and differ by party and news source. Many respondents understate the economic tradeoffs of fighting inflation and support policies that do not always match their stated cause. | NBER working paper | It does not link one person's price basket, information path, belief and later vote or household action. |
| US-NBER-REAL-WAGES-VOTE | [Do Voters Punish Inflation or Pay Cuts?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301) | County-level US evidence provides a separate test of how local purchasing power and inflation relate to election results. | NBER working paper | County results do not identify each voter's story, information source or reason for voting. |
| US-NBER-FED-TRUST | [Perceived Political Bias of the Federal Reserve](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33071) | Trust in the messenger changes how much weight consumers give an inflation message. | NBER working paper | It measures trust and belief updates, not the full political response. |

## First pattern to test

```text
household price or pay loss
  -> cause named and source believed
  -> policy tradeoff judged
  -> trust, blame, vote or demand for action
```

The sources support differences in perceived cause, policy preference and some economic voting outcomes. They do not establish one complete household-to-ballot path.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Different explanations may reflect different real exposures rather than simple misinformation. A person can blame a firm and still support a government response, or distrust a messenger and accept part of its information.

## Main gaps

- household price basket and income change;
- party identity, news source and local experience;
- belief about cause and policy tradeoff;
- trust, blame, turnout and vote;
- changes in spending, saving and borrowing;
- differences by income, race, age, place and education.

## Decision rule

Keep price experience, cause belief, policy preference, trust and political action separate. A stated explanation is evidence of belief, not proof of the economic cause.
