# Finding 001: People can share a safety goal while disagreeing about the means

**Status:** bounded working-paper finding on safety beliefs and firearm choices · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER working paper uses an original U.S. survey of more than 5,400 people and
randomized information experiments with firearm owners and non-owners. It finds
that both groups describe protection of themselves or family as a central goal,
but they disagree sharply about whether lethal firearms produce safety.

The paper reports that owners feel safer and more confident with firearms,
while non-owners generally feel less safe around them. About one-third of
non-owners say they are interested in acquiring a lethal firearm, and this group
reports the lowest day-to-day safety.

The route is:

```text
shared goal: safety
  -> different threat experiences and beliefs about the tool
  -> lethal firearm, non-lethal alternative, or no weapon
  -> policy and storage preferences
  -> [open] actual behavior and realized safety
```

## What the experiments add

Information about the private legal and medical costs of lethal ownership made
people more concerned about harms and more supportive of safe-storage policies,
with a modest increase in stated willingness to keep lethal firearms locked.

Information about a non-lethal firearm increased stated willingness to pay for
that alternative, support for incapacitating rather than lethal tools, and
support for policies encouraging alternatives. The paper reports that this did
not make current owners want to give up lethal firearms. The alternative-tool
effects were more persistent than the private-cost treatment.

The practical lesson is narrow but important:

> A shared value does not automatically produce a shared policy. Agreement on
> safety can coexist with different beliefs about what safety requires.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a cultural-political version of the program's recurring “same goal,
different route” pattern. Political conflict may be less about whether people
want protection and more about their different maps of risk, identity, cost,
and acceptable tradeoffs.

It also supplies a useful correction to a simple persuasion story. Information
can move safer margins—storage, alternatives, or policy support—without
removing the identity-linked core choice. A changed attitude about storage is
not the same as relinquishing a firearm, and neither is the same as lower
community harm.

## Limits and next test

- The study measures reported feelings, attitudes, stated willingness to pay,
  and stated storage or ownership preferences, not completed behavior or injury
  reduction.
- The survey and experiment have study-specific subgroup denominators and do
  not establish a national causal effect of firearm ownership on safety.
- Owners and non-owners may differ in threat environment, experience, identity,
  local norms, and selection into ownership; the shared safety goal does not
  erase those differences.
- The information treatments do not show durable policy change, actual adoption
  of non-lethal tools, or lower violence.

The next useful test is a privacy-protected household or place record joining
perceived safety, tool choice, storage, actual exposure, local threat, policy
change, and later safety outcomes. Until then, the safe claim is that safety
beliefs and tool preferences can diverge even when the stated goal is shared.
