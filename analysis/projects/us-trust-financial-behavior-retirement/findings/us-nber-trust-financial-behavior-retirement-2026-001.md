# Finding 001: Institutional trust is not one financial mechanism

**Status:** bounded U.S. trust-to-financial-behavior finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

Using the Health and Retirement Study, NBER Working Paper 35220 examines how
trust and financial literacy relate to retirement-security behavior. Trust in
financial institutions aligns with behaviors such as retirement-account
ownership, stockholding, and household wealth, while trust in government
programs does not show the same positive alignment and is linked to lower
stockholding and wealth among minority households.

The relationship is heterogeneous: the positive financial-institution-trust
association appears for White respondents but not in the same form for Black
or Hispanic respondents. Financial literacy is also associated with retirement
behavior.

```text
institutional encounter and accumulated experience
  -> domain-specific trust
  -> financial choice and participation
  -> wealth and retirement-security position
  -> [open] adequacy, legitimacy, political action, or later resilience
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This closes an important observed middle between cultural meaning and material
behavior. “Trust” is not a single general attitude: the institution being
trusted, the group experiencing it, and the practical financial route all
matter. A program can have formal availability while trust, access, and
participation diverge across groups.

## Limits and next test

- The evidence is observational and does not establish that trust caused the
  financial behavior.
- The public abstract does not provide a common analytic denominator or all
  subgroup specifications.
- Older-household results should not be generalized automatically to younger
  workers or current retirement systems.
- Wealth and account ownership are not the same as adequate retirement income,
  financial wellbeing, or durable institutional legitimacy.

The next decisive test links dated institutional encounters, fees or advice,
trust change, account access, saving or withdrawal decisions, wealth, later
retirement income, and any switching, non-use, or political response.
