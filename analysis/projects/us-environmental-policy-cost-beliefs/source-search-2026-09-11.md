# Source search: US environmental policy cost beliefs

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States  
**Status:** opening pass; policy beliefs and a political-choice model are described, actual household bills and environmental results remain open

## Working question

When people think one climate or energy policy will raise their bills, how does that belief shape the policy they support?

## Opening source

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-ENVIRONMENTAL-INSTRUMENTS | [Understanding Support for Cost-Ineffective Environmental Policy Instruments](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35073) | The NBER study finds that respondents think market-based environmental tools raise consumer energy bills more than standards do, unlike expert environmental economists. Its model shows that political limits and imperfect cost beliefs can push policy toward a less cost-effective standard. | NBER working paper | The opening evidence does not show each household's actual bill, policy knowledge, pollution result or later political action. |

## First pattern to test

```text
policy tool
  -> expected household bill
  -> support or opposition
  -> chosen public rule
  -> actual cost and environmental result
```

The source supports a difference in cost beliefs and a model of policy choice. It does not establish the full household result.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

People may care about fairness, control and trust, not only total cost. A standard can be easier to understand or enforce even if an economist judges another tool more efficient.

## Main gaps

- actual policy cost by household;
- energy use and income;
- who receives rebates or protection;
- pollution and health results;
- trust in experts, firms and government;
- support after people see the real bill.

## Decision rule

Keep expected cost, actual bill, policy support, environmental result and fairness judgment as separate steps until evidence links them.
