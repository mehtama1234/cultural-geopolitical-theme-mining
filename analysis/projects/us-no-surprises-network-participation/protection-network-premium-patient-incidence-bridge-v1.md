# No Surprises protection: from patient liability to network and household incidence

**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Status:** bounded consumer-protection and provider-market realization pass  
**Scope:** surprise-bill protection, arbitration, provider participation,
network breadth, premiums, patient cost, appointment access, and care continuity

## What we found

The No Surprises Act limits patient liability for covered surprise bills and
creates final-offer arbitration for insurer-provider payment disputes. GAO's
claims analysis found that in-network claim shares rose after implementation in
three of four selected specialties, while several inflation-adjusted payment
trends largely continued directions that began before the law.

NBER Working Paper 35717 adds the bargaining mechanism. It links arbitration
records to a national provider-insurer network panel and ACA marketplace plan
data. Arbitration was high-volume and provider-favorable, with awards above the
statutory benchmark. Provider network participation declined in specialties and
states most affected by the reform, including after provider groups' first
favorable arbitration outcomes. The paper also reports suggestive evidence that
more exposed insurers raised premiums.

The combined finding is not that the Act failed. It is that a consumer
protection can remove liability at the point of care while changing the outside
options of providers and the prices or network choices faced by plans and
households.

## The deeper question

When does protection from a surprise bill become durable household care security,
and when does it shift cost, network restriction, waiting, or provider choice to
another stage of the health system?

## Why this matters

Patients do not experience a policy as an arbitration award or network share.
They experience whether they can find a clinician, wait an acceptable time,
pay the premium and cost sharing, receive continuous care, and avoid debt. A
law can improve one point in that chain while worsening or leaving unchanged
another. Measuring only the protected bill or the provider's payment can miss
the household result.

Priority is **high** for decision value, missing-link value, connection value,
and disconfirmation value. Effort is medium and scope risk is high because
claims, arbitration, provider, plan, and patient units must be joined without
turning a market-level study into an individual effect.

## What we know so far

- Patient liability is capped for covered surprise-bill situations, while
  provider-insurer payment disputes move through arbitration.
- GAO's selected-specialty claims data show a post-law network signal, but
  payment trends do not all move in the same direction or begin with the law.
- NBER links arbitration exposure to provider participation and reports
  suggestive insurer-premium effects.
- Neither source establishes patient-specific out-of-pocket spending, debt,
  appointment wait, travel, provider substitution, treatment continuity,
  health, or household financial recovery.

## What could change the answer

The protection would look like durable household security if matched claims and
plan records show lower patient liability without offsetting premium or
cost-sharing increases, stable network access, acceptable waits, continuous
treatment, and lower medical-debt or delayed-care outcomes.

It would look more like cost displacement if premiums, deductibles, travel,
waits, or network exits rose for affected patients, or if patients could not
find equivalent substitutes. It would also weaken a harm claim if narrowed
networks retained equivalent local capacity and patient costs fell.

## Challenge checks

- Arbitration awards were not treated as patient bills or household savings.
- Network participation was not treated as usable access or adequate care.
- Premium changes were not attributed solely to arbitration without the paper's
  design and assumptions.
- GAO's four specialties and 2019–2023 claims were not generalized to every
  patient, service, or insurer.
- The NBER national panel and GAO claims comparison were retained as related
  but non-identical evidence, not pooled estimates.
- Patients with chronic illness, disability, rural travel, limited plan choice,
  employer coverage changes, and low financial room remain visible alongside
  providers, plans, employers, and regulators.

## Current answer

**Partly answered.** The Act changes a formal protection and appears to change
provider-market behavior in affected settings. The evidence does not yet show
whether patients receive lower total costs, better access, continuous care, or
more durable household security.

## What to look for next

```text
covered surprise-bill exposure
  -> patient liability protection and provider/plan dispute
  -> arbitration, negotiated payment, and network status
  -> premium, deductible, cost sharing, travel, wait, and provider choice
  -> treatment continuity, health, debt, delayed care, and household room
  -> complaint, appeal, switching, trust, or exit
```

Build a privacy-protected claim-to-care ledger with service date, specialty,
network status, arbitration exposure, patient liability, premium and
cost-sharing path, appointment search and wait, substitution, treatment
continuity, and later financial outcome. Keep unaffected or substitute-provider
cases as counterexamples.

## Stopping rule

Stop this pass when one compatible patient or claim-to-care cohort links
protected liability, network and payment status, premium/cost sharing,
appointment access, treatment continuity, and later household outcome, with a
credible substitute-provider or unaffected counterexample. If the evidence
stops at arbitration, network, or premium aggregates, retain the patient-
incidence gap instead of calling the law successful or harmful overall.
