# Finding 002: No-Surprises arbitration can strengthen provider bargaining power while narrowing networks and raising premiums

**Status:** bounded provider-market and consumer-protection finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

The No Surprises Act capped patients' liability for certain surprise bills and
created federal final-offer arbitration for insurer-provider payment disputes.
An NBER study links arbitration records to a national longitudinal
provider-insurer network panel and ACA marketplace plan data.

The study reports that arbitration was high-volume and provider-favorable, with
awards well above the statutory benchmark. Provider network participation fell
in the specialties and states most affected by the reform, including after
provider groups' first favorable arbitration outcomes. The paper also reports
suggestive evidence that insurers more exposed to arbitration raised premiums.

The route is:

```text
surprise-bill protection and final-offer arbitration
  -> provider outside option and insurer-provider bargaining
  -> network participation and negotiated payment
  -> premiums and network breadth
  -> [open] appointment access, patient cost, care continuity, health, and household security
```

## What this adds to the atlas

This is a stronger provider-power complement to the existing GAO claims
surface. It shows how a formal consumer protection can alter the market power
of an intermediate actor. The patient may be protected from a particular
surprise bill while the system responds through network design or premiums.

That is not evidence that the law failed. It is a reminder that protection,
provider bargaining, insurer pricing, usable access, and household welfare are
different stages and must be measured separately.

## Limits and next test

- The public abstract does not disclose every identification assumption, sample
  restriction, coefficient, or network definition.
- Provider participation is not the same as patient access; a narrower network
  may or may not reduce usable choice depending on substitutes and travel.
- The premium result is described as suggestive and does not isolate every
  insurer cost or selection channel.
- The study does not establish patient-specific bills, appointment waits, care
  continuity, health, or financial recovery.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected claim-to-care ledger linking
network status, arbitration exposure, patient liability, appointment search and
wait, provider substitution, premium and cost-sharing changes, treatment
continuity, and later household financial outcomes.
