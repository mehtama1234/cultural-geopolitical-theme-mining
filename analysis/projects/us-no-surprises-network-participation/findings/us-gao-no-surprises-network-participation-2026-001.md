# Finding 001: A billing protection can change provider participation without proving lower patient costs

**Status:** bounded health-insurance, provider-market, and consumer-cost finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The No Surprises Act generally prohibits out-of-network providers from
balance-billing privately insured patients in certain circumstances, including
emergency care. GAO compared claims and payments from 2019 through 2023 for
emergency medicine, radiology, anesthesiology, and air ambulance—the specialties
most likely to be affected by the protection.

The share of in-network claims increased after the law took effect for three of
the four specialties. Emergency-medicine facility and professional in-network
claim shares declined before the law, then increased afterward. This may mean
that more providers participated in networks, but GAO describes the claim trend
as an indicator, not proof of the law's sole effect.

Payment changes tell a different story. Inflation-adjusted facility payments
for in-network emergency services increased in 2022 and 2023, while physician
or practice payments decreased. GAO says these largely continued trends that
began before the law.

The route is:

```text
legal ban on some surprise bills
  -> provider network participation
  -> negotiated payment and claim status
  -> patient bill and financial responsibility
  -> affordability, care choice, and financial security [not measured here]
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a provider-market version of the atlas's recurring conversion problem:

> A formal consumer protection can change the behavior of firms or providers
> without showing whether the affected household paid less, received better
> care, or gained durable choice.

It also provides a useful counterexample to simple policy attribution: network
claims shifted after implementation, while several payment trends continued
their pre-law direction.

## Limits and counterevidence

- GAO studied four selected specialties and claims from 2019–2023; this is not
  every service, insurer, patient, or surprise-bill event.
- In-network claims are an indicator of participation, not a direct measure of
  provider contracting or patient experience.
- The before-and-after comparison does not isolate the law from other market
  changes.
- The report does not estimate patient bills, total out-of-pocket costs,
  avoided debt, care delays, or health outcomes.
- Payment increases or decreases do not by themselves show who gained or lost
  bargaining power.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected claim and bill route joining
network status, law applicability, negotiated payment, patient liability,
dispute or correction, care continuity, and later financial outcome.
