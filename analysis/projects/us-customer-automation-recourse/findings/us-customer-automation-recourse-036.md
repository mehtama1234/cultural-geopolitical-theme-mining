# Finding 036: Delivery convenience can turn into a pricing and recourse problem

**Status:** bounded platform pricing, consumer-recourse, and market-governance finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The FTC announced that Instacart would pay $60 million in consumer refunds to
settle allegations about delivery-fee disclosures, automatic paid membership
enrollment, and difficult refund or cancellation routes. The FTC alleged that
some consumers saw “free delivery” claims but encountered service fees, and
that some free-trial users were enrolled in Instacart+ without express
informed consent.

The enforcement route is:

```text
delivery convenience and free-trial offer
  -> fee, membership, and refund design
  -> consumer charge or confusion
  -> complaint, enforcement, and announced refund
  -> [open] individual receipt, continued use, switching, and trust
```

The later FTC request for public comment on online food-delivery fees expands
the question beyond one company: how platforms disclose mandatory charges,
refundability, recipients, and consent.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a consumer-market version of the recurring offer-versus-usability
pattern:

> Convenience can be real while the price, consent, and exit route remain hard
> to see or use.

The important cultural point is that delivery platforms are not only shopping
tools. They shape what counts as a normal price, how much time a household
spends contesting a charge, and whether “free,” “subscription,” and “refund”
feel like understandable promises or traps.

## Limits and next test

- The FTC materials describe allegations and settlement terms, not a
  representative estimate of all Instacart users' experiences.
- The public materials used here do not show how many consumers received
  refunds, the amount each received, or the time spent seeking relief.
- A firm rule change or refund pool does not prove lower grocery costs,
  restored trust, continued use, or switching.
- Some customers may have understood the terms and valued the service; a
  settlement does not make every charge unwanted.

The next decisive artifact is a protected order-level ledger joining the
advertised price, final fees, membership consent, cancellation attempt,
refund request, refund receipt, repeat purchase, and later platform choice.
Until then, the safe claim is that platform pricing and subscription design
can create a visible enforcement and refund route without closing the
individual consumer-recovery chain.
