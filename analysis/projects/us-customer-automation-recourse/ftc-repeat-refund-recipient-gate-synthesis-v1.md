# Finding 034: Repeat refunds are often gated by prior reach

**Status:** bounded cross-case FTC refund-process synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-10-19

## The bounded finding

Four current FTC refund routes show a consistent administrative pattern:

- **ACRO Services:** 5,685 second-round checks totaling more than $6.2 million
  were sent to people who accepted the first payment; the first round produced
  more than $3.8 million in refunds.
- **Next-Gen:** 104,820 second-round checks totaling more than $13.5 million
  were sent to people who cashed the first check; the first round produced more
  than $10.7 million in refunds.
- **American Vehicle Protection:** 9,074 second-round checks totaling more
  than $186,000 were sent to people who accepted the first payment; the first
  round produced more than $223,000 in refunds.
- **Yellowstone Capital:** 3,759 third-round checks totaling more than
  $549,000 were sent to small businesses that had cashed a previous check; the
  first two rounds produced more than $9.1 million in refunds.

The FTC's general refund-process guidance says additional payments generally
require that the consumer cashed the previous payment. It also describes
address updating, reissue, and the possibility that unclaimed funds are not
usable by the original recipient.

The FTC's current process description adds system-level scale: in 2024 the
agency sent first-round payments in 33 cases and additional payments in 22
cases, while tracking which payments reached affected consumers. This confirms
that delivery, cashing, reissue, and repeat-distribution stages are part of the
operating remedy system. It is not a common claimant-level recovery rate.

Cleo AI provides a useful contrast rather than another repeat-refund row. The
FTC announced 2,124,796 planned PayPal payments totaling more than $15.8
million, with a notification window, a future payment start date, and a
30-day acceptance instruction. At this checkpoint it is a first planned
distribution: no prior-cashing gate, recipient delivery, or acceptance result
is yet public. The comparison shows that recipient selection and payment timing
are separate administrative mechanisms, and neither should be collapsed into
one refund-success rate.

The October 15 Walmart wire-transfer distribution supplies the next stage:
the FTC reports checks being sent to 65,022 affected consumers totaling more
than $9.8 million and gives a 90-day cashing window. This is reported dispatch,
not verified cashing. Read together, the routes form a staged ladder—planned
distribution (Cleo), dispatched checks (Walmart), and prior-cashing-conditioned
repeat distribution (ACRO, Next-Gen, American Vehicle Protection, and
Yellowstone)—without a common recipient denominator or adequacy measure.

## What this adds

Repeat distribution is not random outreach. It is a form of administrative
selection: people who were reached and completed the first payment route are
more likely to receive a later payment. Seeing the same condition in three
different FTC refund programs strengthens the mechanism-level finding, but it
does not make the programs one denominator. The business case also shows the
same gate outside household consumer refunds. Repeat-payment totals still
cannot be read as a recovery rate for everyone harmed—including people who
never received or could not cash the first payment.

```text
consumer harm
  -> first refund route
  -> receipt/cashing gate
  -> second refund
  -> [open] debt/account correction, residual burden, security, trust, exit
```

## Limits

- The pages report checks sent and prior acceptance/cashing, not second-check
  cashing or individual adequacy.
- The cases have different harms, dates, administrators, and denominators;
  their amounts must not be pooled into one refund rate.
- None of the routes reports debt or account correction, remaining loss, household
  adaptation, trust, switching, or exit.

The next decisive record remains a claimant-level payment and account ledger
that includes failed delivery, reissue effort, final receipt, correction, and
later financial behavior.
