# Finding 001: Elite social networks can open a route to political office

**Status:** bounded comparative political-access finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study examines historical families connected to Buenos Aires's Jockey
Club. Using staggered family entry and a difference-in-differences design, it
reports that membership increased the probability of holding legislative office
by about four percentage points—more than twice the control-group mean.

Membership also increased families' centrality in elite marriage networks. The
office-holding advantage declined substantially after Argentina's 1912 Sáenz
Peña democratizing reforms.

The route is:

```text
elite social membership
  -> network centrality and access to nominations or mobilization
  -> greater chance of holding legislative office
  -> [open] legislative behavior, public legitimacy, and representation
```

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This gives the atlas a concrete comparative version of a broader question:

> Political systems can be formally open while access to office still runs
> through socially concentrated networks.

It also supplies an important condition: when electoral institutions change,
the value of those networks can change too. Social connection is therefore not
automatically political power; its payoff depends on the surrounding rules.

## Limits and next test

- The study concerns historical Argentina, not present-day United States.
- The public abstract does not disclose the exact sample or all controls for
  family wealth, status, kinship, and prior political ambition.
- The finding concerns access to office, not policy quality, representation,
  public trust, or democratic inclusion.
- It does not show that ordinary social clubs have the same effect.

The next decisive U.S. test is a linked civic-participation record joining
membership or network access, nomination, campaign resources, election, office,
constituent representation, and later public judgment. Until then, the safe
claim is that concentrated social networks can be a route into political office
under some institutional conditions.

## Source

- [NBER Working Paper 35611: Social Clubs and Political Office](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35611)
