# Firm platform adoption, worker control, customer conversion, and exit bridge

**Status:** thirty-second active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** firm capacity, digital discovery, generative-AI task adoption, worker control, platform data, customer choice, and replaceability

## Question

When firms adopt platform tools or generative AI, who gains practical capacity,
who bears the work and data burden, and does adoption produce durable customer
choice or deeper dependence on a platform gatekeeper?

```text
firm resources / infrastructure
  -> platform or AI adoption
  -> discovery, task change, data, and worker discretion
  -> sales, productivity, prices, or labor response
  -> customer choice, supplier power, switching, or exit
```

## Evidence ledger

| Stage or link | Comparable observation | Method and unit | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firm capacity is size-stratified | In the 2024–25 World Bank Enterprise Survey of 2,589 U.S. establishments, formal training was reported by 29.1% of small, 59.1% of medium, and 78.9% of large establishments; employment growth was 4.2%, 6.4%, and 12.6%. | Establishment survey by firm size. | Size differences do not establish that size caused training, adoption, productivity, worker control, or survival. |
| Digital discovery is also size-stratified | Eurostat reports social-media use by 60.6% of small EU enterprises, 76.2% of medium enterprises, and 89.1% of large enterprises in 2025, a 28.5-point large-small gap; country shares ranged from 88.2% in Malta to 41.5% in Bulgaria. | Harmonized enterprise survey, covered firms with at least 10 employees or self-employed persons. | Presence is not reach, sales conversion, customer welfare, market power, or causal productivity. |
| Small firms face uneven commercial capacity | In the 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, the share of firms expecting higher revenue in 2026 fell 6 points year over year; smaller firms reported lower pricing power than larger firms, while small-firm employment growth remained positive. | Weighted non-random employer-firm survey; 6,389 firms reported revenue expectations and 6,361 reported employment change. | Expectations and diffusion measures do not establish survival, prices paid by customers, or local worker outcomes. |

## Cross-source finding

The central pattern is a **capacity-to-replaceability gap**. Larger firms are
more likely to possess training and platform presence, but the adoption signal
does not tell us whether workers gain discretion, customers receive better
choice, or firms become less dependent on the platform that supplies discovery,
data, and transaction infrastructure.

The chain needs five distinct gates:

1. **Capacity:** money, skills, infrastructure, training, and financing.
2. **Adoption:** actual platform or AI use at a defined task or firm.
3. **Control:** worker discretion, monitoring, correction, bargaining, and
   ability to refuse or modify the tool.
4. **Conversion:** sales, prices, quality, productivity, wages, or customer
   access.
5. **Replaceability:** data portability, alternative channels, switching cost,
   supplier diversity, and practical exit.

The strongest counter-signal is that adoption can appear high at the aggregate
task level while remaining shallow within similar occupations. Conversely, a
firm may remain commercially viable with low platform use by relying on local
relationships or alternative channels. Neither case proves that platform use
improves or reduces autonomy.

## Bounded public discussion

A LinkedIn business account describes a large software-renewal increase and a
costly successful switch, making switching cost concrete but not representative.
A Reddit policy discussion debates whether consolidation raises prices and harms
workers or creates efficiency and investment, preserving the pro-competition
counterargument. A LinkedIn relay about platform non-use describes exit as
nonlinear and reversible because social and economic resources can pull users
back into use.

These discussions identify pricing power, dependence, and exit mechanisms. They
are selected experiences or arguments, not firm-level market evidence.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- Large firms may train more because they have more complex work, not because
  platform adoption itself creates capacity.
- Social-media presence can be a low-value brand signal while a smaller firm's
  local relationships generate more durable demand.
- AI exposure may reflect task design, worker choice, or reporting differences
  rather than management pressure or productivity opportunity.
- Strong neutrality can improve competition but reduce data investment; a
  platform can also innovate through privacy-preserving or shared infrastructure.
- A high switching cost can be overcome by a sufficiently large price shock, but
  the ability to exit may depend on firm size and alternative suppliers.
- Workers can use AI without losing control, while a formally “human-in-the-
  loop” system can still narrow discretion through monitoring or quotas.

## Stronger next test

Build an identifier-bearing firm and workplace episode that records:

1. firm size, place, financing, infrastructure, training, and platform terms;
2. named tool adoption at task level, worker exposure, discretion, monitoring,
   correction, representation, and refusal rights;
3. customer discovery, conversion, prices, quality, repeat use, and complaints;
4. platform fees, data access, portability, alternative channels, migration
   effort, and supplier switching; and
5. later employment, pay/hours, firm survival, customer welfare, worker voice,
   trust, and exit.

Compare a small firm that gains durable customers, a firm that becomes more
dependent despite adoption, and a firm that exits or diversifies successfully.
Use both worker-reported and system-logged task measures without treating them
as interchangeable.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The corpus shows that firm capacity, platform presence, AI
task adoption, worker control, and customer replaceability are separate gates.
It does not yet follow one firm and its workers from adoption through measured
conversion, distribution of gains, switching cost, and durable exit or survival.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call platform or AI adoption productive, empowering, competitive, or
harmful until a named firm or workplace has task-level exposure, worker control,
customer or revenue outcomes, platform terms, and a real alternative or exit
episode with comparable denominators.
