# Migration status, local capacity, belonging, and civic conversion bridge

**Status:** sixteenth active depth artifact in the ongoing program  
**Checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Scope:** U.S. and comparative migration evidence, with bounded public discussion

## Question

When migration status and settlement conditions meet local institutions, what
turns arrival into practical belonging and civic participation—and what turns
ordinary public contact into avoidance, exclusion, or conflict?

This extends the project's existing demand, capacity, housing, and belonging
layers. It does not replace them with an "integration score." The object of
study is a sequence of conversions that can succeed at one stage and fail at
the next.

## Stage chain

```text
legal status / visa stream / enforcement signal
  -> work, income, rent, language, housing and service capacity
  -> perceived safety, recognition, relationships and local agency
  -> use or non-use of health, school, police, benefits and community life
  -> civic participation, political attribution or private authority
  -> institutional response, durable inclusion or exit
```

## Cross-source finding

The strongest recurring pattern is a **conversion gap**. Settlement can show
employment, income, service contact, and local policy support without producing
felt influence or safe public participation. Conversely, a formal service can
exist while household risk perception makes it practically unavailable.

The stages should therefore be reported separately:

1. **Capacity:** resources and institutional contact are available.
2. **Safety:** people believe ordinary contact will not expose or punish them.
3. **Recognition:** people experience relationships, equal standing, and local
   agency.
4. **Use:** they actually seek care, work, school, police, benefits, and
   community life.
5. **Conversion:** use becomes participation, voice, or durable trust.

The evidence supports the first four as distinct measurable stages. It does
not yet show a general causal path from one to the next. The most consequential
failure may be between safety and use: a system can be formally present but
functionally absent for a household that anticipates status, data-sharing, or
enforcement risk.

## Bounded public discussion

The LinkedIn relay of the Urban Institute work frames fear as a mechanism that
can turn public systems into practical non-use. A Marshall Project LinkedIn
discussion adds selected cases in which contacting police exposed victims or
witnesses to immigration enforcement, making reporting avoidance a public-safety
question rather than simple distrust. An Australian settlement-network post
frames relationships, social capital, inclusive systems, and local work
experience as settlement infrastructure beyond income. A Reddit discussion
adds a generational layer: language loss and ancestry can change cultural
belonging even when formal residence and attendance measures remain unchanged.

These discussions are useful for mechanism discovery and counterexample
search. They are not prevalence estimates, representative samples, or causal
evidence.

## Counterexamples and alternative explanations

- High GP attendance does not establish language access, trust, or broader
  belonging; contact may be mandatory, urgent, or mediated by others.
- A legal-status policy can receive majority support while mixed-status
  families still avoid work, health care, benefits, or police.
- Higher income may improve material room without creating local recognition or
  influence; age and income associations in the Belonging Barometer do not
  identify the mechanism.
- Local conflict attributed to migration may instead be driven primarily by
  housing scarcity, labor-market weakness, spatial segregation, crime, or weak
  public administration. South Africa is a warning against single-cause
  attribution.
- Social discussion can reveal an omitted mechanism while overrepresenting
  people motivated to post. Treat it as a hypothesis generator.

## Stronger next test

Build a matched-place panel linking, for the same places and periods:

1. visa/status composition and enforcement exposure;
2. rent burden, housing supply, language access, wages, and service capacity;
3. repeated measures of safety, belonging, and perceived local influence;
4. administrative or survey indicators of service use, non-use, reporting,
   school attendance, and benefit take-up; and
5. observed civic action and institutional response.

The key comparison is not simply immigrant versus non-immigrant. It is whether
places with similar demand and material pressure differ in the conversion from
formal capacity to safe use and civic voice. Pre-register alternative paths:
status concern, housing pressure, language access, prior institutional trust,
and local political messaging.

## Current answer state

**Partly answered.** The repo now has evidence for separate capacity, safety,
belonging, non-use, policy meaning, and conflict stages. It does not yet have
a longitudinal or matched-place test showing when those stages convert into
durable civic participation and institutional trust.

## Stopping rule for this rotation

Do not call this chain resolved until one matched-place or longitudinal source
can follow at least three transitions—material or legal condition, perceived
safety/belonging, and observed use or civic action—with a credible comparison
and a documented counterexample. Until then, rotate to the next open chain
while preserving this as an active research question.
