# Pew 2026 Latino American-dream, mobility, and cultural meaning layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** Pew Research Center National Survey of Latinos
**Field dates:** October 6–16, 2025; published September 1, 2026
**Status:** cultural meaning and intergenerational-mobility layer; not a causal mobility estimate

## Why this update matters

The atlas needs to distinguish material position from the meaning people attach
to progress, belonging, and the possibility of a future. Pew's bilingual
National Survey of Latinos provides that distinction in one compact design:
respondents report whether they have achieved the American dream, whether it is
out of reach or nonexistent, how their living standard compares with their
parents', and what they expect for their children.

The result is a useful tension rather than a single optimism or pessimism
score. Latinos are divided on whether the dream is attainable, while majorities
say their own living standard is better than their parents' and their
children's will be better than theirs. Belief in progress and belief in the
availability of the dream are related but not interchangeable.

## Recorded findings

| Surface | Result | Careful interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Personal position | 20% say they have achieved the dream; 33% are on the way; 18% say it is out of reach; 27% say it does not exist | Attainment, pursuit, exclusion, and rejection are four distinct cultural positions |
| Own intergenerational change | 57% say their standard of living is better than their parents'; 23% say worse | Retrospective mobility is more positive than the overall dream-attainability distribution |
| Future generation | 53% expect their children's standard of living to be better; 14% expect worse | Forward-looking hope coexists with present uncertainty |
| Meaning | About 60% connect the dream to financial security, economic opportunity, or homeownership; about 40% describe a good life for self, family, or community | The dream is both material and relational; the categories are open-ended and can overlap |
| What achievement requires | Among Latinos who say the dream exists, 81% call hard work/motivation a major factor and 76% name access to the right opportunities; 35% name luck/chance | Effort and opportunity are jointly salient, while luck is less commonly named |

## Nativity and generation are not one mobility story

Immigrant Latinos report stronger intergenerational gains than US-born
Latinos: 70% say their living standard is better than their parents' versus
49% of US-born Latinos, and 63% expect their children to be better off versus
48% of US-born Latinos. At the same time, 35% of immigrant Latinos say the
American dream is harder to achieve than for their parents, compared with 48%
of US-born Latinos. This is a descriptive subgroup contrast, not evidence that
immigration causes optimism or that nativity determines political identity.

The finding matters because migration, labor, housing, family support,
consumer aspiration, and belonging can all be present in the same narrative
without being the same outcome. It also creates a reversal to test: people can
report upward family mobility while judging the social opportunity structure
as harder or unavailable.

## What this adds to the broad goal

This layer separates five currencies that are often collapsed into “the
American dream”:

1. **Material position:** reported standard of living relative to parents.
2. **Future expectation:** expected standard of living for children.
3. **Cultural attainability:** achieved, pursuing, out of reach, or nonexistent.
4. **Meaning:** financial security, opportunity, homeownership, quality of
   life, family/community, freedom, equality, and safety.
5. **Mobility narrative:** immigrant and US-born respondents' different
   retrospective and prospective assessments.

The source therefore supports a cultural trend record and a subgroup reversal,
not a causal story about income mobility, consumer spending, voting, or
immigration policy. The open-ended categories also show why a single “dream
confidence” index would erase important meaning differences.

## Open arrows and next test

The next stronger design would align this meaning measure with local housing or
labor-market exposure, documented migration or status episodes, household
financial room, language/access conditions, and a later political or consumer
choice. A valid follow-up would need to preserve the respondent unit, survey
mode, immigrant-generation definitions, item nonresponse, and the difference
between optimism, achieved mobility, policy attribution, and action.

The current report does not measure vote choice, political participation,
consumer switching, actual income change, wealth, home purchase, legal status,
or causal effects of migration. It also cannot tell us whether respondents'
definitions of the dream produce behavior or institutional demands.

## Boundaries and storage

The sample contains 4,923 Latino respondents within an 8,046-adult survey,
conducted in English and Spanish and weighted by Pew. The published page
reports subgroup percentages and an open-ended thematic coding; individual
responses, full coding data, and microdata were not downloaded. The 2016
comparison is not treated as a trend estimate because Pew notes the change from
telephone to online survey mode.
