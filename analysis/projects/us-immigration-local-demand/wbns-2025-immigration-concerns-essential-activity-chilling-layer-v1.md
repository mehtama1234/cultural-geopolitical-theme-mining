# WBNS 2025 immigration concerns, essential activity, and safety-net chilling layer v1

**Status:** published cross-sectional context layer; no causal policy effect or
political-behavior estimate  
**Checked:** 2026-09-16  
**Unit:** adults in immigrant families with children under 19; 1,036 WBNS
respondents  
**Machine record:** [chilling-effect record](data/wbns-2025-immigration-concerns-essential-activity-chilling-layer-v1.json)

## Why this matters for the broad goal

This layer makes a cultural and institutional mechanism visible that ordinary
participation statistics can miss: concern about immigration status can alter
whether families use health, education, work, transportation, religious, and
public-benefit systems. The action is not simply “support” or “opposition.” It
can be protective non-use, avoidance, delayed participation, or reduced public
visibility under perceived surveillance risk.

The published sample is not a census of immigrant families, and the estimates
are not administrative records of immigration status or benefit eligibility.
Mixed-status categories include undocumented people, lawfully present people,
and/or US citizens as defined by the brief; do not equate a reported concern
with an adjudicated legal risk.

## Mechanism and counterexamples

```text
immigration-policy/enforcement concern
  -> perceived surveillance or status risk
  -> avoid work, school, care, transport, community, or benefit route
  -> material and child well-being exposure
  -> belonging, trust, legitimacy, complaint, or collective action [open]
```

The evidence supports a reported avoidance and worry surface, not a causal
claim that a particular enforcement action produced a particular missed visit
or benefit decision. It also preserves a meaningful counterexample: emotional
distress and activity avoidance differ by family status composition, with
mixed-status families reporting more child distress than all-citizen families,
while all-citizen families are not free of concern.

The material-hardship result prevents a purely cultural reading. Sixty percent
of adults in immigrant families with children reported one or more material
hardships, so avoidance can interact with an already constrained option set.
But the published brief does not identify which hardship preceded avoidance,
whether a substitute route was available, or whether avoiding a program
protected a family from perceived risk or reduced material security.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

The layer strengthens the migration/belonging, public-system access, unequal
exposure, child/care, health, work, and political-meaning lanes. It shows that
institutional trust and participation can be expressed through non-use and
protective withdrawal, not only through complaint or turnout. It does not
measure political party, vote, protest, collective organizing, later trust,
remedy, or durable exit, and should not be converted into an immigrant-family
political-behavior rate.

The 2025 WBNS age expansion is a separate vintage issue for all-age analyses,
but this subgroup remains defined by immigrant families with children and must
still be interpreted with the 2025 questionnaire, sample, weights, and
retrospective window.

## Smallest next test

Use authenticated respondent-level data or a compatible repeated design to
condition the reported avoidance on family status composition, language,
income, disability, geography, prior benefit use, and material hardship. The
stronger end-to-end test is a dated policy/enforcement or service encounter
with perceived risk, alternative route, actual non-use or participation,
child/work/health consequence, institutional response, and later trust,
collective action, or recovery.
