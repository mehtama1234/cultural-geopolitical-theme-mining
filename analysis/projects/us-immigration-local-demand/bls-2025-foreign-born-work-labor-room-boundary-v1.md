# BLS 2025 foreign-born work, earnings, and labor-room boundary v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Foreign-Born Workers: Labor Force Characteristics — 2025
**Reference period:** 2024–2025 annual averages; release May 19, 2026
**Status:** reported migration/work distribution layer; not a causal migration or household-welfare estimate

## Why this update matters

The migration lane should not stop at population presence or cultural identity.
Foreign-born workers are part of the US labor, service, care, construction,
production, and household systems, but participation, occupational placement,
earnings, gender, region, and family structure move differently. This BLS
release supplies a compact labor-market bridge while preserving the difference
between workforce composition and migration effects.

## Reported 2025 facts

| Surface | Foreign-born result | Native-born comparison or boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| Labor-force share | Foreign-born workers were 19.1% of the US civilian labor force | This is composition, not an estimate of jobs created or displaced by immigration |
| Unemployment | 4.2%, unchanged from 2024 | Native-born unemployment rose to 4.3% from 4.0% in 2024; the near-parity is descriptive |
| Participation | 66.3% | Native-born participation was 61.6%; men and women show different patterns |
| Median weekly earnings | $1,059 for foreign-born full-time wage and salary workers | $1,236 for native-born workers; BLS identifies education, occupation, industry, and region as explanations that are not fully adjusted here |
| Occupational distribution | Foreign-born workers were more concentrated in service, natural resources/construction/maintenance, and production/transportation/material-moving occupations | Native-born workers were more concentrated in management/professional and sales/office occupations |
| Family/care pattern | Foreign-born mothers with children under 18 had a 64.8% participation rate; mothers of children under age 3 were at 52.4% | Native-born counterparts were 76.9% and 68.5%, respectively; this is a work/care difference, not a childcare-causality estimate |
| Regional distribution | Foreign-born workers were 24.5% of the labor force in the West and 22.7% in the Northeast | Shares were 18.7% in the South and 10.8% in the Midwest; place and labor-market structure remain confounded |

## What this adds to the broad goal

This layer separates four commonly conflated routes:

1. **Population and labor supply:** who is present and working.
2. **Occupational allocation:** where workers are positioned in the production
   and care economy.
3. **Household room:** weekly earnings, family composition, and paid-work
   participation.
4. **Place and public meaning:** regional concentration, service demand,
   belonging, and political interpretation.

The release supports a reported distributional pattern, not a claim that
immigration caused lower earnings, that foreign-born workers displace native
workers, or that labor-force participation equals household security.

## Open arrows and next test

The next stronger design would join local labor-market exposure to housing,
transport, childcare, public-service capacity, employer demand, wages, and
resident/customer experiences. It should compare immigrant generation, legal
status where lawfully available, occupation, gender, family care, and region
without turning nativity into a causal treatment. The cultural meaning and
political-action endpoints in the existing migration layers remain separate.
