# Migration, belonging, and political meaning layer v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Status:** measurement specification; direct cultural/political evidence remains to be acquired

## The question

When a place changes demographically and economically, how do residents decide
what the change means? The same population change may be experienced as new
customers and jobs, housing competition, public-system strain, revitalization,
loss of control, or a more plural local culture. Those meanings cannot be read
from population, rent, language, or firm data.

```text
measured local change and personal encounter
  -> perceived benefit, cost, fairness, dignity, or control
  -> attribution and information environment
  -> belonging, trust, identity, and threat/opportunity interpretation
  -> consumer, civic, electoral, protest, or policy action
  -> institutional and political response
```

## Outcomes that must remain separate

| Outcome | Direct question | Do not substitute |
|---|---|---|
| Belonging | Does the respondent feel accepted, represented, and able to remain in the place? | Foreign-born share, language, or tenure |
| Perceived fairness | Are costs, jobs, services, and rules distributed fairly? | Rent, wages, or establishment counts |
| Attribution | Who is held responsible: landlords, firms, local government, national government, migrants, employers, or broader forces? | Researcher's preferred explanation |
| Trust/legitimacy | Are institutions competent, fair, responsive, and representative? | Administrative spending or HPSA status |
| Identity/status | Does the change affect dignity, recognition, threat, or social standing? | Race, nativity, or party label |
| Action | Did the person vote, contact, volunteer, organize, complain, move, withdraw, or support a policy? | Attitude alone |

## Competing interpretations to test

Do not begin with one migration narrative. Test whether measured local change is
associated with each of these, conditional on exposure and alternatives:

1. **Capability:** more workers, customers, firms, skills, and cultural
   exchange increase perceived opportunity and local vitality.
2. **Capacity lag:** demand rises faster than housing, schools, clinics,
   transport, or administration, increasing perceived unfairness or insecurity.
3. **Distribution:** aggregate growth looks positive while renters, low-income
   workers, language-minority residents, or existing businesses absorb costs.
4. **Control and ownership:** residents distinguish local growth from who owns
   land, firms, data, services, and political decisions.
5. **Information and attribution:** media, party identity, direct contact,
   institutional trust, and local narratives shape whether the same conditions
   are blamed on government, firms, migrants, outsiders, or nobody.

These are competing mechanisms, not findings. Several may operate in one place
or population at once.

## Minimum cultural-political record

For a respondent or community observation, record:

- direct contact with newcomers and local institutions;
- perceived change in jobs, prices, housing, services, safety, and culture;
- personal exposure versus secondhand information;
- perceived fairness, control, dignity, and future ability to stay;
- blamed actor and confidence in that attribution;
- trust in local and national institutions and relevant information sources;
- belonging, recognition, threat, opportunity, and social distance;
- vote, turnout, policy preference, contact, protest, volunteering, complaint,
  organizing, consumer exit, or withdrawal;
- income, tenure, race/ethnicity, nativity, language, age, work, family,
  disability, party identity, and place alternatives where available.

## Design

Attach survey or civic records to the matched-place panel only at the level the
source supports. Use the place panel to define exposure context—population
change, housing, capacity, firms, and language access—and use the respondent
source to measure interpretation and action. A county average can contextualize
a respondent; it cannot replace the respondent's reported meaning or motive.

Preferred contrasts are:

- similar population change, different housing/service capacity;
- similar rent pressure, different job and firm dynamics;
- similar local exposure, different information environments;
- similar perceived pressure, different ability to stay, move, or exit;
- similar attitudes, different measured material conditions.

Seek counterexamples: places with fast growth and rising belonging; places with
little demographic change but strong anti-outsider narratives; and places with
capacity strain where residents attribute responsibility to institutions rather
than newcomers.

## Evidence boundary

Until direct belonging, attribution, and action measures are aligned with local
exposure, the safe conclusion is only that material and demographic change can
create conditions for competing interpretations. Population change does not
prove exclusion, revitalization, polarization, or political responsibility.
