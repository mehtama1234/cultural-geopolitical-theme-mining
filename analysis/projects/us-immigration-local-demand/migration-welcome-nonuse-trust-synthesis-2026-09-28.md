# Welcome, non-use, and trust can coexist in the same migration system

**Checked:** 2026-09-28  
**Status:** bounded cross-source cultural and political synthesis; no causal
or respondent-level join

## The finding

Migration-related legitimacy is not one public attitude. A place can report
broad cultural welcome and support for legal inclusion while some families
avoid ordinary work, health, safety-net, or community routes because the
perceived risk of public contact is too high. County-level trust and civic
action add another boundary: neither follows a simple growth or foreign-born
share gradient.

The defensible route is:

```text
place and legal-status context
  -> perceived welcome, exposure, or institutional risk
  -> route use, protective non-use, or substitution
  -> material and belonging consequences
  -> trust, attribution, civic action, or exit [open]
```

## Three distinct evidence surfaces

### 1. Local welcome and conditional inclusion

The 2026 Chicagoland survey reports that roughly six in ten residents view
immigrants as good for culture, the national and local economy, and their own
communities. About eight in ten support maintaining or increasing legal
immigration, and support for a citizenship path ranges from 52% in suburban
Cook County to 66% in Chicago. Support for permanent status for Dreamers is
72%–79% across the three surveyed areas.

This is direct local meaning and policy preference, not observed service use,
household security, or political action.

### 2. Household-level risk and non-use

The Urban Institute's 2025 WBNS analysis finds that 18% of adults in immigrant
families reported that their family did not engage in an activity essential to
well-being because of immigration concerns. Reported non-use included work,
health-care settings, and religious or community events. Mixed-status families
reported more worry about visiting a doctor or hospital, going to work, or
talking to police than immigrant families made up entirely of U.S. citizens:
29% versus 10% for health care, 38% versus 8% for work, and 38% versus 10% for
police contact.

The same analysis reports safety-net avoidance because of green-card or
information-sharing concerns. This is a perceived-risk and self-reported
non-use surface; it does not identify the specific encounter, actual legal
exposure, substitute route, forgone amount, remedy, or later persistence.

### 3. Place context, trust, and action

The 2024 CES contextual screen crossed county population growth with ACS
foreign-born share across 620 counties. Federal and state trust were
non-monotonic across the 16 cells, while the displayed civic-action range was
comparatively narrow. The screen therefore weakens both a simple “migration
causes distrust” claim and a simple “stable civic action means acceptance”
claim.

These are county-keyed contextual estimates. They do not identify a
respondent's migration history, direct service encounter, legal status,
language access, or belief about what caused local change.

## Cultural-political interpretation

The important distinction is between **symbolic inclusion** and **operational
legibility**. A resident may support immigrants' contribution or a legal path
to citizenship while a mixed-status household still treats a clinic, employer,
police department, school, or benefit office as risky. Public legitimacy is
therefore route-specific: welcome in the abstract does not guarantee usable
access in practice, and non-use does not prove generalized hostility or
political withdrawal.

This also explains why place-level trust and civic action should remain separate
endpoints. People can continue participating while distrusting a particular
institution, or avoid one public route while using community, private, or
informal alternatives.

## What remains unproven

- whether a named enforcement or information event caused a specific family's
  non-use;
- whether non-use protected the family or sacrificed care, income, schooling,
  safety, benefits, or belonging;
- whether trusted intermediaries or substitute routes restored capability;
- whether a later encounter changed trust, attribution, civic action, or exit;
- whether local welcome translated into improved service access or durable
  intergroup belonging.

The next decisive test is a local or administrative episode with a stable
household or case key that follows perceived risk through attempted use,
substitution, material consequence, institutional response, and later trust or
action. Until then, this is a comparative cultural-legitimacy synthesis, not a
causal immigration or public-withdrawal finding.
