# Finding: earnings persistence reflects both family-linked traits and firm-linked social sorting

**Status:** bounded labor-market inequality and social-interaction finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35746 links roughly 44,000 U.S. twin pairs observed as
adolescents in the 2000 Census to earnings about 20 years later. Its extended
twin design estimates heritability of log earnings at about 0.36 and separates
person-specific earnings from employer-specific pay premiums.

The employer-premium component is mainly driven by same-sex twins who work at
the same firm. When siblings who work together are excluded, estimated
earnings heritability falls by 15%.

The cultural-economic insight is:

> A stable earnings pattern can look like family inheritance while also
> reflecting social interaction, employer sorting, and access to firm-specific
> pay. “Heritable” is not the same as fixed, purely genetic, or outside the
> reach of institutions.

## Limits and next test

- Heritability is a population statistic, not an individual destiny or an
  immutable genetic share.
- The study does not identify the specific hiring, network, school, geographic,
  or discrimination mechanism behind same-firm sorting.
- Employer-specific pay is not the same as worker control, bargaining power,
  job quality, or household security.
- The historical cohort and linked measures do not automatically describe
  current labor markets.

The next decisive artifact is a worker-firm ledger joining family links,
schooling, occupation, employer entry, referrals or social proximity,
employer premium, worker voice, mobility, and household outcomes.
