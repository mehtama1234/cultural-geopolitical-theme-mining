# Finding: jail healthcare standards can improve through coordination and oversight

**Status:** bounded experimental public-system and health finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 33357 randomized the offer of National Commission on
Correctional Health Care accreditation to 46 midsized U.S. jails, with
immediate versus delayed offer. The study reports improved compliance with
healthcare standards, especially training and patient care, and a substantial
reduction in 12-month mortality.

The improvements occurred without increases in capital or labor inputs. The
reported mechanisms are better coordination, documentation, screening, and
oversight. The institutional setting matters: publicly financed care is often
delivered by private contractors and overseen by elected sheriffs with limited
medical expertise, while people in custody cannot freely switch providers.

The atlas-relevant insight is:

> Public capacity is not only a question of money or staffing. In a constrained
> institution, standards, coordination, and information can change care and
> survival—but the result remains bounded by the institution's population,
> providers, and ability to carry improvements beyond its walls.

## Limits and next test

- The abstract does not report every effect size, confidence interval,
  implementation cost, or post-release outcome.
- The experiment covers 46 midsized jails and does not establish effects in
  prisons, small jails, immigration detention, or other care systems.
- Improved compliance and mortality do not establish equal care, lower
  detention exposure, post-release health, trust, or contractor-wide effects.
- The next test is a named-facility ledger joining accreditation cost,
  staffing, vendor contracts, standards, screening, mortality causes,
  post-release continuity, appeals, and community outcomes.
