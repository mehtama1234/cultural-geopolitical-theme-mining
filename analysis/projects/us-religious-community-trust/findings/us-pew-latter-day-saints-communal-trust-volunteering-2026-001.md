# Finding 001: A strong community institution can show high trust while access remains unequal

**Status:** bounded cross-sectional religious-community finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

Pew's analysis of the 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study includes 565 current
Latter-day Saint respondents within a survey of 36,908 U.S. adults. Seventy-two
percent of Latter-day Saint adults said they had volunteered in the previous
year, compared with 41% of all U.S. adults.

The group was not uniform. Reported volunteering was 91% among members with a
bachelor's degree or more and 61% among those with less education. It was 80% in
the Mountain region and 62% elsewhere in the country.

Pew also reports that 48% of Latter-day Saints said most people can be trusted,
compared with 34% of all U.S. adults. Within the group, trust was about 60%
among members with a bachelor's degree and about 40% among those without a
college diploma.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a community-institution version of a recurring pattern:

> A real social institution can produce connection and practical help while
> access to that resource remains uneven inside the group.

Religious congregations can provide roles, repeated contact, service, and a
language of mutual obligation. But the data do not tell us whether the reported
volunteering was congregational or outside the church, how many hours it took,
who received the help, who carried the burden, or whether members felt equally
included in the institution.

The education and regional differences matter because they prevent a simple
“community solves isolation” story. A strong average can coexist with unequal
time, status, geography, and opportunity to participate.

## Limits and next test

- The analysis is cross-sectional and does not show that membership caused
  volunteering or trust.
- Affiliation is not the same as attendance, assigned service, leadership,
  friendship, or receipt of help.
- Generalized trust is not institutional trust, political trust, safety, or
  observed cooperation.
- Volunteering is self-reported and does not establish hours, recipient,
  adequacy, burden, or later political action.
- The current Latter-day Saint subgroup is not a national estimate of all
  religious communities or of the effects of religion.

The next useful test is a repeated or place-linked community record joining
membership or participation, role assignment, time burden, received support,
belonging, exclusion, trust, and later civic or political action. Until then,
the safe claim is that this community reports high connection-related outcomes
with meaningful internal differences.
