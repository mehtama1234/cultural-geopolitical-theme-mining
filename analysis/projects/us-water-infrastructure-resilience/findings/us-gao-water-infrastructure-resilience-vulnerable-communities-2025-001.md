# Finding 001: Water-infrastructure money can be large while dependable protection remains hard to finance and measure

**Status:** bounded water-infrastructure and place-inequality finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

GAO reviewed 14 EPA, FEMA, and USDA programs that provided water-infrastructure
assistance from fiscal years 2014 through 2023. Together, the programs provided
about $35 billion in grants supporting at least 22,000 projects and about $29
billion in direct loans supporting roughly 4,800 projects. State revolving funds
also supported at least 17,121 projects.

Yet selected utilities described a difficult local middle. Five of the 14
utilities interviewed cited financing roadblocks such as local officials
rejecting rate increases; two said officials might reject projects because the
results were not visible enough to the public. Three of five technical-assistance
providers described routine operations and maintenance costs as a challenge,
and eight utilities said loan repayment could be costly for some communities.
Most federal assistance reviewed could not be used for routine operations and
maintenance.

The route is:

```text
disaster exposure and water need
  -> federal grant or loan
  -> local rate, repayment, and project decision
  -> construction and maintenance
  -> [open] operating resilience and service continuity
  -> [open] household affordability, safety, health, and trust
```

GAO also found that agencies had difficulty identifying exactly which
communities benefited because utility service areas do not always match
municipal boundaries. In a selected Clean Water State Revolving Fund analysis,
more socially vulnerable and high-poverty communities received less assistance
on average, while communities with larger non-white or Hispanic/Latino
populations received more on average. Those are statistical associations, not
proof of discrimination or a causal explanation.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a water-infrastructure version of a recurring cultural and political
pattern:

> A society can approve large public investment while the hardest questions—who
> can afford the local share, who sees the benefit, who maintains the system,
> and who stays protected during a disaster—remain local and politically
> difficult.

The pattern is not that federal assistance is ineffective. Money can finance
real upgrades, and rate resistance can protect households from unaffordable
charges. The bounded finding is that investment totals cannot answer whether
the system remains dependable, affordable, or visible to the people who rely on
it.

## Limits and next test

- The 14 utility interviews were selected for varied perspectives and are not
  generalizable to all utilities.
- Assistance obligations and project counts do not prove completed construction
  or operating resilience.
- The report does not link a financed project to household bills, water
  quality, service interruption, health, or later disaster recovery.
- The statistical results are associations within selected programs and use
  program-specific definitions of vulnerability.
- Service-area mapping improves targeting but does not itself prove that a
  household received safe or continuous water.

The next decisive artifact is a utility- and project-identified ledger joining
award, local match or rate decision, construction, maintenance funding,
operating performance during a disruption, service-area households, bill or
shutoff effects, and later recovery. Until then, the safe claim is that water
resilience has a visible federal-finance stage but an unresolved local
affordability, maintenance, and lived-service middle.
