# Finding: risk orientation can redirect both collective participation and technology adoption

**Status:** bounded historical U.S. behavioral and institutional finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-23

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35634 estimates historical U.S. risk preferences from crop
choice, treating crop portfolios as a form of portfolio allocation and using
predicted crop returns. It produces county-level estimates and farmer-level
estimates for Kansas during 1889–1929.

More risk-averse farmers leveraged less, were less likely to buy WWI Liberty
Bonds, and were more likely to participate in local risk-sharing institutions.
Higher risk aversion also predicted slower tractor adoption and farm
mechanization during the 1920s.

The cultural-economic insight is:

> Risk orientation does not point in only one direction. The same caution that
> limits leverage and slows technology adoption may increase participation in
> local risk-sharing institutions, while reducing participation in a national
> financial campaign.

## Limits and next test

- The exact county and farmer denominators are not disclosed in the abstract.
- Crop choice may reflect credit, land, weather, market access, or knowledge,
  not preference alone.
- Historical risk preference is not a fixed cultural trait or contemporary
  individual psychology.
- Liberty Bond and local-institution participation are not interchangeable
  measures of trust, political action, or legitimacy.

The next decisive artifact is a place–farmer–institution ledger joining risk
exposure, credit, wealth, crop choice, local association membership, civic
investment, technology access, and later productivity or household security.
