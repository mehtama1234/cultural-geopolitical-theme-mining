# Federal Reserve household insurance pass v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-12  
**Source:** [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-housing.htm) and its [consumer-response appendix](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-supplemental-appendixes-report-economic-well-being-us-households-2025-appendix-b.htm)  
**Evidence type:** weighted household survey; reported conditions, not a causal study.

## The household-level numbers

| Measure | 2025 result | Why it matters | Limit |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Homeowners without homeowners insurance | 6% | A nontrivial share of owners had no standard protection on the primary home. | The survey does not show the full reason for each household or whether a lender required coverage. |
| Owners without coverage in low- or moderate-income neighborhoods | 12% | The gap is larger where household resources are likely to be tighter. | Neighborhood income is not the same as the owner’s income or disaster exposure. |
| Owners without coverage with family income below $50,000 | Roughly 20% | Lower-income owners were much more likely to go without coverage. | This is a cross-section, not proof that income caused the gap. |
| Main reason uninsured owners gave | 43% could not afford it; 16% said it was not worth the cost | Cost, not only refusal, is central in the reported answers. | Respondents may have more than one reason, and the survey asks about reported status. |
| Insured owners who wanted more coverage but could not afford it | 20% overall; 33% below $50,000 income | Being insured can still mean the household is accepting a larger uncovered loss. | “More coverage” does not identify which peril or amount is missing. |
| Insured owners who struggled to afford premiums | 14% overall; nearly 30% below $50,000 income | The premium can be a current budget problem even when coverage remains in place. | The survey does not link the struggle to a missed payment, dropped policy, or later loss. |
| Insured owners who said costs rose more than expected | More than 60% | Price surprise may matter separately from the current premium level. | It is a perception of recent increases, not a policy billing history. |

The Federal Reserve says the results are weighted to represent the US adult population. The appendix records the survey question, response options, and unweighted respondent counts; the housing page explains that owners with a mortgage generally must carry insurance, while owners who own free and clear have more room to go without it.

## What this adds to the market evidence

The NBER and CBO material shows how risk can enter premiums, home values, and public insurance. The Federal Reserve survey shows the household point where that system is felt:

```text
premium or coverage choice
  -> current cash pressure or accepted uncovered loss
  -> less room for repair, mortgage, food, care, or transport
  -> later protection, stay, sale, or recovery choice
```

The first arrow is reported by households. The later arrows remain to be measured. We should not say that the 6% uninsured rate was caused by climate risk, or that it caused a specific move or default.

## A useful split

The survey separates three situations that are often treated as one:

1. **No coverage:** the household faces the full loss unless another source helps.
2. **Coverage that is too small:** the household has protection but says it cannot afford the amount it wants.
3. **Coverage that is hard to carry:** the policy exists, but the premium strains the current budget.

These require different remedies. A public plan that keeps a policy active may not solve an inadequate limit. A premium reduction may not solve a deductible the household cannot produce. A repair grant may reduce risk but still be unusable if the owner cannot finance the work.

## Next join

The next property-level test should pair household insurance status with:

- mortgage or no mortgage;
- premium, deductible, limits, exclusions, renewal, and payment timing;
- disaster exposure and mitigation work;
- repair, claim, temporary housing, and unpaid family help;
- food, medicine, transport, debt, and work tradeoffs;
- sale, move, or continued residence.

Keep the Federal Reserve figures as the population reference. They do not replace the dated property and household record needed to test the full chain.

## What would change the picture

The working picture would weaken if uninsured or underinsured owners were not more exposed to budget stress; if coverage gaps did not differ after accounting for mortgage, income, place, and risk; or if households with coverage problems recovered without sacrificing repairs, debt, work, care, or safe housing.
