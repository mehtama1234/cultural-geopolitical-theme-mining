# Finding 002: The US financial-access chain is broad at the doorway and thin at the remedy

**Status:** provisional cross-source route synthesis · **Checked:** 2026-09-15

## The bounded finding

The current evidence can trace four different parts of financial life, but it
cannot yet follow one person or account through all four:

1. **Doorway:** account and device ownership are broad in the 2024 Global
   Findex US estimates.
2. **Provider environment:** the IMF Financial Access Survey reports fewer US
   commercial banks, credit unions, branches, and lower household-bank balance
   ratios from 2020 to 2024.
3. **Failure or loss:** SHED respondents report fraud exposure, unrecovered
   money, and recovery time; CFPB records show a public route from complaint to
   company-response label.
4. **Remedy and exit:** verified correction, repeat effort, switching,
   continued use by necessity, trust change, and exit are not measured in a
   common route.

The strongest synthesis is therefore not that financial inclusion is working or
failing. It is this:

> Formal access has become broad, while the practical power to recover from a
> failed payment, fraud event, blocked account, or bad service episode remains
> a separate and weakly observed capability.

## The route map

| Stage | Evidence currently available | Unit and denominator | What can be said safely |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal doorway | US adult account ownership 88.0% in 2011 and 97.0% in 2024; mobile ownership 98.0%, smartphone ownership 92.6% in 2024 | Repeated cross-sectional adult survey estimate | Entry and connectivity are broad; account ownership is not control or liquidity |
| Physical/provider setting | Commercial banks 4,344 → 3,912; branches 75,500 → 68,436; branches per 100,000 adults 29.51 → 25.74, 2020–2024 | Provider/institution and adult-normalized administrative/provider-reported series | Traditional provider infrastructure contracted; person-level reach, service quality, and competition are not identified |
| Household failure burden | 2024 SHED: 46.13% of selected P2P account-involved fraud cases reported some/all money unrecovered; 43.76% reported ten or more recovery/consequence hours | Weighted conditional shares among selected fraud/account-involved respondents | Loss and time burden differ by route; these are self-reports, not verified provider liability or remedy |
| Administrative response | CFPB published records: 2024–2025 pooled response mix shifted toward explanation and away from relief labels; student-loan timing fields changed sharply | Published complaint records, product- and field-conditioned | The public response route is visible; a response label is not a verified correction |
| Practical exit | No common measure of switching, abandonment, continued use from necessity, or later trust | Missing same-person/account episode | The final power stage remains open |

The table is deliberately not a causal funnel. The rows come from different
populations, years, sampling frames, and institutional processes. Their value is
diagnostic: each row tells us which question the next row cannot answer.

## Why account ownership is the wrong endpoint

The Findex result is important. In the United States, female account ownership
rose from 84.1% in 2011 to 97.1% in 2024, while male ownership moved from 92.0%
to 96.9%. The aggregate ownership gap nearly disappears. Mobile and smartphone
access are also high.

But the account is only an institutional doorway. It does not reveal whether
the person can:

- keep the account open without recurring fees or an inaccessible identity
  process;
- receive wages or benefits reliably;
- make a payment during an outage or device loss;
- dispute a transaction through a human or effective digital route;
- recover money after fraud;
- borrow at a workable price; or
- leave the provider without losing payment history, income access, or a
  needed financial product.

This is why the atlas records the ownership convergence as an access-stage
finding rather than a general equality or power result.

## Why provider contraction does not have one obvious meaning

The IMF FAS US series show contraction in traditional provider measures:

- commercial banks: −9.9% from 2020 to 2024;
- credit unions/credit cooperatives: −12.6%;
- commercial-bank branches excluding headquarters: −9.4%;
- branches per 100,000 adults: −12.8%;
- household commercial-bank deposits/GDP: 55.52% to 42.43%; and
- household commercial-bank loans/GDP: 19.90% to 16.80%.

None of these movements has a single welfare interpretation. A branch can
close because digital service is a workable substitute, because a market is
consolidating, or because a place has lost a human route that some customers
need. A deposit/GDP ratio can fall because of denominator growth, balance
composition, interest-rate conditions, or household withdrawal. The provider
series do not identify the customer, geography of service loss, product terms,
or remedy capacity.

The cross-country comparison reinforces this limit. India reports a small rise
in branch density despite a fall in commercial-bank count; Brazil reports more
commercial banks but lower branch density. Provider count and usable access are
not the same variable.

## The failure route reveals unequal practical power

The SHED fraud layer provides a more concrete household endpoint than account
ownership. Among the selected 2024 account-involved fraud cases, the P2P group
reported some or all money unrecovered at 46.13%, compared with 28.83% for
non-P2P cases. Ten or more recovery/consequence hours were reported by 43.76%
of P2P cases and 30.93% of non-P2P cases. Among non-credit-card fraud
reporters, unrecovered money was 53.78% in the $10,000–$24,999 income group and
22.57% in the $100,000–$149,999 group.

These are not simple route or income effects. The cells can differ in fraud
type, account balances, reporting, resources, selection, and provider process.
But they establish why “connected” is not the same as “protected”: a payment
channel can be widely available while the cost of contesting a failure is
unequally distributed.

## The administrative route is visible, but the remedy is not

The CFPB annual endpoint and event-ledger work add the institutional middle.
The public record can preserve receipt, routing, submission channel, narrative
presence, timing fields, and company-response labels. A bounded 25-record
student-loan retrieval sample can show a route from complaint receipt to
publication and response classification.

That route should not be overread. The public record does not show whether the
customer received money, regained access, corrected a credit record, avoided a
repeat contact, changed providers, or continued using the service because no
substitute existed. The 2025 pooled change toward explanation, and the sharp
student-loan timing-field movement, may reflect product mix, taxonomy,
publication, routing, or servicing changes rather than a uniform change in
consumer welfare.

The correct institutional chain is:

```text
account/device access
  -> payment, credit, or service episode
  -> failure, fraud, denial, or complaint
  -> route, effort, and provider/agency response
  -> verified correction or residual loss
  -> continued use, switching, exit, trust, or collective demand
```

The atlas currently observes pieces of every stage except the final linked
outcome. The missing final stage is not a reason to discard the earlier
evidence; it is the reason to keep the evidence from being mislabeled as
consumer power.

## Counterexamples the program must preserve

- A person may have a smartphone and account but still lack reliable data,
  accessible authentication, cash access, or time for a dispute.
- A branch closure may be harmless for one customer and consequential for
  another; national branch density cannot identify that heterogeneity.
- A complaint can receive a timely explanation and still leave the underlying
  loss unresolved.
- A person may remain with a provider because wages, benefits, credit history,
  or housing payments are locked into the route; continued use is not proof of
  satisfaction.
- A household may recover money after substantial unpaid effort; recovery is
  not the same as low burden.

## The next empirical design

The next pass should seek a lawful, de-identified, account- or case-linked
design for one essential financial service. It should preserve:

| Required field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Starting account/product and place | Defines exposure and replaceability |
| Dated failure, fraud, denial, or payment event | Provides a real trigger rather than a general attitude |
| Contact attempts and elapsed time | Measures administrative and household burden |
| Accessibility, language, device, and cash constraints | Identifies who can use the route |
| Provider/agency decision and authority | Distinguishes explanation from actual correction |
| Money restored, access restored, or residual loss | Establishes the protected and sacrificed outcome |
| Repeat contact, switching, abandonment, or necessary continued use | Measures practical exit |
| Later trust, attribution, civic action, or political judgment | Separates meaning and action from hardship |

The immediate public-data version is a matched chronology, not a causal join:
align Findex/IMF access context, OFR financial-visibility milestones, CFPB
administrative routes, SHED loss and recovery burden, and FTC reported fraud
losses. A causal claim should wait for a common respondent, account, case, or
credible quasi-experimental design.
