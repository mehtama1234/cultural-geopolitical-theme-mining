# Finding 001: Outdoor recreation is a major regional economy, but economic scale does not prove equal cultural access

**Status:** bounded outdoor-recreation, cultural-resource, and place-economy finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The Bureau of Economic Analysis estimates that outdoor recreation produced
$696.7 billion in U.S. value added in 2024, equal to 2.4% of national GDP.
Employment increased in 36 states and the District of Columbia. The outdoor
recreation share of state GDP ranged from 6.1% in Hawaii to 1.0% in the
District of Columbia, showing that recreation has very different economic
weight across places.

The account also shows that recreation is not only the visible activity. In
2024, conventional activities such as boating, hiking, and hunting made up
29.5% of national outdoor-recreation value added; other activities made up
19.0%; and supporting activities such as travel and tourism, local trips,
construction, and government spending made up 51.5%.

The practical route is:

```text
place, land, water, culture, and recreation demand
  -> firms, travel, jobs, and public or supporting infrastructure
  -> regional value and employment
  -> [open] resident access, affordability, belonging, conservation,
     local distribution, and displacement
```

The cultural insight is that recreation can be both a way people experience a
place and a market that reorganizes the place. A large outdoor economy may
create jobs and services while still being expensive, seasonal, visitor-led, or
unevenly reachable for residents. Economic importance and lived cultural access
are related but different measures.

## What this adds to the atlas

This adds a new bridge between cultural life, firms, and place. It prevents the
atlas from treating culture only as attitudes or participation surveys: land,
travel, businesses, and public spending help determine which cultural activities
are available. It also preserves the opposite caution: a large regional
industry does not prove that local people share the gains or can use the
resource.

## Limits and next test

- BEA's satellite account measures economic production, value added,
  compensation, and employment—not individual participation or access.
- State GDP share is not a measure of resident wellbeing, local ownership, or
  the distribution of earnings.
- Employment growth does not establish job quality, seasonality, wages,
  bargaining power, or durable local opportunity.
- The account does not identify visitor versus resident spending, transport and
  entrance costs, disability access, land-use conflict, displacement,
  conservation outcomes, or cultural belonging.
- Supporting activity is economically connected to recreation but is not the
  same as a person's recreation experience.

The next decisive evidence would join state or place economic accounts to
resident participation, travel and entrance costs, local wages and ownership,
public-land access, and later measures of community benefit, displacement,
belonging, or conservation.
