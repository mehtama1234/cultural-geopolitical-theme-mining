# Finding 001: Transportation technology grants can fund demonstrations before usable public service is proven

**Status:** bounded public-system and infrastructure finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

The Department of Transportation's SMART grant program received 1,073
applications in its first three years. By September 2025, DOT had announced
about $289 million for 135 projects, or about 58% of the program's original
$500 million authorization and appropriation. GAO reported that most projects
were not yet complete, and interviewed recipients described difficulty buying
the technology and equipment they needed.

The route is:

```text
community need or application
  -> federal award
  -> technology procurement
  -> demonstration project
  -> [open] completed operation and usable public service
  -> [open] safety, reliability, access, affordability, and durable adoption
```

DOT had gathered information about the projects but had not yet fully built a
lessons-learned process for analyzing, validating, documenting, and sharing
what worked. GAO recommended such a plan, and the recommendation remained
open when the report was published.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a public-technology version of a recurring societal pattern:

> A public program can create money, experimentation, and visible technical
> capacity before it creates a dependable service that people can use.

The finding is not that the grants failed. Demonstrations can produce useful
knowledge before they become routine service. The narrower point is that an
award and a pilot are not the same as a completed, accessible, reliable, or
durable public outcome.

It also adds a cultural and political layer: communities are asked to trust a
technology program while the program is still learning how to record and share
which projects actually work. That can make local enthusiasm, skepticism, and
future adoption depend on whether lessons become visible and credible, but the
GAO report does not measure those attitudes.

## Limits and next test

- The report does not measure completed deployments or ongoing operations for
  every awarded project.
- It does not identify passenger, worker, resident, or emergency-responder
  use of a particular project.
- It does not establish changes in travel time, safety, reliability, access,
  affordability, emissions, or household welfare.
- The 17 recipient interviews are selected interviews, not a representative
  survey of all recipients.
- Procurement delay does not prove that a project will fail; later completion
  may change the result.

The next decisive artifact is a project-identifier ledger joining award,
procurement, launch, operating output, users served, reliability or safety,
local cost, and later continuation or abandonment. Until that exists, the safe
claim is that SMART created a grant-to-demonstration route with an incomplete
conversion to usable public service.
