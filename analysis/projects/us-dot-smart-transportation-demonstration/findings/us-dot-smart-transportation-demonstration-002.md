# Finding 002: A transportation-technology project can become operational without proving better service

**Status:** bounded named-project and public-technology finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

Seattle received nearly $2 million from DOT's SMART program for a project to
digitize commercial-vehicle permits and curb access. Seattle's final report
says the digital curb inventory was completed and vehicle-to-curb
infrastructure was installed and operational in late 2024.

The project also took longer than planned. Procurement finished in Q4 2024
instead of Q1 2024, the infrastructure was operational in Q4 2024 instead of
Q2–Q3, the survey finished in Q1 2025, and the technology assessment extended
into Q3 2025. The report attributes delays to procurement limitations,
deployment and configuration problems, participant recruitment, and camera
reporting issues.

The route is:

```text
federal demonstration grant
  -> procurement and stakeholder outreach
  -> digital curb inventory and operational infrastructure
  -> commercial-vehicle permit system
  -> [open] faster, fairer, safer, or cheaper curb access
  -> [open] continued use and durable public service
```

This is a stronger conversion than an award or announcement: a named project
reached reported operation. But operation is still not the same as a measured
improvement for delivery workers, businesses, residents, or other curb users.

## What this adds to the broader atlas

The project shows how public technology changes the work of governing a shared
space. A curb is not only a physical place; it is also a set of permits,
time windows, data rules, enforcement decisions, and competing users.

The wider pattern is:

> Digital infrastructure can make a contested public resource more legible and
> administratively manageable before it proves that access has become fairer,
> faster, or less costly.

The project report documents implementation and learning. It does not document
who gained time, who lost access, who paid more, or whether the system changed
delivery reliability.

## Limits and counterexamples

- This is one Seattle project and a grantee implementation report, not an
  independent evaluation or national estimate.
- The report does not give a before-and-after measure of curb-search time,
  delivery reliability, safety, enforcement, price, or access by user group.
- “Installed and operational” describes infrastructure status, not regular
  user adoption or successful service.
- Engagement with some local businesses and non-permit holders was difficult;
  additional outreach was planned for Stage Two.
- A delayed project can still produce useful institutional learning, and later
  Stage Two work could produce outcomes not visible in the report.

## Next decisive test

The next useful artifact is a project-linked operating ledger with permit
applications, approval and enforcement records, curb occupancy or delivery
time, user costs, accessibility, complaints, and continuation after Stage Two.
Until then, the safe claim is that Seattle converted a federal technology grant
into reported operating infrastructure, not that it delivered better curb
access.
