# Source search: US eviction as a household cascade

**Search date:** 2026-09-11  
**Geography:** United States, with detailed evidence from Cook County and New York City  
**Status:** opening pass; causal effects are measured, wider national and prevention effects remain open

## Working question

What does an eviction order change beyond the loss of a home?

## Opening sources

| ID | Source | What it tells us | Status | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-NBER-EVICTION-CASCADE | [Eviction and Poverty in American Cities](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30382) | Using linked administrative data and judge assignment, NBER finds that an eviction order increases homelessness and hospital visits and reduces earnings, durable-goods consumption and credit access in the first two years; longer-term effects include more debt and lower credit scores. | NBER working paper | The study covers two urban areas, and tenants show financial distress and health visits before court, so the full fall is not caused by the order alone. |
| US-NBER-EVICTION-CAUSAL | [Does Eviction Cause Poverty?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26139) | Quasi-experimental evidence finds that eviction reduces credit access and durable consumption for several years. | NBER working paper | It does not measure every health, work or housing pathway and is not a national estimate for all filings. |
| US-NBER-LEGAL-AID | [The Effects of Legal Representation on Tenant Outcomes](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29836) | NBER studies New York City's universal legal-representation program for low-income tenants in housing court. | NBER working paper | The program setting and measured outcomes do not tell us how every form of rent aid or legal help works. |
| US-HBS-RENTAL-HOUSING | [Revisiting Rental Housing](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/podcast-revisiting-rental-housing) | HBS describes renters as a large group whose housing needs and policy position have often received less attention than home ownership. | HBS Working Knowledge | This is policy context, not a causal estimate of eviction effects. |

## First pattern to test

```text
income loss, rent rise, illness, or other shock
  -> arrears and housing court
  -> eviction order or forced move
  -> homelessness, work loss, hospital visit, lower spending or credit loss
  -> more debt and fewer future housing choices
```

The NBER evidence supports a causal effect of an eviction order on several later outcomes while also showing that distress often precedes court. That distinction matters: preventing the order may help, but it may not remove the original income, health or rent problem.

## Counterpoint to keep visible

Not every filing becomes an order, and legal help, payment plans or family support can prevent a move. A move can also be voluntary or improve a household's situation; the analysis must identify the event and its reason.

## Main gaps

- filing, judgment, order, move and homelessness as separate events;
- rent arrears and the first income or health shock;
- legal help, rental aid and landlord payment plans;
- work, school, care and hospital outcomes;
- credit use, debt and later housing;
- differences by race, gender, children, disability and place.

## Decision rule

Keep pre-court distress beside the estimated order effect. If only a filing or correlation is measured, record housing risk without claiming a full eviction cascade.
