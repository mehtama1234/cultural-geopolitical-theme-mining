# Finding 001: Closing a SNAP enrollment office can reduce nearby participation before household need disappears

**Status:** bounded causal place-and-public-benefit finding · **Checked:** 2026-09-22

## The bounded finding

An NBER study links administrative SNAP receipt data to the locations of
enrollment offices in one state. Using event-study comparisons around office
openings and closures, the authors estimate that SNAP participation in a
census tract fell by about 7% to 9% over the two years after an office closed.
The study reports suggestive increases after office openings, with the effects
concentrated in urban areas.

The practical route is:

```text
need or eligibility
  -> local enrollment office exists or closes
  -> application and participation route changes
  -> [open] benefit adequacy and food security
  -> [open] household room, trust, and later action
```

This is a meaningful place effect because it shows that participation can
change when the physical route changes, even though eligibility rules and the
underlying need may not have changed at the same time.

## What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a causal place mechanism to the recurring societal pattern:

> Access to a public benefit is partly a geography problem, not only a legal
> eligibility problem.

The finding sharpens the difference between “the program exists” and “people
nearby can practically reach the route.” Office closure can make a benefit
less usable without proving that people became less eligible or that the
program's formal rules changed.

It also adds a counterweight to a purely digital story. The study does not say
that every person needs a physical office; remote, mobile, telephone, or other
routes may substitute. But the observed area-level decline means those
substitutes did not fully replace the local route in the studied setting.

## Limits and next test

- The study uses one state's administrative SNAP data; the estimate is not a
  national participation effect.
- The outcome is census-tract participation, not a same-household measure of
  application effort, benefit amount, food security, or hardship.
- The summary reports effects concentrated in urban areas but does not establish
  the mechanism for rural, suburban, disabled, older, or transportation-limited
  households.
- Office closure may coincide with other neighborhood or administrative changes,
  although the authors report robustness checks addressing office-placement
  endogeneity.
- Participation does not show whether benefits were adequate or whether a
  household later stayed fed, trusted the agency, or took political action.

The next decisive artifact is a multi-state, office- and household-protected
ledger joining office distance and closure, application attempts, channel used,
approval, benefit amount, travel/time cost, food security, and later renewal or
non-use. Until then, the safe claim is that physical enrollment access is a
causal place boundary for SNAP participation in the studied setting.

## Source

- [NBER Working Paper 34529, The Intersection of Place and Need](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34529)

**Evidence status:** primary research reports an event-study association with a
causal design linking office closure to lower nearby SNAP participation; the
national, household-welfare, and substitution effects remain open.
