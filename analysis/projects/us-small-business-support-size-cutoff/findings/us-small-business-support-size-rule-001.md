# Finding 001: Expanding a small-business boundary can redistribute opportunity toward scale

**Status:** causal firm-allocation evidence · **Checked:** 2026-09-17

## The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 35703 studies expansions in US small-business program
eligibility using quasi-random variation in the timing of those expansions and
administrative Census data. The abstract reports that larger firms gained
access while the smallest firms—especially younger, more productive, and
financially constrained firms—experienced lower revenue. Government contracts
shifted to larger firms; exits increased, wages declined, and patenting fell.

The result is important because “more eligible firms” can mean less access for
the firms at the bottom of the size distribution. It is a firm-allocation and
labor/innovation finding, not yet a customer-welfare or owner-household result.

The earlier CES/IZA version gives scale to the exit margin: the smallest firms
became about 0.5 percentage points more likely to exit, while industry-wide
exits rose 12.7% and new firm creation was reported unchanged. These are
version-specific estimates from the earlier paper, recorded in the
[effect-size audit](../effect-size-audit-v1.md); they are not silently treated
as estimates from the current NBER revision.

## Event chain

```text
eligibility boundary expands toward larger firms
  -> support and procurement reallocate
  -> smallest firms lose revenue and some exit
  -> wages and patenting fall
  -> [open] prices, quality, workers' lived security, owners' households,
         local choice, legitimacy, and political response
```

## What this contributes to the broad goal

| Stage | Supported | Still open |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | Eligibility standards expand toward larger firms | Exact program, threshold, timing, and compliance cost |
| Allocation | Government contracts reallocate to larger firms | Contract value, quality, duration, and who actually receives support |
| Small-firm effect | Revenue falls; exits increase; effects are stronger for constrained firms | Owner wealth, debt, closure reason, and alternative financing |
| Labor/innovation | Wages and patenting decline | Hours, benefits, job quality, worker mobility, and patent commercialization |
| Public consequence | A support label can conceal redistribution by firm scale | Prices, local services, consumer switching, trust, political action |

This is a useful counterweight to the disaster-loan case. Disaster credit can
keep constrained firms operating, while a broadened eligibility rule can shift
public opportunity away from constrained small firms. The two results concern
different policy settings and should not be pooled; together they show why the
policy instrument and eligibility boundary are part of the social trend.

## Interpretation rule

```text
more eligible firms       != more access for the smallest firms
contract reallocation     != automatic consumer harm
revenue decline           != worker job loss for every firm
wage decline              != measured household poverty
patenting decline         != immediate innovation disappearance
```

The abstract does not reveal whether larger firms' gains improved service,
lowered prices, or increased employment elsewhere. Those are essential open
arrows for a societal and consumer interpretation.

## Next decisive test

Acquire only the smallest official table or appendix needed to record the
program identity, threshold changes, effect sizes, uncertainty, firm-size and
owner heterogeneity, contract outcomes, worker hours/benefits, and customer
prices or quality. Then connect support receipt to owner households and local
service availability where identifiers permit; do not infer welfare from
revenue or patenting alone.
