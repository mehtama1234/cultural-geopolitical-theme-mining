# Claims ledger: US small-business support and size rules

**Check date:** 2026-09-11  
**Scope:** United States; eligibility rules, public contracts, firm size, revenue, survival, wages, and innovation

| ID | Claim | Evidence type | What supports it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | Raising a program's size limit can reduce revenue for the smallest firms. | Census-linked causal study | Brown, Denes, Duchin, and Hackney use quasi-random timing of US size-standard changes and find revenue declines for the smallest firms. | The paper studies the firms and industries in its data, not every support program. |
| C-002 | Some public contracts move toward larger firms after eligibility expands. | Procurement and firm data | The study links the small-firm decline to reallocation of government procurement contracts to larger firms. | A larger supplier may offer scale or reliability; contract share alone is not public harm. |
| C-003 | The smallest firms can face higher exit, lower wages, and less innovation after the rule change. | Firm and industry outcomes | The NBER abstract reports increased exits, lower wages, and lower patenting, with stronger effects for younger, more productive, and financially constrained firms. | It does not show the owner's household result, customer prices, or service quality. |
| C-004 | “More eligible firms” and “more help to the firms with the least room” are different goals. | Policy interpretation | The evidence shows a wider rule can increase access for larger firms while crowding out smaller ones. | The best cutoff still depends on price, quality, jobs, and delivery capacity. |
| C-005 | A size rule is a distribution choice, not just an administrative detail. | Our inference | C-001 through C-004 connect eligibility, contracts, firm survival, wages, and innovation. | Household and customer effects remain open. |

## Mechanism under test

```text
eligibility rule changes
  -> larger firms gain access to support or contracts
  -> public money and demand move across firms
  -> smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival
  -> wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change
```
