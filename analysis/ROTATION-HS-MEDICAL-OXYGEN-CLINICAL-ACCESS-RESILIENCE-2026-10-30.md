# Rotation HS: Medical oxygen, bedside treatment and resilient clinical access

**Date:** 2026-10-30
**Lane:** health security / clinical infrastructure / public health / conflict / energy / geography
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 oxygen-system-to-patient-outcome question

## Research question

When governments and partners install oxygen plants, concentrators, cylinders,
solar systems and service contracts, do those assets convert into reliable
bedside treatment, equitable emergency referral and survival—or do power,
maintenance, distribution, skills, cost and clinical workflow leave the asset
underused or inaccessible?

## Conversion chain

```text
respiratory/critical-care need
  -> oxygen policy, plant/equipment, power, procurement and distribution
  -> quality assurance, maintenance, trained staff and clinical indication
  -> oxygen reaches the patient at the right flow and time
  -> treatment completion, referral and recovery
  -> resilient facility, lower mortality and trusted local care
```

Oxygen access is a system property, not an equipment inventory. The unit is the
patient, facility, oxygen source, power, pipeline/cylinder, staff, indication,
referral, price and outcome.

## Evidence packet

| ID | Source and direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| HS1 | [WHO access-to-oxygen statement](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/statement-on-access-to-quality-and-safe-medical-oxygen) estimates fewer than half of health facilities in low- and middle-income countries have uninterrupted oxygen access and identifies production, distribution, power, equipment, storage, funding, skills and maintenance barriers. | Global access problem and system-barrier map | Facility uptime, bedside receipt, clinical indication, equity and outcome |
| HS2 | [WHO national oxygen scale-up guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240106512) sets six planning sections: situation analysis, goals, governance, implementation, impact/outcomes and costing/financing. | National planning architecture and evaluation requirement | Actual plan execution, financing, maintenance and patient outcomes |
| HS3 | [WHO oxygen initiative](https://www.who.int/initiatives/oxygen-access-scale-up) describes oxygen as an essential medicine and provides tools for total cost, energy, plant performance and preventive maintenance. | Technical planning and lifecycle tools | Local staff use, uptime, quality, affordability and treatment completion |
| HS4 | [WHO Kabul oxygen-plant case](https://www.emro.who.int/afg/afghanistan-news/a-new-oxygen-facility-at-kabuls-childrens-hospital-cuts-child-mortality-rates.html) reports a plant opened in May 2025 can produce up to 210 cylinders per day and supports the children’s hospital and nearby hospitals after unreliable deliveries. | Named production, distribution and service-expansion case | Independent mortality attribution, cylinder delivery, maintenance and equity |
| HS5 | [WHO Somalia case study](https://www.emro.who.int/images/stories/somalia/strengthening-public-health-systems-case-study-august-2-2023.pdf?ua=1) reports solar-powered systems in six facilities treated 1,059 patients with low oxygen saturation and 93.6% were discharged without disability. | Named facility-to-patient chain and bounded outcome | Counterfactual, selection, recurrence, system scale and long-run uptime |
| HS6 | [WHO Yemen 2025 response](https://www.emro.who.int/yemen/news/saving-lives-amid-escalating-conflict-in-yemen.html) reports approximately 3.96 million litres of oxygen delivered to 52 facilities and fuel to 177 facilities during conflict. | Named conflict logistics and continuity operation | Patient receipt, clinical outcomes, distribution, security and sustainability |
| HS7 | [UNICEF Uganda oxygen-as-a-service](https://www.unicef.org/supply/media/25326/file) reports a performance-linked model piloted in 35 facilities since March 2024, reaching an estimated 6,000 children/newborns in 2025 with near-100% uptime. | Named procurement/maintenance model and service signal | Independent patient outcomes, affordability, rural equity and post-contract continuity |
| HS8 | [WHO Nepal oxygen-roadmap update](https://www.who.int/nepal/news/detail/14-01-2026-koshi-province-hosts-high-level-meeting-on-advancing-national-oxygen-roadmap) records concerns about plant functionality, maintenance, supply gaps and inter-provincial coordination. | Named governance and operational risk boundary | Corrective action, uptime, patient access and provincial equity |

## Stage audit

| Observed signal | Safe interpretation | Prohibited leap |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen plant, concentrator or cylinders installed | Production or equipment capacity exists | Oxygen reaches the indicated patient safely and continuously |
| Litres delivered or cylinders produced | A logistics quantity moved through a system | Bedside administration, correct flow, affordability or recovery |
| Uptime percentage | System availability was measured for a defined period | Clinical demand was met, maintenance is durable or all facilities benefited |
| Staff training or oxygen roadmap | Capability-building and governance activity occurred | Staff use equipment correctly and patients survive because of it |
| Discharge without disability in a named case | A bounded outcome was observed in a treated cohort | The intervention caused the outcome or generalizes to the system |

## Bounded finding

> Oxygen plants, equipment and service models can close critical infrastructure
> gaps, but evidence supports promotion only of the distinction between asset/
> logistics capacity and reliable equitable bedside treatment. The mechanism
> remains Tier 2 until a named facility or cohort links need, equipment, power,
> quality, uptime, staff, administration, referral and patient outcome.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Oxygen is experienced through trust in clinicians, family decisions, language,
cost, gender, disability, conflict and distance. A plant can reduce dependence
on imported cylinders while still failing patients who cannot reach the facility,
whose family cannot pay for transport, or whose staff lack pulse oximeters and
clinical confidence.

Remote islands, conflict zones and rural districts expose the geography of
maintenance: spare parts, fuel, solar batteries, roads, procurement and trained
technicians matter as much as the plant. Local production can support dignity
and sovereignty, but only if governance, tariffs, safety and referral systems
remain accountable to patients.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Plant capacity or delivery volume does not establish bedside administration or
  clinical appropriateness.
- A high uptime measure can coexist with low demand capture, staff shortages,
  stock-outs of accessories or patients unable to reach care.
- The Somalia cohort is a named observational case and does not establish a
  counterfactual causal effect.
- Emergency logistics in Yemen can preserve care temporarily without proving
  sustainable system financing or long-run equity.
- Oxygen outcomes depend on diagnosis, staffing, antibiotics, ventilation,
  referral and broader clinical infrastructure; attribution must remain bounded.

## Promotion decision

Promote the asset/logistics-capacity-versus-bedside-treatment distinction to
Tier 1. Keep the oxygen-system-to-equitable-survival-and-resilience mechanism in
Tier 2.
