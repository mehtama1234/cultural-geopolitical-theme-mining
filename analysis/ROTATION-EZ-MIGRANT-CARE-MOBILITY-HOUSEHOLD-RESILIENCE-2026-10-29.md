# Rotation EZ — Migrant care mobility, reciprocal care and household resilience

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 migrant-care-mobility-to-reciprocal-care-and-household-resilience conversion question  
**Research lane:** migrant care work / domestic work / health-worker mobility / recruitment / gender / social protection / remittances / family separation / care continuity / skills recognition / rights / destination communities

## Why this rotation

The atlas contains care infrastructure, ageing, gender mobility, remittances,
health workforce and household resilience themes. This packet connects them
through the migrant care worker: how a person moves through recruitment,
recognition, employment, care work, remittance and family-care systems, and how
the origin and destination households and communities share benefits and risks.

Migrant-worker counts, vacancies filled, remittance totals, bilateral
agreements, recruitment programmes, skills certificates and care-service
coverage are important signals. They do not by themselves establish decent
wages, safe recruitment, social-protection portability, care quality, family
continuity, worker agency, reduced unpaid-care burden, destination capacity or
reciprocal benefit across the migration corridor.

## Research question

When care systems rely on migrant domestic, home-based, community or health
workers, under what conditions does cross-border mobility produce decent work,
ethical recruitment, recognized skills, portable protection, continuous care
and resilient origin and destination households without shifting unpaid care,
debt, separation, exploitation or health-system loss onto women, children,
older people, disabled people or migrant communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
care shortage / recruitment corridor / visa / training / bilateral agreement
  -> recruitment fee, migration decision, skills recognition and work placement
  -> wage, hours, task, employer, residence, social protection and care encounter
  -> worker safety, care quality, remittance, family care, health, income and agency
  -> household resilience, destination care continuity, origin investment and rights
  -> trust, reciprocity, retention, return, bargaining power and durable policy
```

The minimum unit is a named worker, household, recruiter, employer, care
recipient, facility, corridor, contract, benefit, remittance and time period.
Migrant-worker volume or remittance flow is an intermediate signal—not proof
of reciprocal care or resilience.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Migrant care work fills demand while protection gaps persist

ILO’s 2024 policy brief identifies growing demand for migrant care workers,
particularly women, alongside gaps in labour-migration governance, decent work
and adequate protection. It highlights the need to meet labour-market needs
while safeguarding migrant care workers’ rights. The operational chain must
therefore include recruitment, contract, working time, pay, status, grievance
and care quality.

Source: [ILO, Migrant workers in the care economy](https://www.ilo.org/publications/migrant-workers-care-economy).

### 2. Migration status and employment form shape social protection

ILO’s evidence notes that migrant care and domestic workers can face legal and
practical barriers to social protection, particularly in insecure or
non-standard work. It also highlights maternity-protection gaps and the risks
of recruitment, platform work and employer dependence. Portability must be
tested as actual enrollment, contribution, claim, payment and family coverage.

Source: [ILO, migrant workers in the care economy—technical note](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/Migrant%20Workers%20in%20the%20Care%20Economy_Note_2024_Final.pdf).

### 3. Health-worker mobility needs mutual benefit and ethical governance

WHO guidance on bilateral agreements for health-worker migration emphasizes
well-managed mobility, avoiding health-workforce shortages and safeguarding
international health security. A signed agreement is a governance mechanism;
the atlas must still follow origin staffing, destination workload, recognition,
retention, worker rights and care-recipient outcomes.

Source: [WHO, bilateral agreements on health-worker migration and mobility](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news/detail/29-05-2024-bilateral-agreements-on-health-worker-migration-and-mobility--who-releases-new-guidance).

### 4. Fair migration requires justice, not only labour supply

ILO’s 2025 evidence-based labour-migration brief estimates 167.7 million
international migrants in the labour force in 2022 and emphasizes sustainable
governance, labour-market linkages, rights, protection and access to justice.
The care corridor should be assessed as an institution of rights and bargaining
power, not merely as a way to fill vacancies.

Source: [ILO, evidence-based governance of labour migration](https://www.ilo.org/publications/promoting-evidence-based-policymaking-fair-and-effective-governance-labour).

### 5. Gendered origin and destination effects can be simultaneous

UN Women’s Ethiopia evidence describes women migrating to Gulf countries for
care and domestic work, with remittance contributions and planned measurement of
social-protection access. This illustrates the double ledger: paid care can
increase migrant women’s income and household support while exposing them to
recruitment cost, unsafe work, family separation and weak protection.

Source: [UN Women, gender data and migration policy](https://data.unwomen.org/index.php/features/gender-data-inform-gender-equality-and-migration-policies-and-shape-economic-programmes).

### 6. Corridor-level protection needs practical grievance and portability

The ILO’s 2026 Kerala engagement identifies unsafe recruitment pathways, high
recruitment costs, uneven working conditions, social-security access and
grievance redressal as practical priorities for migrant care workers. It also
points toward care corridors beyond nursing, including domestic and caregiver
work. This is a live test of whether policy architecture reaches workers in
contracts and households.

Source: [ILO, Kerala and migrant care workers](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-kerala-strengthen-efforts-promote-decent-work-migrant-care-workers).

## Bounded proposition 157

**A migrant-care recruitment programme, bilateral health-worker agreement,
visa pathway, skills certificate, placement, remittance flow, care-service
expansion or social-protection portability policy is not by itself decent work,
ethical migration, safe and continuous care, household resilience, gender
equality, reciprocal development or migrant agency.** Worker counts, vacancies
filled, contracts issued, credentials recognized, remittances sent, care hours
provided or agreements signed do not establish that named workers avoided debt
and abuse, received fair pay and remedy, retained family and social protection,
that care recipients received safe continuous care, or that origin and
destination households shared durable benefits rather than exporting unpaid
care and risk.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Care moves through relationships.** A worker’s migration changes care for
   the recipient, the worker’s children or elders, co-workers and the employer
   household at both ends of the corridor.
2. **The corridor has unequal bargaining power.** Recruiters, employers, states,
   agencies and workers control different documents, wages, information and
   exit rights.
3. **Remittances do not replace care.** Money can support food, schooling and
   health while physical absence transfers emotional, supervisory and unpaid
   care to other household members.
4. **Skills recognition is social recognition.** Credentialing affects status,
   pay, task allocation, mobility, trust and whether migrant expertise is treated
   as professional or disposable.
5. **Portability is a lived route.** A right that cannot travel across employer,
   visa, country, language or family status is not practical social protection.
6. **Reciprocity includes destination care quality.** A system that protects
   workers but leaves care recipients without continuity is incomplete; a system
   that supplies care through exploitation is also incomplete.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A vacancy can be filled while recruitment debt, wage theft, unsafe housing or
  employer-tied status makes the job unsustainable.
- Remittances can rise while children, older people or disabled relatives lose
  daily care, supervision and emotional support.
- A bilateral agreement can protect nurses while domestic, home-based or
  platform-mediated caregivers remain outside coverage.
- Skills recognition can improve status for some workers while excluding those
  without documents, language access or money for credentialing.
- Destination care quality can improve in aggregate while migrant workers face
  long hours, violence, unpaid overtime or high turnover that disrupts continuity.
- Social-protection portability can exist in law while contributions, claims,
  maternity benefits or family coverage cannot be accessed across borders.
- Recruitment formalization can reduce some exploitation while increasing fees,
  surveillance or exclusion for workers who use informal community networks.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. ILO, WHO and UN
Women evidence supports the care-demand, protection, health-worker, justice,
gender and corridor mechanisms. Keep the integrated migrant-care-mobility-to-
reciprocal-care-and-household-resilience outcome at **Tier 2** because public
sources rarely follow the same worker and linked households through recruitment,
care work, remittances, social protection, family care and long-term outcomes.

## Next test

Select one corridor and follow one domestic or home-care worker, one migrant
health worker, one destination care recipient and one origin household. Record
recruitment cost, debt, contract, visa, wages, hours, housing, care tasks,
skills recognition, social protection, grievance, remittance, family-care
arrangement, recipient continuity and later retention or return. Compare formal
and informal routes and women- and men-led households.

## Source boundary

The packet closes the care-demand, social-protection, health-worker, justice,
gender and corridor-governance mechanisms. It does not close named recruitment
debt, worker safety, fair pay, care quality, family continuity, portable
benefit receipt, remittance use, origin staffing, destination capacity,
retention, return, bargaining power or remedy. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most migrant-care sources. Labour mobility and remittance statistics are kept
separate from reciprocal care and household resilience.
