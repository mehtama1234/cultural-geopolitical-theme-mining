# Rotation EV — AI-enabled decisions, fair remedy and institutional accountability

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 AI-justice-to-fair-remedy conversion question  
**Research lane:** algorithmic decision-making / public services / benefits / housing / employment / credit / human review / explanation / complaint / remedy / power

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes public AI, AI tax administration, algorithmic hiring,
algorithmic housing, predictive public safety, child welfare and AI work
control. This deepening rotation isolates the common institutional question:
when an AI-assisted decision harms a person, can that person know what happened,
reach a competent human, challenge the decision, obtain a correction and
recover the material loss?

An AI inventory, impact assessment, fairness metric, model card, procurement
clause, human-in-the-loop label, explanation screen or complaint channel is an
important governance signal. It does not by itself establish that a named
person received notice, understood the decision, had evidence access, reached
an empowered reviewer, received a timely correction, avoided retaliation or
recovered the lost benefit, job, housing, credit, service or liberty.

## Research question

When an organization deploys AI or algorithmic decision support in benefits,
tax, health, education, housing, employment, credit, policing or platform work,
under what conditions does governance produce accessible notice, meaningful
explanation, independent human review, correction, compensation and durable
institutional learning for affected people across language, disability, class,
gender, race, migration status, geography and digital-access differences?

## Conversion chain

```text
data / model / vendor / policy / deployment / automated recommendation
  -> score, classification, triage, ranking, denial, flag or account action
  -> notice, reason, evidence access, human review, complaint and appeal
  -> correction, restored access, compensation, changed model or institutional learning
  -> service, income, housing, work, health, education, liberty and dignity
  -> trust, agency, legitimacy, compliance, refusal, litigation or exit
```

The minimum unit is a named affected person or group, decision, model or
workflow, deployer, date, jurisdiction, adverse effect, notice, reviewer,
appeal, correction, remedy and later outcome. A documented policy is an
intermediate governance signal—not proof of effective remedy.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Human review and appeal are explicit requirements, not outcome evidence

GAO’s 2025 review of federal AI requirements records a requirement for
high-impact uses: affected people should have timely human review and a chance
to appeal negative impacts, with remedies designed to avoid unnecessary burden.
This establishes a strong control expectation, but the atlas must still ask
whether the reviewer had authority, relevant evidence, time and independence,
and whether the person received a corrected material outcome.

Source: [GAO, Federal AI efforts and requirements](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107933/index.html).

### 2. Government AI governance still has weak accountability links

The OECD Digital Government Outlook 2026 reports that transparency,
algorithmic accountability and impact assessment remain among the weaker areas
of digital-government performance. It emphasizes governing not only the model,
but also the action space, workflow approval points, oversight and redress.
The operational implication is that model quality cannot substitute for an
accountable decision pathway.

Source: [OECD Digital Government Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/digital-government-outlook_0496b2bc-en/full-report/digital-governments-at-a-turning-point_0491aad4.html).

### 3. International human-rights governance links information to challenge

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI requires information to be
made available to affected persons in a form sufficient to challenge decisions
made through or substantially based on AI, together with effective procedural
guarantees and a possibility to complain to competent authorities. This is a
rights architecture, not evidence that an affected person could practically
use it across language, literacy, disability, cost and power barriers.

Source: [Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence).

### 4. A legal right to explanation is narrower than a restored outcome

Article 86 of the EU AI Act provides an affected person subject to certain
high-risk AI-based decisions with a right to obtain clear and meaningful
explanations of the role of the AI system and the main elements of the decision.
The right is valuable, but an explanation may still be inaccessible, too late,
non-actionable or unable to reverse a decision. The conversion test must
separate explanation, human intervention, correction and recovery.

Source: [EU AI Act Article 86](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-86).

### 5. AI fraud controls expose the tension between accuracy and access

GAO’s 2025 work on AI for improper-payment and fraud detection identifies data
quality and workforce capability as essential to responsible use. An AI system
may improve detection while also generating false positives, documentation
burdens or delayed payment for people who cannot prove eligibility. Error rates
must therefore be joined to appeal reachability, processing time, restored
benefits and hardship during the review period.

Source: [GAO, AI and improper payments](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-108412/index.html).

### 6. The workplace shows why a designated human is not enough

ILO evidence on algorithmic management and recent platform-law developments
emphasize explanation, a contact person, worker participation and access to
remedy for decisions affecting work, pay or account status. A contact person
without authority, data access or protection from the employer can convert a
formal right into procedural theatre. Worker power, collective voice and
retaliation risk must be recorded alongside individual appeal.

Source: [ILO, global case studies of social dialogue on AI and algorithmic management](https://www.ilo.org/publications/global-case-studies-social-dialogue-ai-and-algorithmic-management).

## Bounded proposition 153

**An AI inventory, impact assessment, fairness test, model card, procurement
standard, human-in-the-loop control, explanation, complaint channel or legal
right to appeal is not by itself fair treatment, accurate eligibility, restored
benefits, secure housing, equitable employment, affordable credit, safe care,
institutional accountability or effective remedy.** Model performance,
documentation, notices, review requests, explanations, complaints or policy
compliance do not establish that a named person understood the decision, could
reach an empowered and independent reviewer, obtained timely correction and
recovered the material service, income, housing, health, education, liberty or
dignity lost through the AI-enabled action.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Remedy is a relationship of power.** The person must be able to confront
   an institution, vendor or state with enough information and standing to
   change the outcome.
2. **Human review is culturally and geographically uneven.** Language, office
   distance, disability access, legal aid, connectivity and working time affect
   whether a review right is usable.
3. **Explanation must be actionable.** A technical feature description is not a
   reason a person can understand, contest or use to supply missing evidence.
4. **Error has unequal prices.** A wrong fraud flag, benefit denial, eviction
   score or account suspension can impose hunger, homelessness, job loss, debt,
   stigma or migration risk before appeal is resolved.
5. **Institutions can learn—or individualize blame.** A corrected case should
   change data, workflow, vendor controls or policy where appropriate; otherwise
   remedy remains private repair without public accountability.
6. **The same governance label means different things locally.** A “human in
   the loop” may be an empowered adjudicator in one place and a pressured
   operator who cannot override the system in another.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A model can have high aggregate accuracy while systematically imposing more
  false positives or slower remedy on a subgroup.
- A human review can exist on paper while deadlines, documentation, language or
  travel make it practically unreachable.
- An explanation can identify features without revealing the evidence, legal
  rule, threshold or decision-maker needed to contest the outcome.
- A complaint can be closed procedurally while the benefit, job, housing or
  credit loss remains unrepaired.
- A vendor can provide documentation while the public deployer cannot inspect
  or correct the underlying data.
- A fairness audit can improve measured parity while increasing exclusion or
  reducing access for everyone through overly conservative thresholds.
- A corrected individual case can be restored without changing the model,
  workflow or institutional practice that harmed the next people.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. GAO, OECD,
Council of Europe, EU and ILO sources support the separate governance,
explanation, human-review, workplace and remedy mechanisms. Keep the integrated
AI-justice-to-fair-remedy outcome at **Tier 2** because public sources rarely
follow the same affected person from AI exposure through explanation, appeal,
correction, material recovery and institutional learning.

## Source boundary

The packet closes the requirement, accountability, human-rights, explanation,
fraud-control and workplace-remedy mechanisms. It does not close named
exposure, notice comprehension, evidence access, reviewer authority,
correction, compensation, service restoration, subgroup burden, retaliation,
model change or durable institutional learning. Those remain acquisition
targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most AI governance sources. This is why the packet treats remedy as a chain of
observable events, not as a policy adjective.
