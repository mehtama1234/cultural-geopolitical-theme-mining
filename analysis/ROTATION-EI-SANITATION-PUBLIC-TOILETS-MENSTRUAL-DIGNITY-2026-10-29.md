# Rotation EI — Sanitation, public toilets, menstrual dignity and inclusive access

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** WASH / public health / gender / disability / schools / labour / urban culture

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes water security, wastewater, informal food markets, public
space, school attendance and health information. This rotation isolates the
sanitation encounter: toilets, containment, cleaning, water, soap, menstrual
materials, privacy, accessibility, safety, waste handling, sanitation work and
public or institutional maintenance.

A toilet can exist but be locked, broken, unsafe, inaccessible, unclean,
unaffordable or unusable during menstruation. A school or health facility can
report coverage while girls, patients, workers, disabled people, older people,
vendors and sanitation workers experience lost time, stigma, infection risk,
violence or exclusion. The unit is the usable service and its social meaning.

## Research question

When a government, school, health facility, employer, market, transport authority
or municipality builds toilets, expands sewerage, distributes menstrual products,
improves cleaning, formalizes sanitation work or introduces public-toilet policy,
under what conditions does it produce safe, private, accessible, affordable and
dignified use without shifting risks to users, cleaners or downstream communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
bodily need / sanitation risk / menstruation / disability / public movement
  -> toilet, water, privacy, cleaning, waste, accessibility and service policy
  -> home, school, clinic, workplace, market, transit and public-space encounter
  -> usable access, safety, time, hygiene, stigma, health and attendance result
  -> dignity, learning, work, care, mobility, trust and public belonging
  -> worker protection, maintenance authority, accountability and remedy
```

The unit must be a named toilet, school, facility, workplace, market, transit
site, household, user group or sanitation worker, with usability, privacy,
cleanliness, water, products, accessibility, safety and outcome dates. A toilet
count, coverage rate, product distribution or construction completion is an
intermediate signal, not proof of use, dignity, health or continuity.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Basic sanitation is functional, usable and private—not merely installed

UNICEF’s latest school WASH reporting defines usable sanitation through access,
functionality and privacy: toilets must be accessible, not broken or blocked,
have water where needed, and have doors that close and lock. In 2025, 460
million school-age children lacked a basic sanitation service at school, with
many facilities improved but unusable or not single-sex.

This closes an important measurement distinction: construction and “improved”
classification do not establish daily access or learning continuity.

Source: [UNICEF WASH in schools](https://data.unicef.org/topic/water-and-sanitation/wash-in-schools/).

### 2. Menstrual health joins infrastructure, materials, information and stigma

WHO defines menstrual health as access to accurate information, affordable and
acceptable materials, safe WASH and waste services, care for menstrual problems,
freedom from stigma, and the ability to participate in life. It estimates that
around 500 million people who menstruate lack the materials and facilities needed
for menstrual health, and notes that roughly 39% of schools provide menstrual
health education.

The conversion test must include private washing/changing, disposal or reusable
material management, pain and health support, information, affordability and
attendance—not product distribution alone.

Source: [WHO menstrual health fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/menstrual-health).

### 3. Policy can exist while daily reality remains unequal

UNICEF’s 2026 Ghana note describes persistent school WASH gaps despite policy
progress and a free-sanitary-pad initiative. Its evidence emphasizes that
infrastructure, tracking, safe practice and accountability are needed to sustain
progress. UNICEF school data similarly show that global coverage can rise while
hundreds of millions of children remain without usable services.

This is a source-boundary and implementation lesson: national policy, allocated
budget and distribution targets do not close school-level access, privacy,
cleanliness or attendance.

Sources: [UNICEF Ghana period-equity evidence](https://www.unicef.org/ghana/press-releases/menstrual-health-day-accelerating-progress-toward-period-equity-in-ghana)
and [UNICEF menstrual-health school facts](https://www.unicef.org/press-releases/10-fast-facts-menstrual-health-schools).

### 4. Health facilities require safe, inclusive and dignified sanitation

WHO/UNICEF report that only around half of health facilities had sex-separated
toilets, accessible toilets or menstrual-hygiene facilities in the latest
assessment. They stress that reliable water, sanitation, hand hygiene,
environmental cleaning and waste management must be integrated into health
planning and monitoring.

A health facility without usable sanitation can create risks for patients,
newborns, staff and cleaners. The outcome is care continuity and dignity, not
simply facility coverage.

Source: [WHO/UNICEF health-facility sanitation evidence](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-08-2026-who-unicef-report-highlights-gaps-in-health-facility-sanitation).

### 5. The whole sanitation chain includes workers and downstream places

WHO guidance calls for safe systems across the sanitation chain: durable and
cleanable toilets, privacy, menstrual facilities, safe containment, operation
and maintenance, reduced manual emptying where possible, worker safety, and
adequate treatment before disposal or reuse. Sanitation workers therefore sit at
the conversion point between user dignity and public-health protection.

The test must include cleaners, pit emptiers, sewer workers, waste handlers and
downstream communities. A clean user-facing toilet can coexist with unsafe work,
unpaid labour, exposure or environmental contamination.

Sources: [WHO sanitation-chain guidance](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/378095/9789240095380-eng.pdf?sequence=1)
and [WHO sanitation-worker health, safety and dignity](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/health-safety-and-dignity-of-sanitation-workers).

### 6. Disability, age, gender and public movement change the standard

WHO/UNICEF explicitly identify people with limited mobility, women and girls as
groups whose needs must be met for a sanitation service to qualify as basic.
People who menstruate, children, older people, disabled people, migrants,
homeless people, market workers and people in informal employment may encounter
different access, safety, stigma and time burdens.

The useful standard is not a universal toilet count. It is whether a named user
can reach, enter, use, clean, dispose, leave and return safely and privately at
the time and place required.

## Bounded proposition 140

**A toilet block, sewer connection, sanitation policy, menstrual-product scheme,
school WASH project, public-toilet programme, cleaning contract, accessible
facility, hygiene campaign or sanitation-worker formalization programme is not by
itself safe sanitation, menstrual dignity, health protection, attendance, work
continuity, disability inclusion, public-space belonging or worker justice.**
Toilet counts, coverage rates, products distributed, construction completion,
inspection scores, handwashing stations, complaint closure or worker registration
do not establish that named users could reach and use a clean private facility,
manage menstruation, avoid stigma and violence, maintain attendance and work,
receive accessible service, obtain safe waste handling, or exercise voice and
remedy when the system failed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Sanitation is dignity infrastructure.** Privacy, smell, bodily control,
   cleanliness and the ability to participate shape belonging and self-respect.
2. **The toilet is a time-place institution.** A locked or distant facility can
   change school, work, care, market and transit choices.
3. **Menstrual health is social as well as material.** Products without water,
   privacy, disposal, information and stigma reduction do not produce freedom.
4. **Public access is a mobility right.** People who spend the day outside home
   need sanitation in markets, streets, transit, workplaces and public services.
5. **Invisible labour carries public health.** Cleaners and waste workers absorb
   risks that the user-facing service may conceal.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A new school toilet can reduce absenteeism while remaining locked, unsafe,
  unclean, inaccessible or without water and menstrual disposal.
- Free products can improve participation while stigma, pain, privacy or poor
  sanitation remains unresolved.
- A public toilet can be accessible on paper but unusable for wheelchair users,
  older people, caregivers or people with different bodily needs.
- A cleaning contract can improve visible cleanliness while underpaying workers,
  increasing chemical exposure or failing during nights and weekends.
- Sewerage expansion can improve service for connected households while exposing
  informal users or downstream communities to overflow and contamination.
- Menstrual or sanitation indicators can improve nationally while remote schools,
  migrants, homeless users, informal workers and disabled people remain excluded.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, UNICEF and
WHO/UNICEF sources support the usability, menstrual, health-facility, worker,
accessibility and dignity mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2**
because public sources rarely follow the same users and workers through daily
use, health, attendance, safety, affordability, maintenance and remedy.

## Next test

system, and one under-served comparison site. Follow menstruating students,
disabled users, older people, caregivers, workers, cleaners and sanitation

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the usability, menstrual, health-facility, worker,
accessibility and dignity mechanisms. It does not close named daily use, health,
attendance, safety, affordability, maintenance, worker exposure, downstream
quality or remedy outcomes. Those remain acquisition targets.
