# Rotation IC — Assistive technology access, repair and participation

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When wheelchairs, hearing aids, communication devices, prostheses, screen readers and other assistive technologies are procured or innovated, do people receive fitted, affordable, culturally and linguistically usable devices with training, repair and replacement—or do programmes stop at distribution and product counts?

## Why this packet matters

Assistive technology is an access system, not a product category. The practical chain is:

```text
functional need / person’s goals
  -> assessment in language and context
  -> suitable product and fitting
  -> financing, procurement and delivery
  -> training, family/teacher/workplace support
  -> maintenance, repair, replacement and adaptation
  -> education, work, care, mobility, communication and civic participation
  -> autonomy, safety and sustained inclusion
```

Failure at any stage can make a distributed device unusable. A school resource set may be shared but not fitted; a screen reader may lack local-language content; a wheelchair may be unavailable after a breakdown; and a policy can exist without a coordinated workforce or financing route.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| IC1 | WHO states that assistive products support independent, dignified participation in education, work and civic life, while the global report cited by UNICEF estimates over 2.5 billion people need at least one assistive product and nearly 1 billion cannot access them. | Global need and participation rationale. | Person-level suitability, fitting, use, repair, affordability or outcome. |
| IC2 | WHO’s European Ireland assessment uses the 5P framework—people, policy, products, provision and personnel—and identifies gaps in coordination, data, funding and service continuity. | Whole-system assessment and service-continuity frame. | Device receipt, user satisfaction, repair time, participation or life-course outcome. |
| IC3 | WHO Africa reports Senegal’s 2021 survey found 62.6% of people needing assistive devices lacked access, with cost, geographic inequality and stigma major barriers; in 2025 devices distributed included wheelchairs, orthoses, glasses and hearing aids. | Named access gap, barriers and distribution response. | Appropriate fitting, continued use, repair, affordability, stigma change or participation. |
| IC4 | UNICEF Kazakhstan’s 2025 ecosystem work adapted assessment tools for Kazakh/Russian contexts, assessed 34 children, distributed individualized devices, created a procurement catalogue, trained educators and supplied resource sets to 11 schools supporting more than 16,000 children. | Local-language assessment, individualized provision, procurement and workforce ecosystem. | Device use, classroom participation, repair, replacement, child outcomes or equitable reach beyond participating schools. |
| IC5 | UNICEF Viet Nam launched Vietnamese text-to-speech software in a southern dialect to improve screen-reader access, digital literacy and access to education and information for visually impaired users. | Linguistic localization and digital-access mechanism. | Adoption, accuracy, device compatibility, continued support, learning or employment outcome. |
| IC6 | UNICEF Bhutan reports students with visual and hearing disabilities used audio narration and sign-language videos; teachers reported increased motivation and collaboration, while noting accessibility and Dzongkha-content improvements needed. | Named classroom use and user/teacher experience signal. | Longitudinal learning, broader access, technology maintenance, sign-language quality or transition to work. |
| IC7 | WHO Namibia reports that a 2025 assessment informed the country’s first National Rehabilitation and Assistive Technology Strategic Plan 2026–2030, with implementation still ahead. | Assessment-to-strategy pathway and implementation boundary. | Financing, workforce, delivery, repair, device receipt or participation. |
| IC8 | UNICEF’s 2025 disability report identifies weak follow-up and repair services, poor integration across education, health and rehabilitation, and notes that low-tech or training-based innovations can outperform attention-heavy technology. | Repair/integration failure and technology-choice counterweight. | Named repair completion, replacement, user control or comparative participation outcomes. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Assistive technology must fit language, body, terrain, climate, school practice, family support, gender, work and local repair capacity. Localization is not only translation: it includes sign language, communication norms, software compatibility and culturally safe assessment. Procurement can create dependence on imported parts and vendors; local repair, open standards and user organizations can shift control toward communities.

## Counterexample and source boundary

The packet treats distribution as an intermediate stage. Senegal’s devices and Kazakhstan’s individualized provision are valuable but do not prove sustained use. Bhutan’s positive classroom reports coexist with gaps in accessible materials and local-language content. The UNICEF repair/integration finding is a direct counterexample to assuming that more sophisticated technology is necessarily more effective. Public evidence closes need, system frameworks, barriers, distribution, localization, classroom use and strategy stages, but not a comparable person-level chain through fitting, repair, autonomy and durable participation.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** An assistive-device distribution, product catalogue, procurement policy, assessment, training, localized software or classroom pilot is not by itself suitable fit, affordability, sustained use, repair, autonomy, safety or participation.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **assistive-technology-system-to-sustained-person-controlled-participation**. The strongest next acquisition is a named person/cohort and device chain linking functional goal, assessment, language, product/fitting, financing, delivery, training, repair/replacement, user control, education/work/mobility outcome and complaint.
