# Rotation FD — Transnational repression, safe residence and diaspora agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 transnational-repression-to-safe-residence-and-diaspora-agency conversion question  
**Research lane:** transnational repression / diaspora / exile / civic space / digital surveillance / family pressure / asylum / host-state sovereignty / safe residence / agency

## Why this rotation

The atlas already distinguishes lawful asylum from border control, and it treats
digital access, identity, civic participation and geopolitical power as separate
conversion problems. This packet examines what happens after a person or family
has crossed a border: whether residence abroad remains practically safe when an
origin state, proxy or cooperating institution reaches across that border to
silence, punish or control them.

Threat counts, arrests, sanctions, public statements, police-station closures,
platform takedowns and training programmes are important signals. They do not by
themselves establish safe residence, family safety, free association, reporting,
political participation, work or study continuity, trusted remedy or diaspora
agency.

## Research question

When a host state identifies transnational repression and responds, under what
conditions does a named person or diaspora group actually regain safe residence,
freedom of expression and association, family security, access to justice and
the practical ability to organize, work, study and participate without retaliation?

## Conversion chain

```text
origin-state threat / proxy / surveillance / family pressure / legal abuse
  -> target recognition, safe reporting and host-state protection
  -> residence, travel, work/study and family safety
  -> association, speech, journalism, cultural participation and civic agency
  -> trusted remedy, accountability and durable diaspora autonomy
  -> host-state sovereignty, democratic legitimacy and geopolitical cooperation
```

The minimum unit is a named target or family, diaspora organization, incident,
platform/account, police or court action, residence status, host locality and
time period. A documented tactic is an operation signal—not proof that a host
state prevented harm or that the affected community can act freely.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. The phenomenon is cross-border coercion, not only overseas crime

OHCHR describes transnational repression as states or proxies reaching beyond
their borders to silence, intimidate or harm people abroad. It includes digital
surveillance, online harassment, family threats, misuse of legal and immigration
systems, extradition attempts and, in severe cases, abduction or killing. OHCHR
also links it to reduced options for defenders, restricted civic space and
deterred diaspora participation.

Source: [OHCHR, Transnational repression: Mapping Trends and Improving Responses](https://europe.ohchr.org/node/610).

### 2. Recognition and response still leave protection gaps

The OHCHR workshop record identifies legal gaps, underreporting caused by fear,
limited awareness, fragmented coordination and weak accountability. A host
government can acknowledge the problem while targets still lack safe reporting,
tailored protection, investigation or remedy.

Source: [OHCHR, Transnational Repression Expert Workshop key takeaways](https://europe.ohchr.org/node/616).

### 3. Diaspora networks can be both social infrastructure and an exposure surface

Freedom House’s campus research records how international students use digital
platforms for family, cultural and diaspora connection while surveillance makes
public-event planning and recruitment more difficult. The relevant cultural
question is therefore not simply whether a group exists, but whether members can
use its language, networks and institutions without exposing themselves or
relatives to retaliation.

Source: [Freedom House, Addressing Transnational Repression on Campuses](https://freedomhouse.org/sites/default/files/2024-01/TNR_UniversityReport_2024.pdf).

### 4. Host-state action can change the operating environment, but closure is not outcome

Freedom House’s 2025 UK report describes increased official awareness, a
cross-agency response and the closure of unofficial police stations after public
pressure. It also argues that effective protection requires resilience of
diaspora communities, attention to migration-policy effects and collaboration
with civil society. A visible institutional response should therefore be tested
against later reporting, participation and family safety.

Source: [Freedom House, From Awareness to Action: Combating Transnational Repression in the UK](https://freedomhouse.org/report/transnational-repression/united-kingdom/2025).

### 5. Scale and geography alter exposure

Freedom House reports incidents across many governments and records cases in
transit, including people seeking asylum. This means safe residence is not
equivalent to having crossed a border: airport checks, passport control,
extradition cooperation and travel routes can remain exposure points.

Source: [Freedom House, Ten Findings from Ten Years of Data](https://freedomhouse.org/sw/node/7279).

### 6. Refugee status and rights create a legal baseline, not a lived guarantee

UNHCR describes the right to seek protection and the need to safeguard refugees’
rights. Its earlier guidance also distinguishes the legal status needed for
political activity from the practical vulnerability of people whose status is
uncertain. The packet keeps formal residence, non-refoulement and freedom of
association separate from safety, family protection and usable civic agency.

Sources: [UNHCR, Who we protect](https://www.unhcr.org/about-unhcr/who-we-protect) and [UNHCR, Refugee status and resettlement handbook](https://www.unhcr.org/resettlement-handbook/1-refugee-status-and-resettlement/1-2-determining-refugee-status-under-unhcrs-mandate/).

## Bounded proposition 161

**A host-state designation, public condemnation, sanctions package, police-station closure, platform intervention, legal prohibition, residence permit, protection referral, diaspora grant, security training or prosecution is not by itself safe residence, family protection, freedom of association, cultural continuity, civic participation, trusted remedy or durable diaspora agency.** Incidents recorded, threats referred, officials trained, channels opened, stations closed, cases prosecuted or organizations funded do not establish that named targets can live, travel, work, study, speak, organize and protect relatives without retaliation, surveillance, stigma, data exposure or institutional abandonment.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Residence is relational.** A person may be physically outside the origin state while family, property, finances, language networks and digital accounts remain exposed there.
2. **Diaspora space is civic infrastructure.** Campuses, religious institutions, media, cultural associations and messaging groups can support belonging and political voice while also becoming surveillance or intimidation surfaces.
3. **Risk is geographically uneven.** Legal status, host-country institutions, consular presence, travel routes, police trust and proximity to relatives change the same tactic’s effect across cities and countries.
4. **Silence can be an outcome.** Low incident reporting or cancelled events may indicate safety, but may also reflect fear, self-censorship, distrust or family-protection strategies.
5. **Migration status mediates agency.** Citizens, permanent residents, students, asylum seekers and undocumented people may have different ability to report, travel, organize or challenge an abusive request.
6. **Protection must include relatives and intermediaries.** A target’s safety cannot be declared durable if family members, colleagues, translators, lawyers or community organizers remain exposed.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A new task force or strategy can coexist with low reporting, weak investigations and persistent fear.
- Closure of an unofficial police station can reduce one visible tactic while surveillance, family pressure or digital harassment continues elsewhere.
- A prosecution or sanction can establish accountability for one incident without restoring employment, travel, family safety or community participation.
- High diaspora turnout at a public event can reflect resilience, but cannot prove that less visible members feel safe.
- Low reported incidents may mean effective prevention, underreporting, self-censorship or inaccessible reporting channels.
- Strong residence status can protect a target while relatives in the origin country remain exposed.
- Digital-security training may reduce technical vulnerability while increasing burdens on volunteers and excluding elders, low-literacy speakers or people with disabilities.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the coercion, exposure, reporting,
host-state response and diaspora-space mechanisms. Keep the integrated
transnational-repression-to-safe-residence-and-diaspora-agency outcome at
**Tier 2** because the public record rarely follows the same target and family
from incident through protection, later participation, material security,
remedy and durable autonomy.

## Source boundary

The packet closes the definition, tactic range, exposure surfaces, reporting gaps,
host-response mechanisms and diaspora-space risks. It does not close named
target safety, family protection, residence continuity, travel, work or study,
participation, cultural continuity, data harm, remedy, perpetrator adaptation or
durable diaspora autonomy. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most transnational-repression sources. Incident inventories and policy responses
are kept separate from safe residence and lived civic agency.
