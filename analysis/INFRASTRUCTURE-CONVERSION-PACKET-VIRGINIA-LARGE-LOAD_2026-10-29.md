# Virginia large-load infrastructure conversion packet

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Theme:** infrastructure incidence and local permission  
**Subthemes:** compute as grid-governance actor; cost allocation; operational accountability; public attribution  
**Status:** source-supported mechanism candidate; not yet a household-incidence finding

## Research question

When computational large loads become physically consequential for a power
system, does governance move from general capacity expansion to identifiable
operating accountability and fair cost allocation—and can that governance be
linked to a specific customer, place and later public response?

```text
large-load growth
  -> interconnection and operating event
  -> reliability / backup / cost-allocation response
  -> named payer, customer, locality or service effect
  -> acceptance, contestation, compensation or durable rule change
```

## Stage audit

| Stage | Evidence | What is safe to conclude | What remains open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity growth | PJM and Virginia materials identify rapid computational large-load growth | Compute demand has become a grid-planning object | Exact facility-level load path and forecast error |
| Operating behavior | July 22 event caused a large unexpected load transfer to backup | Large loads can affect voltage, frequency and operator actions during a system event | Named facilities, backup runtime, fuel/emissions, who paid and whether customers noticed service effects |
| Formal governance | PJM proposes information, logging and ride-through requirements; FERC and SCC pursue tariff/cost safeguards | Operational behavior and cost allocation are now explicit governance objects | Compliance, enforcement, exemptions, actual tariff application and later rule effectiveness |
| Cost allocation | GS-5, minimum charges, collateral and direct-assignment frameworks are documented | Virginia is attempting to place more responsibility on large-load customers and reduce cross-subsidy risk | Actual project assignment, customer payment, residential bill effect and avoided cost shift |
| Public attribution | State materials and executive statements frame the issue as protection of families/small businesses and fair share; the Golden-Mars record contains substantial local participation | The distributional question is politically legible: who pays for AI-scale infrastructure and who bears transmission siting costs | Measured public opinion, locality-level attribution to a named data center, compensation or durable acceptance |
| Social/geopolitical meaning | AI infrastructure is linked to reliability, affordability and national capability | Technical operation is being translated into public legitimacy and state-capacity questions | A demonstrated link between a particular burden and later trust, policy reversal or local consent |

## Preliminary finding

**Operational accountability is moving closer to the point of dependence.**
The sequence is visible in two complementary records: a large computational
load operated in a way that produced a measurable grid event, and regulators
responded with requirements about operator information, ride-through, incident
logging, tariff design, minimum charges, collateral and direct cost assignment.

This is stronger than a generic “data centers use more electricity” claim. It
shows a transition in the governance object:

```text
data-center capacity -> facility behavior during stress
                     -> cost and reliability responsibility
                     -> customer protection and public attribution
```

It is not yet evidence that a household paid more, that a specific locality
experienced a service burden, or that public acceptance changed. The observed
event and the policy response are not the same as realized incidence.

The refreshed record adds a second conversion layer: Virginia has moved from
general cost-allocation intent toward enforceable-looking instruments—minimum
payment percentages, a minimum contract duration, direct assignment, a
consumption tax and monthly reporting. PJM’s repeated 2026 backup activations
also show that flexibility is becoming an operating practice rather than only a
planning concept. But the ledger still cannot identify the facility-level payer,
backup fuel/emissions, tax destination, residential bill counterfactual or
local service effect. These are precisely the fields needed to distinguish a
credible protection architecture from realized public value.

## Cultural and political interpretation

The packet exposes a cultural translation problem: AI infrastructure is often
presented as innovation or national capability, while rate and reliability
records turn it into a question of fairness, responsibility and who is allowed
to impose costs on others. Virginia’s public framing makes this attribution
explicit, but the framing itself is not proof that households were protected.

The appropriate cultural question is not whether the public likes data centers.
It is whether affected people can identify:

1. the facility or project causing the incremental requirement;
2. the cost or reliability channel through which they are affected;
3. the institution responsible for assigning or correcting that burden; and
4. the evidence that the assigned party actually paid or changed behavior.

## Counterexamples and boundaries

- The July 22 event did not produce an unresolvable reliability failure; system
  operators restored balance within the reported operating limits. This weakens
  any claim that large-load growth automatically creates household outages.
- A separate rate class and minimum-charge rule can reduce cross-subsidy risk,
  but can also produce higher large-customer costs without proving that all
  transmission or generation costs are correctly assigned.
- A policy announcement or commission order may improve future accountability
  while leaving earlier customers, exemptions or existing infrastructure on a
  different regime.
- Backup generation can protect grid stability while shifting emissions, fuel,
  noise or local burden elsewhere. Backup transfer is therefore not equivalent
  to no social impact.
- A state-level fairness frame can coexist with local acceptance, local
  opposition or no organized public response. Those outcomes must be measured
  separately.
- Repeated backup-generation activation can support system reliability while
  shifting fuel, emissions, noise, maintenance and compensation questions to a
  facility or host place; “the grid remained reliable” is therefore not a
  complete no-harm finding.
- A statutory tax and monthly remittance process can create public revenue
  without establishing that the revenue reaches the affected locality or
  improves a named service, bill, watershed or community decision.

## Decisive next acquisition

The next source should be a named Virginia facility or interconnection docket,
not another national forecast. Required fields:

```text
facility/customer name | docket/interconnection ID | service start/date
contract capacity and actual load | July 22 event participation if disclosed
backup runtime/fuel/emissions | direct-connect facilities
generation/transmission/distribution cost assignment | minimum-charge payment
customer class/bill effect | affected locality/service area
public comments/challenge | commission decision | later compliance or acceptance
```

### Pass condition

Promote this packet from mechanism candidate to infrastructure-incidence
finding only if one named facility or project links an operating or construction
event to a dated cost/service/environmental burden and a later payer, public
response, compensation, acceptance or rule change.

### Fail condition

If the source owner confirms that facility-level event participation or cost
allocation is confidential/unavailable, retain this as a governance and
attribution finding, explicitly record the missing fields, and do not infer
household incidence from system-level load or rate-policy language.

## Decision

This packet is promoted to a **high-priority conversion candidate**. It has
closed the technical-event and formal-governance stages, but not the material
incidence or local-permission stages. Its main contribution to the broad atlas
is the bridge between AI adoption, physical infrastructure, ratepayer fairness,
state capacity and cultural legitimacy.
