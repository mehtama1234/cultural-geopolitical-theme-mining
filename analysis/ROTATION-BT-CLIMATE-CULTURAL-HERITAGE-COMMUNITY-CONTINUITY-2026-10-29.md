# Rotation BT — Climate risk, cultural heritage and community continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** climate change / tangible and intangible heritage / disaster risk reduction / Indigenous and local knowledge / place continuity / cultural livelihoods  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 heritage-risk-to-living-continuity question

## Research question

When climate hazards, disasters, migration and environmental change threaten
cultural heritage, what converts a listing, risk assessment, adaptation plan or
reconstruction project into continued cultural practice, local authority,
livelihood, social cohesion and the right to remain or relocate with identity?

## Conversion chain

```text
hazard, environmental change, displacement or development pressure
  -> exposure of site, landscape, practice, knowledge bearer and cultural livelihood
  -> community-led assessment, preparedness, adaptation, documentation and finance
  -> protection, repair, transmission, safe practice, livelihood continuity and locally governed recovery
  -> continued identity, participation, social cohesion, dignity and place/relocation legitimacy
  -> durable cultural and climate resilience
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** heat, fire, flood, drought, sea-level rise, erosion, ecosystem
  change, conflict, migration and development pressure threaten sites, landscapes,
  cultural workers, knowledge bearers and everyday practices.
- **Mechanism:** exposure, vulnerability, maintenance, land tenure, mobility,
  climate knowledge, documentation, language, funding, insurance, tourism,
  housing, access restrictions and intergenerational transmission determine what
  can continue.
- **Operation:** a community identifies valued heritage, receives authority and
  resources, adapts or relocates safely, repairs or protects assets, transmits
  knowledge, and keeps practicing rather than merely documenting it.
- **Material consequence:** loss or preservation of homes, sacred places,
  livelihood, food systems, income, social networks, safety and the ability to
  return or remain may diverge.
- **Meaning:** a site can be valuable as memory, identity, spiritual relation,
  language, ancestry or belonging even when a market valuation is low. Moving a
  building may preserve fabric while severing its social meaning.
- **Power:** states, heritage agencies, donors, insurers, developers, tourism
  operators, conservation experts and local/Indigenous communities decide whose
  knowledge counts, what is protected, who may access it and who bears the cost.

## Bounded finding

> **A heritage listing, climate-risk assessment or adaptation project is not
> cultural continuity.** A protected-site designation, digital archive, hazard
> map, reconstruction grant, resilience scorecard, museum program or community
> consultation does not establish that a named community can continue its
> practice, transmit knowledge, retain livelihood and authority, remain safely in
> place, relocate with identity, or recover social cohesion after disruption.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Heritage is not only an object or bounded site. It includes seasonal work,
ritual, language, ecological knowledge, foodways, craft, music, religious
practice, customary governance and the social spaces in which these are learned.
Climate adaptation can therefore protect one value while damaging another: a
seawall may protect buildings but change access to a fishing landscape; a tourism
project may finance restoration while pricing residents out; digitization may
preserve a record while removing control from knowledge holders.

Geography determines whether “preservation” means in-place adaptation, managed
retreat, temporary displacement, return, or a new settlement. The relevant
ledger must follow people and practice across those routes. It must also record
who can refuse extraction or publication of sacred or sensitive knowledge, and
whether women, youth, migrants, minority groups and Indigenous authorities have
real decision power rather than symbolic representation.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A World Heritage or national listing can raise visibility without funding
  routine maintenance, household protection or living practice.
- A digital archive can preserve information while weakening community control,
  privacy, customary access and intergenerational embodied learning.
- Rebuilding a structure does not restore social networks, livelihood, ritual
  use, language or the possibility of return.
- Relocation can reduce physical hazard while creating cultural loss, land
  conflict, stigma or exclusion in the receiving place.
- Traditional knowledge may improve resilience in one ecology but cannot be
  romanticized as universally effective or treated as a substitute for safe
  infrastructure and public services.
- Tourism and climate finance can support jobs and restoration while increasing
  rent, crowding, commodification or local control loss.
- Documentation, consultation and a resilience score are process evidence, not
  proof of avoided loss or durable community continuity.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: hazard, exposure, heritage value,
community authority, adaptation, finance, operation, livelihood, practice,
transmission, relocation, identity and recovery are separate stages. Keep
heritage-risk-to-living-continuity claims in Tier 2 until a named place/cohort
ledger joins hazard, decision rights, intervention, use, livelihood, practice,
transmission and post-event continuity.

## Next test

Select one historic urban site, one Indigenous or rural cultural landscape and
one climate-displaced or relocating community. Record hazard and exposure,
heritage elements named by the community, tenure and access, decision authority,
funding, adaptation or relocation action, maintenance, practice frequency,
knowledge-bearer and youth participation, gender and minority inclusion,
livelihood, displacement/return, social cohesion, grievance and cultural
continuity over time. Compare a protected site with a non-listed community and
do not treat missing documentation as absence of cultural loss.
