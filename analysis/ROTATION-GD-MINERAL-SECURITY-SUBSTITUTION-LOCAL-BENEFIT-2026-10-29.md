# Rotation GD — mineral security, qualified substitution and local benefit

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** geopolitical, technological, geographical, industrial and community systems

## Why this rotation exists

Critical-mineral strategies are often narrated through reserves, mine
announcements, refining capacity, export controls, strategic stockpiles or
partnerships. Security is a chain rather than a geological fact: a material
must be produced, processed, qualified, transported, affordable and available
to a downstream user under disruption. For resource-rich places, development
value is another chain: investment must become local skills, procurement,
revenues, environmental protection, community consent and durable livelihoods.
Substitution and recycling can reduce dependence, but only if the alternative
works at the required performance, scale, cost, time and geography.

## Research question

When governments and firms pursue critical-mineral mining, processing,
recycling, substitution or strategic partnerships, under what conditions does
the effort produce qualified supply resilience and legitimate place-based
benefit rather than concentrated dependency, stranded infrastructure,
environmental burden, price exposure or geopolitical leverage without local
agency?

## Conversion chain

```text
resource / project / policy / export control / partnership / technology
  -> mine, refine, recycle, substitute, qualify and move material
  -> resilient input reaches a named downstream product or service
  -> industrial continuity, affordability, skills, revenue or local livelihood
  -> strategic autonomy, community legitimacy, bargaining power or cooperation
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. IEA's 2025 outlook finds that the average share of the top three refined
   suppliers of key energy minerals rose from about 82% in 2020 to 86% in
   2024, with China dominant in many refining chains and Indonesia dominant in
   nickel. Announced projects suggest only modest diversification by 2035.
2. IEA identifies copper and lithium as major supply-gap exceptions and reports
   that supply shocks could raise global average battery-pack prices by
   40–50%. A reserve or mine pipeline therefore does not equal qualified,
   affordable downstream security.
3. IEA states that substitution options are limited for many minerals and that
   emerging battery chemistries can shift rather than eliminate chokepoints;
   LFP, sodium-ion and manganese-rich pathways have their own concentrated
   inputs and downstream dependencies.
4. IEA's 2026 market overview reports refining concentration reaching record
   levels in 2025, with the top refined supplier averaging 70% share, while
   investment in critical minerals fell 9% amid volatility and policy
   uncertainty. This is a current security signal, not a forecast of a single
   inevitable outcome.
5. UNCTAD's 2025 critical-minerals analysis argues that resource-rich countries
   need productive linkages and midstream/downstream activity to capture more
   value, but face barriers to integration, diversification and sustainable
   growth. Extraction revenue alone is not local industrial benefit.
6. World Bank work on a just transition in energy-transition minerals shows
   that local procurement and community-company partnerships can improve local
   participation, while poorly planned domestic-processing requirements can
   create underused infrastructure. Local value addition must therefore be
   commercially viable, environmentally defensible and socially beneficial.

## Bounded proposition

**A mineral reserve, mine, refining plant, recycling target, export control,
strategic partnership, stockpile, substitution claim or local-content rule is
not by itself qualified supply security, industrial continuity, affordability,
environmental legitimacy, local value or geopolitical autonomy.** A project
announcement, investment, production volume, offtake agreement or processing
capacity does not establish that material meets downstream specifications,
survives disruption, reaches a user, reduces dependence, shares benefits or
protects the agency and livelihoods of affected communities.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Mineral security is experienced at multiple scales: a mine watershed, a
transport corridor, a processing town, a downstream factory and a consumer
market. A national security narrative can obscure land rights, water, cultural
heritage, worker safety, artisanal livelihoods and community consent. Local
benefit is not merely employment; it can include procurement, skills transfer,
revenue transparency, decision power, environmental repair and the ability to
refuse or renegotiate. Geopolitical diversification can also relocate burden
from one place to another. Recycling, substitution and urban mining may reduce
pressure on extraction, but their own waste, labour, technology and trade
geographies need examination.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Concentration can be economically efficient and diversification can be more
  expensive, slower or environmentally worse; security is not identical to
  maximum supplier count.
- A mine can increase national exports while producing little local income,
  skills, downstream capability or community power.
- Refining capacity is not qualified material: purity, certification, logistics,
  buyer contracts, equipment and reliability determine usability.
- Substitution can shift dependence to another mineral, processing input,
  patent, manufacturing country or infrastructure bottleneck.
- Recycling reduces primary demand in the long run but requires collection,
  product design, safe labour, economics and sufficient feedstock.
- Export controls may protect strategic leverage or domestic value while
  raising costs, provoking retaliation and harming downstream users or
  communities dependent on trade.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded**: mineral security is a multi-stage geopolitical
and industrial conversion problem whose key tests are qualified downstream
availability, disruption performance, substitution quality and place-based
benefit. Keep the named mineral-to-resilience-and-local-agency chain as **Tier
2** until project-level, community-level and downstream evidence closes the
later arrows.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | reserves, demand, project, export control, partnership or target | strong IEA, UNCTAD and World Bank evidence |
| Mechanism | concentration, processing, qualification, cost, technology, logistics and local-value policy | strong bounded evidence |
| Operation | named mine/refinery/recycler/substitute supplies a qualified downstream user | partial; pipelines and examples exist |
| Material consequence | continuity, price, affordability, emissions, jobs, revenue, water or livelihood result | not closed for a general claim |
| Meaning | consent, cultural continuity, legitimacy, trust and perceived fairness | not closed; place-specific evidence required |
| Power | bargaining leverage, autonomy, community agency, substitution and durable cooperation | source boundary; do not promote |

## Next test

Select one mineral and one corridor. Trace mine, processing, certification,
transport, offtake, downstream qualification, disruption response and end-user
continuity. In parallel, trace affected communities’ land, water, jobs,
procurement, revenue, consent, grievance and environmental outcomes. Compare a
diversification project, a recycling/substitution route and the incumbent
supply chain using the same time, cost, performance and local-benefit units.

## Source boundary

The public record closes concentration, demand, project-pipeline,
substitution, recycling, investment and local-value mechanisms. It does not
justify a universal claim that a critical-mineral strategy produces qualified
supply resilience, affordability, community legitimacy, local prosperity or
durable geopolitical autonomy. Those later outcomes remain acquisition targets.
