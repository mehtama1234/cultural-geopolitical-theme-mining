# Rotation DL — Sport, community infrastructure, youth inclusion and wellbeing

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** sport / community facilities / youth / refugees and displaced people / gender inclusion / mental health / physical activity / peacebuilding / social cohesion / civic participation / safeguarding / local institutional capacity
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 sport-investment-to-durable-inclusion-and-wellbeing question

## Research question

When governments, donors and communities invest in sport, clubs, tournaments or
recreation as a development or peacebuilding tool, how does participation become
safe belonging, health, youth voice, gender inclusion, social cohesion and local
capacity—and who remains excluded by cost, disability, gender, identity,
competition, violence or lack of facilities?

## Conversion chain

```text
youth, household, community, club, field, school, coach, health worker, refugee,
displaced person, local authority and place
  -> facility, grant, tournament, team, coach training, celebrity message,
     psychosocial program, safeguarding rule and community partnership
  -> recruitment, attendance, play, peer interaction, coaching, referral,
     conflict dialogue, leadership, health screening, complaint and local governance
  -> physical and mental wellbeing, belonging, safety, gender/equity inclusion,
     youth agency, social cohesion, civic participation and durable local capacity
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN Peacebuilding Fund youth review | Sport, arts and cultural events helped create cooperation, intergenerational dialogue and trust; examples included divided communities forming joint teams. | plausible peacebuilding mechanism and named cases | participant safety, exclusion, conflict recurrence, institutional change and durability |
| WHO Europe sport-club guidance | Sport can support physical, mental and social health, but participation does not automatically improve health; injury, exclusion, discrimination and unhealthy sponsorship can undermine benefits. | health opportunity and risk boundary | participant-level health, safety, access and long-term behavior |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** a city builds a field, a donor funds a tournament, a school starts
  a sport program, a club promotes mental health or a peace project uses teams
  to bridge divided communities.
- **Mechanism:** shared activity, physical place, peer identity, coaching,
  visibility and repeated contact become a pathway for trust, health information,
  youth leadership or conflict transformation.
- **Operation:** young people enroll, travel, play, interact across groups,
  receive coaching or screening, disclose distress, use a referral, participate
  in leadership and encounter rules, discrimination, injury or safeguarding.
- **Material consequence:** people gain safe activity, care, belonging and voice—or
  face cost, injury, harassment, exclusion, unpaid labor, competitive pressure,
  unsafe facilities or no practical route to support.
- **Meaning:** a team or field can be a shared civic space, but sport can also
  reproduce nationalism, gender hierarchy, racism, ableism, sectarian division or
  winner/loser status.
- **Power:** funders, federations, coaches, schools and local authorities control
  access, selection, safety and recognition; youth and marginalized participants
  often have limited voice in governance and complaints.

## Bounded finding

> **A sports facility, tournament, team, participation count, celebrity message,
> mental-health campaign or peacebuilding grant is not by itself youth wellbeing,
> inclusive belonging, gender equity or durable social cohesion.** Attendance,
> reach, medals, reported cohesion, training completion or a new field does not
> establish that named participants were safe, included, healthier, connected to
> support, able to influence decisions or that local institutions sustained the
> benefit.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Sport is place-making. A field, club or tournament creates recurring contact,
ritual, identity and informal governance, which can make it a powerful bridge
between displaced people, hosts, generations or divided neighborhoods. But the
same place may be inaccessible to girls, disabled youth, minorities, LGBTQ+
participants, poorer households or young people who cannot travel safely. Sport
also carries national, ethnic and gendered meanings that differ across places.
The strongest programs use sport as an entry point to health, dialogue, rights,
education and youth agency, while preserving non-sport routes for people who do
not want or cannot safely participate. Durable value is measured through repeated
belonging, safety, referral, voice and local stewardship—not through event size.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Sport can build trust, physical activity and social connection, but positive
  effects are not automatic and may depend on coaching, safety and context.
- A participant’s improved perception of cohesion is useful evidence but is not
  the same as reduced violence, durable friendship or institutional change.
- Facilities can expand access while increasing displacement, maintenance costs,
  exclusion or commercial capture in the surrounding place.
- Celebrity communication can reduce stigma while not guaranteeing service
  availability, confidentiality or treatment.
- Competitive sport and recreational/community sport have different selection,
  injury and inclusion dynamics and should not be pooled.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: investment signal, social mechanism, local
operation, material wellbeing/inclusion consequence, cultural meaning and power
are separate stages. Keep the sport-investment-to-durable-inclusion-and-wellbeing
claim in Tier 2 until a named program joins access, safety, attendance, subgroup
experience, referral, health/wellbeing, youth voice, complaint, local governance
and multi-year continuation.

## Next test

Select one named program—Cameroon’s Na We We jamboree, a refugee/host sports
program, a school/community club or Serbia’s youth mental-health initiative.
Track participant and nonparticipant outcomes: access cost and travel, safety,
attendance, gender/disability/inclusion, social ties, mental and physical health,
referral completion, civic participation, complaints, facility maintenance and
local funding. Compare the field as a social place with the event as a one-time
intervention.
