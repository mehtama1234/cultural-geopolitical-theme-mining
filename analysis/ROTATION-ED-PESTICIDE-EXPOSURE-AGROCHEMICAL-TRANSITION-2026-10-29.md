# Rotation ED — Pesticide exposure, agrochemical transition and rural health justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** agriculture / public health / labour / food systems / biodiversity

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers soil restoration, fertilizer-to-yield, food safety, ambient air,
groundwater and biodiversity finance. This rotation isolates pesticide decisions
and their multi-site exposure pathway: worker and family contact, consumer
residues, drift, water and soil contamination, pollinators, farm economics and
the social feasibility of reducing chemical dependence.

The central tension is not “chemicals bad, alternatives good.” Farmers may use
pesticides to protect yield and income under pest, climate and market pressure;
workers and nearby residents may face exposure; and transition programmes may
shift cost, labour or crop risk onto smallholders. The useful unit is the named
farm, worker, household, food item, water body, pollinator habitat and rule.

## Research question

When a government, buyer, farm, donor or community introduces pesticide
restrictions, integrated pest management, agroecology, safer products, residue
standards, protective equipment, monitoring or compensation, under what
conditions does it reduce worker, family, consumer and ecological harm without
reducing food availability, farm income or the practical ability to manage pests?

## Conversion chain

```text
pest / disease / climate / yield / market pressure
  -> product, application, training, regulation, alternative or buyer decision
  -> farm, worker, family, food, soil, water and pollinator operation
  -> dose, residue, yield, cost, labour, illness, biodiversity and income result
  -> health, food safety, livelihood, ecosystem service and place legitimacy
  -> worker voice, accountability, safer transition, remedy and durable agency
```

The unit must be a named crop, farm, worker group, household, application,
food item, water body, school or habitat, with product, dose, timing, route,
protective control, weather and outcome dates. A banned product, training session,
residue test or “integrated management” label is an intermediate signal, not
proof of safer work, food, water or livelihood.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Pesticides create linked human and ecological pathways

UNEP describes pesticides and fertilizers as pressures that can degrade soils,
harm pollinators and beneficial organisms, reduce oxygen in aquatic ecosystems,
and produce neurological, endocrine and reproductive impacts, especially for
agricultural workers. Its indicator work also notes acute and long-term human
health effects and substantial uncertainty and monitoring gaps.

The relevant system is therefore larger than the applicator: workers, nearby
residents, children, consumers, water users, soil organisms and pollinators may
experience different routes and timing of exposure.

Sources: [UNEP chemical pollution and biodiversity](https://www.unep.org/en/chemical-pollution-and-biodiversity)
and [UNEP pollution indicators](https://sdgs.unep.org/article/pollution-indicator-framework).

### 2. Occupational exposure is a control hierarchy problem

ILO agricultural safety guidance identifies pesticides as a major occupational
hazard and emphasizes elimination or substitution, engineering and administrative
controls, and PPE only as a last line of protection. This is important because
training or PPE alone may leave exposure high where products, equipment, heat,
application timing, re-entry intervals, storage and disposal remain unsafe.

The conversion test is worker-level exposure and illness, not merely whether a
training or PPE distribution occurred.

Sources: [ILO safety and health in agriculture](https://www.ilo.org/resource/other/safety-and-health-agriculture)
and [ILO occupational chemical-control guidance](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/%40protrav/%40safework/documents/normativeinstrument/wcms_161135.pdf).

### 3. Children and families can experience indirect exposure

WHO notes that workers applying pesticides and people in the immediate area can
be exposed during and after spraying, and that food and water are additional
routes. Its child-health material identifies exposure during preparation,
application, re-entry, storage, disposal and contaminated clothing; protective
equipment may be impractical or unaffordable in hot climates and among small
farmers.

The farm is also a household and care environment. A worker’s clothes, storage
container, field schedule and child accompaniment can link occupational exposure
to domestic exposure.

Sources: [WHO pesticide residues in food](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pesticide-residues-in-food)
and [WHO pesticide poisoning and children](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/childrens-environmental-health/pestpoisoning.pdf?sfvrsn=4317cd2f_1).

### 4. Food safety is a risk-assessment and trust pathway

The 2025 FAO/WHO Joint Meeting on Pesticide Residues assessed toxicology,
metabolism, residue behaviour and dietary exposure for 38 pesticides to support
Codex maximum residue levels. Such standards are important for consumers and
trade, but a maximum residue level is not evidence that every worker, nearby
resident or water user was protected, nor that testing covers informal markets
and small-scale production equally.

The evidence chain must distinguish regulatory dietary exposure from worker
dose, drift, water contamination and household handling.

Source: [FAO/WHO Pesticide Residues report 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240122963).

### 5. Reducing chemical dependence requires farmer agency and viable alternatives

FAO’s Ghana participatory agroecology case describes intercropping, companion
planting, compost, mulching, reduced tillage, flowering habitats, monitoring and
integrated pest management as a combined pathway linking biodiversity, soil
health and farmer agency. The report cautions that reducing pesticide dependence
is not simply replacing one input with another.

This supports a practical transition mechanism: farmers need knowledge, labour,
timely observation, market access, crop insurance or risk-sharing, and the
ability to adapt practices to local ecology. A rule without alternatives can
create noncompliance, crop loss or hidden substitution.

Source: [FAO participatory agroecological practices in Ghana](https://www.fao.org/agroecology/new-knowledge-module/education-and-learning-tools/detail/1681530/en).

### 6. Biodiversity goals have implementation and livelihood trade-offs

UNEP reports that the global biodiversity framework calls for reducing pollution
risk, including pesticide and highly hazardous chemical risk, but also identifies
weak enforcement, finance and monitoring, and the need to manage food-security
and livelihood implications fairly. The 2026 global review is a progress signal,
not proof that targets have been achieved.

The conversion question is whether a named farm or landscape reduces exposure
and protects biodiversity while maintaining viable food production and worker
agency—not whether a national target is reported.

## Bounded proposition 135

**A pesticide restriction, residue standard, integrated-pest-management plan,
agroecology programme, safer product, training course, protective-equipment
distribution, farm certification, pollinator initiative or food-safety test is
not by itself safer work, healthy families, compliant food, clean water,
biodiversity recovery, stable farm income or a just agrochemical transition.**
Bans, sales declines, training attendance, PPE distribution, residue compliance,
certification, reduced application, pollinator counts or alternative-practice
adoption do not establish that named workers and households received lower dose,
fewer symptoms, safe re-entry and storage, protected income, affordable food,
clean water, restored ecosystem function, equal enforcement or remedy.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **The farm is a work-and-care system.** Application schedules, clothing,
   storage and field access connect workplace, home, school and food.
2. **Agrochemical risk is geographically mobile.** Drift, runoff, residues and
   trade move exposure beyond the plot and across administrative boundaries.
3. **Farmer agency is part of safety.** A practice that cannot be afforded,
   observed, repaired or adapted locally is not a durable transition.
4. **Food safety is relational trust.** Producers, traders, regulators and
   consumers may experience different benefits and burdens from the same rule.
5. **Biodiversity has livelihood meaning.** Pollinators, soil organisms, fish,
   water and seed diversity are both ecological assets and cultural knowledge.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A pesticide ban can reduce exposure while increasing crop loss, food prices or
  illegal substitution if viable alternatives are not available.
- PPE and training can reduce risk where controls are practical, but PPE alone
  is weak protection in heat, poverty, poor storage or repeated exposure.
- Lower pesticide use can improve biodiversity while increasing labour demands or
  reducing income for farmers without market or risk support.
- Residue compliance protects a measured dietary pathway but does not prove safe
  worker dose, drift, groundwater quality or informal-market coverage.
- Certification can reward safer production while excluding smallholders through
  fees, paperwork, digital systems or buyer concentration.
- Participatory agroecology can strengthen farmer agency, but one small case
  cannot establish yield or transition effects across crops and regions.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, FAO, UNEP
and ILO sources support the worker, family, food, biodiversity and transition
mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because public sources
rarely follow the same farm and households through dose, illness, food safety,
water, income, biodiversity, enforcement and remedy.

## Next test

Compare one integrated-pest-management or agroecology project, one regulated
conventional farm system, and one smallholder or informal production zone. Track
workers, nearby households, children, food samples, water, pollinator habitat and
farm economics across at least two crop cycles, including a pest or weather shock.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the pesticide-health, occupational-control, family,
food-safety, biodiversity and transition mechanisms. It does not close named
dose, illness, food and water safety, farm income, ecosystem recovery,
enforcement or remedy outcomes. Those remain acquisition targets.
