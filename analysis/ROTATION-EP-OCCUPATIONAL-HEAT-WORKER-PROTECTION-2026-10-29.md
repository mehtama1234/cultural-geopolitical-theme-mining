# Rotation EP — Occupational heat stress, worker protection and livelihood continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** climate / labour / occupational health / informality / migration / gender / productivity / social protection

## Why this rotation

The atlas includes extreme heat, cooling, indoor environment, agriculture,
construction, waste work, care work, just transition and worker control. This
rotation isolates heat as a workplace power and livelihood problem: whether
workers can slow, rest, hydrate, adapt schedules, refuse unsafe work, obtain
care and compensation, and retain income when heat makes normal production
dangerous.

Heat protection is often represented by a temperature threshold, advisory,
cooling intervention, productivity estimate, workplace plan or personal
protective equipment. Those signals can coexist with piece-rate pressure,
informal employment, migrant status, gendered clothing or care work, no paid
rest, weak insurance, inaccessible health services or income loss when a worker
follows safety guidance. The unit is a named worker’s exposure, decision and
livelihood route, not a regional temperature average.

## Research question

When governments, employers, unions, cities, occupational-health systems or
climate programmes introduce heat alerts, work-rest rules, water and shade,
cooling, schedule changes, acclimatization, protective equipment, insurance,
formalization or social protection, under what conditions does the intervention
reduce illness and injury while preserving pay, dignity, bargaining power,
productivity, family security and the ability to refuse unsafe work—especially
for outdoor, indoor-hot, informal, migrant, agricultural, construction, waste,
transport, care and public-service workers?

## Conversion chain

```text
heat / humidity / radiant load + job task + worker vulnerability + income pressure
  -> heat-risk standard, forecast, schedule, rest, water, shade, PPE and protection policy
  -> workplace exposure, task pace, supervision, reporting and medical encounter
  -> safe recovery, fewer injuries, usable rest and protected wage/benefit
  -> health, income, family security, productivity, retention and livelihood continuity
  -> worker voice, refusal, enforcement, compensation and climate-labour legitimacy
```

The unit must be a named worker, job, site, shift, employer, contract,
temperature episode or occupational-health claim with dates, heat conditions,
task, pace, rest, water, pay, incident, treatment, protection and remedy fields.
Heat index, advisory issuance, employer policy, cooling equipment or lost-hours
estimate is an intermediate signal—not proof of safe work or livelihood
continuity.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Heat is already a global occupational hazard

WHO and WMO’s 2025 report and guidance state that more than 2.4 billion workers
are exposed to excessive heat globally and that excessive heat results in more
than 22.85 million occupational injuries each year. It identifies agriculture,
construction and fisheries among heavily exposed sectors, while stressing risks
for indoor and outdoor workers, productivity loss, illness and long-term health.

The policy implication is that heat action must be tailored to job, region and
worker vulnerability, and co-created with workers, unions, employers and public
health experts. A warning alone does not alter bargaining power or pay.

Sources: [WHO/WMO workplace heat-stress report and guidance](https://www.who.int/news/item/22-08-2025-who-wmo-issue-new-report-and-guidance-to-protect-workers-from-increasing-heat-stress)
and [WHO climate change and workplace heat stress](https://www.who.int/publications/b/75307).

### 2. Health harm is acute, chronic and unequal

ILO’s global review describes immediate heat exhaustion, heatstroke and death,
along with longer-term cardiovascular, respiratory and kidney disease. WHO
identifies accidents, mental-health impacts and vulnerability among outdoor and
manual workers, while noting that gender, housing and poverty shape exposure.

The conversion test must capture symptoms, treatment, lost work, chronic
effects, household care and compensation—not only daily temperature or
productivity.

Sources: [ILO Heat at Work](https://www.ilo.org/publications/heat-work-implications-safety-and-health)
and [WHO heat and health](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health).

### 3. Informality converts heat into income risk

World Bank urban-heat evidence shows that informal workers are especially
exposed and less able to adjust hours because they lack social protection and
savings. Vendors, rickshaw drivers, domestic workers, garment workers,
construction workers and waste pickers can face a direct tradeoff: keep working
in dangerous heat or lose the income needed for food, rent and care.

This closes the livelihood mechanism but not the named worker outcome. The
research must follow pay, schedule, ability to stop, illness, health costs and
household coping when a heat plan is used.

Source: [World Bank Urban Heat Management Handbook](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099090225111530574/pdf/P170027-8734c3d2-9b13-4cd7-9ea9-7cea8dab1706.pdf).

### 4. Heat exposure can be linked to occupational mortality while protection remains incomplete

An ILO 2026 study in Colombia combines mortality, meteorological and labour data
and finds elevated temperatures associated with increased worker mortality,
particularly in agriculture, assembly, health, teaching, construction,
manufacturing, mining and transport. It reports that general health coverage
was much broader than occupational-risk coverage, leaving many informal workers
without adequate employment-related protection.

This is a valuable named-country outcome bridge, but it does not prove that any
particular heat intervention prevented deaths. It supports the need to match
heat, job, worker status, exposure, outcome and protection records.

Source: [ILO Colombia heat-stress and occupational-mortality study](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/high-temperatures-linked-increased-mortality-among-workers-ilo).

### 5. Productivity loss is a material consequence, not a substitute for rights

WHO/WMO report that productivity drops by roughly 2–3% for each degree above
20°C. World Bank evidence from Bangladesh estimates substantial lost workdays,
illness and economic costs from extreme heat, with disproportionate effects on
working-age people, women, children and poorer households.

Productivity metrics can help expose economic stakes, but employers may respond
by intensifying pace, shifting risk to contractors, cutting hours or penalizing
rest. The worker-centred test must distinguish output from safe and fairly paid
work.

Sources: [WHO/WMO workplace guidance](https://www.who.int/news/item/22-08-2025-who-wmo-issue-new-report-and-guidance-to-protect-workers-from-increasing-heat-stress)
and [World Bank Bangladesh heat and work evidence](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/endpovertyinsouthasia/when-heat-drains-growth--the-human-cost-of-rising-temperatures-i).

### 6. Effective heat action requires worker voice and enforceable implementation

WHO/WMO guidance calls for heat-health policies tailored to local weather and
jobs, worker and union participation, training, acclimatization, water, rest,
cool areas and emergency procedures. WHO’s occupational-health guidance also
recommends scheduling heavy work at cooler times, hydration monitoring and
reporting symptoms.

The implementation question is power: can the worker refuse, report, slow down,
receive paid rest, obtain treatment, keep the job, and secure compensation
without retaliation? A written plan or sensor cannot answer those questions.

Sources: [WHO occupational ambient-work guidance](https://www.who.int/tools/occupational-hazards-in-health-sector/risks-in-ambient-work-environment)
and [WHO/WMO occupational heat guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/b/75307).

## Bounded proposition 147

**A heat warning, occupational heat standard, employer heat plan, schedule
change, water and shade provision, cooling investment, acclimatization protocol,
protective equipment, productivity intervention, insurance scheme or worker
formalization programme is not by itself safe work, health protection, decent
work, income continuity, productivity, climate resilience or labour justice.**
Temperature thresholds, alerts, cooling equipment, rest rules, training,
reported incidents, lost-hours estimates, insurance coverage or policy adoption
do not establish that named workers could slow or refuse unsafe work, received
paid usable rest and care, avoided acute or chronic harm, retained income and
employment, or obtained voice, compensation and remedy.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Heat is a labour bargain.** The ability to rest or stop depends on contract,
   wage system, immigration status, union power, gender, caste, class and
   household dependence.
2. **Exposure follows task and place.** A shaded city office, hot factory,
   rooftop, farm, fishery, waste route, kitchen or care facility has different
   heat and control conditions.
3. **Informality makes adaptation expensive.** Rest can mean lost pay, a missed
   market window, a lost shift or inability to buy food and medicine.
4. **Migration links climate and labour geographies.** A worker may face heat,
   recruitment debt, tied housing, language barriers and limited ability to
   report unsafe conditions.
5. **Heat protection is collective infrastructure.** Forecasts, clinics, water,
   transport, shade, social protection, inspection and compensation determine
   whether a worker can act on an alert.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A heat plan can reduce injury in a formal workplace while subcontractors,
  informal workers or unpaid family workers remain outside its coverage.
- Paid rest can protect health while piece-rate pay, quotas or deadlines make
  workers afraid to use it.
- Cooling equipment can lower indoor temperature while energy cost, maintenance,
  noise, ventilation or chemical exposure creates another hazard.
- A productivity decline can reflect safer pacing rather than worker failure;
  do not treat output alone as the target.
- Heat illness may be misdiagnosed, unreported or recorded as absence, cardiac
  disease or general injury, weakening apparent intervention effects.
- A forecast can be accurate while workers lack water, shade, transport, social
  protection, healthcare or bargaining power to respond.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, WMO, ILO
and World Bank evidence supports the exposure, health, informality, mortality,
productivity and worker-voice mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same worker through heat
exposure, employer response, illness, pay, employment, family security and
remedy.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the exposure, health, informality, mortality,
productivity and worker-voice mechanisms. It does not close named intervention
effects, paid rest, illness, chronic harm, income/employment continuity,
household security, retaliation, insurance, compensation or remedy outcomes.
Those remain acquisition targets.
