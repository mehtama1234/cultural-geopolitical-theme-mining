# Rotation EQ — Antimicrobial resistance, equitable access and One Health stewardship

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** AMR / access to medicines / diagnostics / stewardship / One Health / health systems / agrifood / environment / geopolitics

## Why this rotation

The atlas has an earlier antimicrobial-resistance signal packet. This rotation
refreshes and narrows it around the central policy tension: people need timely,
quality-assured antimicrobials and diagnostics, while unnecessary or poorly
targeted use accelerates resistance across human, animal, plant and environmental
systems.

AMR is often represented by resistance prevalence, antibiotic consumption,
national action plans, surveillance coverage, stewardship guidance or a global
target. Those signals can coexist with no medicine, no diagnostic, counterfeit
or poor-quality products, unaffordable care, informal sale, delayed treatment,
over-prescribing, agricultural dependence, polluted water, weak infection
prevention, or a community that bears resistance risk without access to care.
The unit is a named infection, patient, prescriber, farm, facility, pathogen,
medicine and place over time.

## Research question

When governments, health systems, clinicians, pharmacies, laboratories,
agricultural producers, food companies, communities or international funders
introduce AMR surveillance, diagnostics, infection prevention, vaccine,
stewardship, access, procurement, agrifood controls or environmental measures,
under what conditions does the intervention produce timely effective treatment
and lower resistance without shifting cost, illness, livelihood risk or
geopolitical dependence to patients, health workers, farmers, animals,
consumers or downstream communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
infection / treatment need / animal or crop disease / medicine supply / pollution
  -> diagnostics, access, prescribing, stewardship, IPC, agrifood and environmental policy
  -> clinic, pharmacy, farm, food chain, hospital, laboratory or community encounter
  -> correct treatment, prevention, adherence, monitoring and safe disposal
  -> recovery, survival, livelihood, health-system trust and lower resistance
  -> local capability, equitable burden sharing, accountability and global health security
```

The unit must be a named patient, pathogen, infection, prescriber, facility,
pharmacy, medicine, farm, animal group, food item, water body or procurement
episode with dates, diagnosis, test, treatment, price, availability, outcome,
use, resistance, exposure and remedy fields. Antibiotic volume, surveillance
coverage, action-plan adoption or a target is an intermediate signal—not proof
of correct access, recovery or resistance reduction.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Resistance is now measurable across common infections and treatment routes

WHO’s 2025 global surveillance report analyzes more than 23 million
bacteriologically confirmed cases reported by over 100 countries and tracks
resistance trends across urinary, bloodstream, gastrointestinal and gonorrhoea
infections. WHO reports that one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections
causing common infections in 2023 was resistant, and resistance rose in over 40%
of monitored pathogen-antibiotic combinations between 2018 and 2023.

This closes the signal and surveillance mechanism. It does not establish that a
named patient received a diagnostic, effective treatment, follow-up or remedy.

Sources: [WHO Global antibiotic resistance surveillance report 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09585)
and [WHO 2025 surveillance release](https://www.who.int/news/item/13-10-2025-who-warns-of-widespread-resistance-to-common-antibiotics-worldwide).

### 2. Access and stewardship must be measured together

WHO’s 2025 AWaRe analysis reports that only one in three reporting jurisdictions
met the target that at least 70% of human antibiotic use be from the Access group.
It also observes large differences in use and warns that low reported use can
reflect lack of access rather than good stewardship. Access antibiotics are
generally lower cost and narrower spectrum, while Watch and Reserve categories
require more careful use.

The conversion test must therefore distinguish no treatment, appropriate Access
treatment, unnecessary treatment, wrong drug, delayed treatment and resistant
failure. A lower volume is not automatically success.

Sources: [WHO antibiotics-use analysis](https://www.who.int/news/item/29-04-2025-antibiotics-most-responsible-for-drug-resistance-are-overused---who-report)
and [WHO AWaRe 2025 classification](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09489).

### 3. Global targets depend on local health-system capacity

The 2024 UN political declaration commits to a 10% reduction in bacterial-AMR-
associated deaths by 2030 against the 2019 baseline, a 70% Access share target,
stronger surveillance, infection prevention, diagnostics, sustainable financing
and multisectoral action. It reports that many countries have national action
plans while fewer have functioning multisectoral coordination or implementation
capacity.

This is a geopolitical and institutional conversion problem: targets, plans and
financing do not close laboratory, pharmacy, workforce, procurement, primary
care, water, sanitation, vaccine or enforcement capacity in a named place.

Source: [UN General Assembly AMR political declaration](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4064023?v=%5B%27pdf%27%5D).

### 4. One Health moves the unit beyond the clinic

WHO’s current AMR framing and Global Action Plan connect human, animal and plant
infections with antimicrobial use and environmental pollution. The Quadripartite
approach joins WHO, FAO, UNEP and WOAH and seeks equitable access, appropriate
use, infection prevention and reduced environmental contamination.

The practical test must follow a pathway from farm, hospital, pharmacy, food,
wastewater or manufacturing discharge to people, animals, ecosystems and local
livelihoods. One Health is not a reason to pool unlike outcomes into a single
index; the units and causal clocks must remain separate.

Source: [WHO antimicrobial resistance fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/antimicrobial-resistance).

### 5. Prevention can improve access while reducing pressure on antibiotics

The UN declaration emphasizes universal health coverage, infection prevention,
vaccination, WASH, diagnostics and access to essential antimicrobials. Uganda’s
2024/25–2028/29 national plan illustrates a country-level One Health approach
that combines awareness, infection prevention, stewardship, surveillance,
research and innovation.

These are plausible system mechanisms, but a plan or facility intervention does
not prove improved patient survival, reduced inappropriate use, farm continuity,
or lower resistance without named follow-up.

Sources: [Uganda national AMR action plan](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/uganda-second-antimicrobial-resistance-national-action-plan-2024-25-2028-29)
and [UN AMR declaration](https://mandates.un.org/mandate/A/RES/79/2?subject=Disease+Prevention).

### 6. Surveillance is also a legitimacy and equity system

WHO’s GLASS dashboard reports substantial gaps in national use data and
participation, especially across non-European and lower-income settings. Data
quality determines whose infections are visible, which medicines are procured,
which prescribers are scrutinized and where resources flow.

The evidence audit must therefore ask who is missing from surveillance, whether
data are actionable at facility level, whether communities can see and contest
decisions, and whether stewardship rules impose costs without ensuring access.

Source: [WHO updated GLASS dashboard](https://www.who.int/news/item/25-09-2025-updated-who-dashboard-offers-new-insights-on-antimicrobial-resistance-and-use).

## Bounded proposition 148

**An AMR surveillance system, antibiotic-use target, national action plan,
stewardship programme, diagnostic, infection-prevention intervention, vaccine,
procurement reform, agrifood restriction, wastewater control or One Health
strategy is not by itself effective treatment, equitable access, recovery,
survival, livelihood protection, lower resistance, public trust or global health
security.** Resistance prevalence, antibiotic volume, Access-share targets,
countries reporting, action plans, tests performed, medicines procured,
prescribing rules or environmental monitoring do not establish that named
patients received timely quality-assured treatment, that unnecessary use fell
without denying care, that farmers and animals remained protected, or that
resistance and downstream harms declined with accountability and remedy.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Access and restraint have to coexist.** A community asked to reduce use
   without reliable diagnostics and affordable effective treatment may hear
   stewardship as rationing or abandonment.
2. **Antibiotics move through social trust.** Families, pharmacists, clinicians,
   informal sellers, farmers and patients interpret illness and treatment through
   local knowledge, cost, time and prior experience.
3. **One Health is geographically connected.** A hospital, farm, factory,
   wastewater channel and downstream settlement can share resistance pathways
   while holding unequal power and protection.
4. **Data can redistribute resources.** Surveillance makes some pathogens,
   facilities and populations visible while leaving others uncounted.
5. **Global targets require local sovereignty.** Imported medicines, diagnostics,
   laboratories, financing and standards can build capacity or deepen dependence.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Lower antibiotic consumption can reflect improved prescribing or untreated
  infection and weak access; the outcome cannot be inferred from volume alone.
- A diagnostic can reduce unnecessary prescriptions while being too expensive,
  unavailable at night, unreliable or disconnected from a treatment supply.
- A national action plan can exist without financing, coordination, laboratories,
  trained staff, procurement continuity or enforcement.
- Restricting veterinary or agricultural use can protect public health while
  shifting disease and income risk to small producers without vaccines or support.
- High surveillance coverage can miss informal pharmacies, rural clinics,
  private providers, animals, wastewater or cross-border movement.
- A new antibiotic can improve treatment while access, affordability, stewardship
  and resistance selection remain unresolved.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, UN and
country-level evidence supports the surveillance, access, stewardship, One
Health, financing and legitimacy mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same patient, prescriber,
facility, farm or downstream community through treatment, recovery, use,
resistance and remedy.

## Next test

Follow one named infection from symptom, diagnostic and prescription through
treatment, recovery or resistant failure; one facility’s stewardship and supply
route; and one agrifood or wastewater pathway. Compare an urban and rural site,
private and public care, and a high- and low-surveillance setting while recording
prices, stock-outs, informal purchase, trust, treatment completion and later
resistance.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the surveillance, access, stewardship, One Health,
financing, prevention and legitimacy mechanisms. It does not close named
treatment, recovery, survival, appropriate-use change, farm/animal protection,
environmental exposure, supply continuity, affordability or remedy outcomes.
Those remain acquisition targets.
