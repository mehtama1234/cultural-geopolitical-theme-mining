# Rotation EA — Soil health, land restoration and rural livelihood justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** food systems / geography / climate / land tenure / rural culture

## Why this rotation

The atlas contains fertilizer-to-yield, Indigenous food sovereignty, climate
adaptation and small-scale farming themes. This rotation isolates the soil and
land-restoration layer: degradation, erosion, organic matter, land-use change,
regenerative practice, restoration finance, carbon claims and land tenure.

The key question is whether “healthy soil,” “land degradation neutrality,”
regenerative agriculture or restoration finance becomes a lived improvement for
named farmers, pastoralists, tenants, women, customary land users and future
generations—or whether the metric is satisfied through offsets, land
consolidation, new reporting burdens or projects that weaken local control.

## Research question

When a state, donor, buyer, carbon market, conservation body or agribusiness
introduces soil-health standards, restoration, payments, carbon projects,
regenerative practices, land-use planning or drought adaptation, under what
conditions does it improve soil function, food production, income, resilience
and cultural continuity while protecting tenure and avoiding land appropriation?

## Conversion chain

```text
erosion / nutrient loss / drought / land pressure / insecure tenure
  -> land-use rule, practice, incentive, restoration or carbon intervention
  -> farm, pasture, watershed, household and market operation
  -> soil function, yield, input cost, labour, income, food and water result
  -> resilience, nutrition, livelihood, biodiversity and place continuity
  -> tenure security, local authority, intergenerational investment and remedy
```

The unit must be a named farm, plot, pastoral route, watershed, land user,
household, cooperative, project or community, with baseline, time horizon,
comparison and land-rights context. Hectares restored, carbon tonnes, training
attendance, practice adoption or yield on demonstration plots are intermediate
signals, not proof of household security or durable soil recovery.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Land degradation is a production and livelihood problem

FAO’s State of Food and Agriculture 2025 reports that land degradation affects
crop yields and producers across landholding scales, and that policy must be
tailored to different farm sizes and conditions. FAO’s land-and-water assessment
describes land, soil and water as foundations of agricultural production and
food security, while noting that more than 60% of human-induced land degradation
occurs on agricultural lands.

This closes the broad production and resource-pressure mechanism. It does not
close whether a named farmer can adopt a practice, afford the transition, retain
the benefit or remain on the land long enough to realize it.

Sources: [FAO State of Food and Agriculture 2025](https://www.fao.org/agrifood-economics/publications/detail/en/c/1749667/)
and [FAO land and water resources assessment](https://www.fao.org/publications/fao-flagship-publications/the-state-of-the-worlds-land-and-water-resources-for-food-and-agriculture/).

### 2. Land degradation neutrality has a rights and distribution condition

UNCCD principles say that land-degradation-neutrality action should protect
human rights and wellbeing, support livelihoods, preserve productive capacity
and cultural value, and avoid burden-shifting to other regions or future
generations. They call for local validation, participation, gender sensitivity,
traditional and scientific knowledge, and responsible tenure governance.

This is a governance mechanism, not evidence that a national neutrality target
has protected local land users. “No net loss” can hide who loses access, who
receives restoration finance, and whether gains occur in the same land type,
community and time horizon as the losses.

Sources: [UNCCD land-degradation-neutrality principles](https://www.unccd.int/land-and-life/land-degradation-neutrality/ldn-principles)
and [UNCCD scientific framework](https://www.unccd.int/resources/unccd-library/land-balance-scientific-conceptual-framework-land-degradation-neutrality-0).

### 3. Tenure is an enabling condition, but not a sufficient outcome

UNCCD and FAO evidence links secure and legitimate tenure with longer-term
investment in sustainable land management and restoration. It also cautions
that formal tenure is not the only legitimate form: informal and customary
rights matter, and restoration projects can fail when they rely on formal
registration that excludes actual land users. The evidence remains cautious
about causal direction; investment can also increase perceived tenure security.

The conversion test is therefore not “titles issued.” It is whether named users
can cultivate, graze, conserve, inherit, transfer, refuse or renegotiate land
use, and receive benefits without fear of dispossession.

Sources: [UNCCD tenure and restoration brief](https://www.unccd.int/resources/brief/securing-tenure-restore-land-and-safeguard-livelihoods)
and [UNCCD tenure working paper](https://www.unccd.int/sites/default/files/2022-03/UNCCD%20GLO%20WP%20tenure.pdf).

### 4. Restoration is scaling, but monitoring and local durability lag

The UNEP/FAO/CBD Restoration Progress Report records restoration activity across
many countries and identifies food security, water security, climate resilience,
jobs and wellbeing as potential benefits. It also reports uneven progress,
weakness in freshwater, marine and coastal restoration, and the need for much
more finance. The report emphasizes people, secure tenure and Indigenous
leadership as conditions for lasting results.

The headline hectares and jobs are discovery signals. They do not establish
that restored land remained functional, that jobs were decent and local, or that
communities retained authority after the project and finance cycle ended.

Source: [UNEP Restoration Progress Report](https://www.unep.org/en/publications/report/restoration-progress-report-natures-comeback-scaling-ecosystem-restoration).

### 5. Practice adoption is constrained by scale, risk and cash flow

World Bank evidence from Ethiopia describes soil-health practices, training,
farmer groups, enterprise development, irrigation, animal-health services and
inputs as part of a broader resilience programme. This illustrates an important
mechanism: soil practice cannot be isolated from labour, credit, equipment,
market access, water and extension capability.

A demonstration plot or training attendance is not the same as adoption at
scale. Farmers may rationally reject a practice if returns arrive after several
seasons, labour rises, tenure is insecure, yields are volatile, or buyers do not
pay for the claimed quality or carbon attribute.

Source: [World Bank Ethiopia soil and farming livelihoods case](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2025/08/19/in-ethiopia-reviving-soil-and-improving-farming-livelihoods).

### 6. Carbon and restoration finance introduce additional power questions

UNCCD’s restoration and land-neutrality materials identify finance as necessary,
but also stress local validation, rights, monitoring and avoidance of land
appropriation. The risk is not that all carbon or restoration finance is harmful;
it is that a project may count carbon or hectares before it verifies land-user
consent, benefit distribution, additionality, permanence, food effects and the
ability to exit or challenge the project.

The atlas therefore treats carbon tonnes and hectares as separate from soil
function, farm income, food security and local authority.

## Bounded proposition 132

**A soil-health standard, regenerative-agriculture programme, land-degradation-
neutrality target, restoration project, carbon-credit scheme, drought-adaptation
package, conservation payment, land-use plan or sustainable-practice training
is not by itself restored soil, higher farm resilience, food security, rural
income, tenure security or intergenerational place continuity.** Hectares
restored, carbon tonnes issued, practice adoption, training attendance, titles
registered, yields on demonstration plots or finance committed do not establish
that named land users experienced durable soil-function improvement, affordable
transition costs, stable yield and income, improved nutrition, protected rights,
fair benefit sharing, cultural continuity or remedy when the project changed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Soil is a slow clock.** A project can show early adoption or vegetation
   before soil function, income, nutrition and resilience are observable.
2. **Land is livelihood and identity.** Plot, pasture, seed, water, burial,
   ceremony, inheritance and seasonal movement may be inseparable.
3. **Restoration can redistribute authority.** Mapping, registration, carbon
   accounting and monitoring can make land legible to investors while making
   customary users less visible.
4. **Scale changes feasibility.** A practice that works on a demonstration farm
   may fail for smallholders because of labour, cash, machinery, risk or market
   constraints.
5. **Intergenerational benefit requires present consent.** Future ecological
   gains cannot justify present dispossession or unremedied livelihood loss.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Carbon or restoration revenue can improve local income when rights, consent,
  monitoring and payment continuity are real; it can also create land pressure
  or exclude undocumented users.
- A soil practice can improve organic matter while reducing short-term yield or
  raising labour and input costs during the transition.
- Land titles can support investment but can also accelerate sale, consolidation
  or exclusion of tenants, women and customary users.
- Restoration can create green jobs while the jobs are temporary, unsafe,
  externally staffed or disconnected from long-term land stewardship.
- “No net loss” at a national scale can permit local loss, ecological mismatch or
  time-shifted compensation.
- Farmer groups and local knowledge can make adoption durable, but participation
  without control over finance, land or market terms is not power.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. FAO, UNCCD, UNEP
and World Bank sources support the soil-production, restoration, tenure, finance
and adoption mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because the
public record rarely follows the same land users through soil function, costs,
yield, income, nutrition, tenure, cultural continuity and post-project power.

## Next test

Compare one farmer-led restoration project, one carbon or conservation-finance
project, and one public sustainable-land-management programme. Follow named
plots and users for at least three seasons, including tenants, women, customary
users and households that do not adopt the promoted practice.

| Stage | Minimum fields |

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the soil-production, restoration, tenure, finance and
adoption mechanisms. It does not close named soil-function recovery linked to
farm income, food security, tenure, cultural continuity, benefit sharing or
post-project bargaining power. Those remain acquisition targets.
