# Rotation EJ — Ageing, disability, care infrastructure and caregiver resilience

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** ageing / disability / long-term care / unpaid care / labour / migration / technology / place

## Why this rotation

The atlas already covers childcare continuity, home- and community-based services,
care-policy-to-worker resilience and some health-service access. This rotation
isolates the wider care infrastructure required when an older person, disabled
person or person with a chronic condition needs support with daily life over
time. It follows the relationship between the person receiving care, unpaid
family and community caregivers, paid care workers, providers, public finance,
housing, transport, respite, assistive technology and cultural expectations.

Care is often treated as a private household obligation or a labour-supply
variable. That framing can hide who gives up paid work, who migrates to provide
care, who is institutionalized, who can age in place, whose preferences are
heard, and whether a worker can sustain a decent livelihood. The unit is a
usable, person-centred care arrangement in a real place, not a budget, bed,
registration or workforce headcount.

## Research question

When a government, employer, insurer, health system, municipality, family,
community organization or technology provider expands long-term care, home care,
community support, respite, caregiver benefits, accessible housing, transport,
assistive technology or care-worker formalization, under what conditions does it
produce autonomy, safety, continuity and belonging for the person receiving care
while preserving health, income, voice and social participation for unpaid and
paid caregivers?

## Conversion chain

```text
ageing / disability / chronic condition / care need / family obligation
  -> finance, eligibility, housing, transport, respite, workforce and technology policy
  -> home, neighbourhood, facility, workplace and cross-border care encounter
  -> reliable help, autonomy, safety, time, income, health and preference recognition
  -> ageing in place, participation, family resilience, decent work and belonging
  -> voice, co-production, portability, correction, accountability and remedy
```

The unit must be a named person, household, caregiver, worker, service,
provider, neighbourhood or facility with dates and geography. A care budget,
benefit entitlement, worker count, training completion, facility bed, digital
tool or policy announcement is an intermediate signal—not proof of daily
support, autonomy, caregiver relief, worker retention or non-institutionalized
life.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Long-term care is a daily-life and place system

WHO describes long-term care as services that help people live independently and
safely when they can no longer perform routine activities alone. Its 2025
ageing-population review notes rising demand, shrinking informal-care supply,
the need for alternatives to acute-care institutions, and the continuing role of
households and communities alongside formal systems. Different financing and
formalization choices reflect economic conditions, political systems and
cultures.

The research implication is that a national entitlement or service capacity
must be followed into the home and community: who arrives, when, for which task,
with what continuity, and with whose preferences and authority.

Source: [WHO long-term care in ageing populations](https://wkc.who.int/resources/publications/m/item/long-term-care-in-ageing-populations).

### 2. Community-based support is an alternative to institutionalization, not merely a cheaper delivery mode

WHO Europe’s 2025 framework for community-based care and support for older
people and people with disabilities calls for diverse, accessible and inclusive
services, identifies professional roles and enabling conditions, and links
community support to preventing institutionalization. It treats care as a
rights-based system that must be mapped across health and social support rather
than as a single provider.

This closes the mechanism that place matters: housing, neighbourhood networks,
transport, home support and community services determine whether a person can
retain everyday life in a chosen place. It does not close whether a particular
person actually received reliable, preferred and culturally safe support.

Source: [WHO mapping community-based care and support](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/WHO-EURO-2025-12081-51853-79478).

### 3. Unpaid care is a labour-market, gender and household-security mechanism

ILO estimates that 748 million people were outside the global labour force in
2023 because of care responsibilities, including 708 million women. The ILO
links the gap to the need to recognize, reduce and redistribute unpaid care,
while improving conditions for paid care workers. Its 2024 care-economy
resolution and 2025 social-development brief make care policy part of decent
work, social protection and gender equality rather than a private-family issue.

The conversion test must follow the same caregiver’s time, paid-work status,
income, health, social participation and later labour-market path. A benefit
announcement or labour-force statistic cannot show that respite, leave or
services actually released time or reduced care overload.

Sources: [ILO unpaid-care labour-force evidence](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/unpaid-care-work-prevents-708-million-women-participating-labour-market)
and [ILO care-economy brief](https://www.ilo.org/publications/advancing-decent-work-and-care-economy-essential-component-social).

### 4. Informal caregivers are an economic and health infrastructure with weak visibility

WHO’s 2024 financing brief notes that informal caregivers support daily
activities such as bathing, toileting, dressing, mobility, eating and
continence, and that the estimated value of informal care ranges from 0.8% to
4.9% of GDP across countries. In lower-income settings, families and friends
often substitute for scarce publicly funded services.

This creates a material-risk distinction: shifting care from public provision to
households may preserve a service statistic while transferring time, injury,
mental-health, income and migration burdens to a caregiver. The value estimate
does not itself establish who benefits or whether care is safe and chosen.

Source: [WHO financing and support for informal long-term caregivers](https://wkc.who.int/resources/publications/i/item/long-term-care-financing-lessons-for-low--and-middle-income-settings-supporting-informal-long-term-caregivers).

### 5. Formal care supply can grow while care work remains unstable and culturally stratified

OECD reports that demand for long-term-care workers is rising while supply has
stagnated. It identifies low wages, physical and mental risks, non-standard
contracts and low recognition as recruitment and retention problems. Its 2025
health-at-a-glance evidence reports that, across 26 European OECD countries,
the foreign-born share of the LTC workforce rose from 14% in 2014 to 21% in
2024, while part-time and fixed-term work remain common.

The care chain therefore includes migration and labour rights: a shortage in one
place can be managed through migrant recruitment, while the sending place loses
care capacity or the worker bears insecure status. Worker headcount or training
completion is not proof of continuity, decent work, safe staffing or recipient
trust.

Sources: [OECD ageing and long-term care](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ageing-and-long-term-care.html)
and [OECD Health at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/11/health-at-a-glance-2025_a894f72e/8f9e3f98-en.pdf).

### 6. Care technology changes relationships and control as well as efficiency

The UN Human Development Report 2025 describes the growth of paid care,
ageing-related demand and care technologies, while warning that care is
relational and that technology can reshape caring practices and relationships.
It emphasizes the need to understand older people’s preferences, beliefs and
fears regarding AI.

The technology test must therefore include consent, usability, surveillance,
false alerts, substitution of human contact, data control, caregiver workload
and the person’s ability to refuse or correct the system. A monitoring device,
telecare installation or AI assistant does not establish autonomy or safe care.

Source: [UN Human Development Report 2025](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4082930/files/hdr2025reporten.pdf).

## Bounded proposition 141

**A long-term-care entitlement, home-care package, community-support service,
caregiver allowance, respite programme, accessible-housing intervention,
assistive technology, care-worker training scheme, migration pathway or
ageing-in-place policy is not by itself autonomy, safety, dignity, family
resilience, decent care work, gender equality, cultural belonging or
non-institutionalized life.** Eligibility, expenditure, beds, visits, registered
workers, training completions, devices installed, hours authorized or people
served do not establish that a named person received reliable and preferred
support, could remain safely in a chosen place, or that unpaid and paid
caregivers retained health, income, voice, rest, protection and remedy when the
arrangement failed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Care is a social contract.** Whether responsibility sits with family,
   community, employer, market or state expresses values about dependence,
   reciprocity, age, disability and citizenship.
2. **Ageing in place is not place-neutral.** Housing design, transport, heat,
   distance, language, neighbourhood safety and local service density determine
   whether “home” is an enabling place or an isolation risk.
3. **The household is a hidden institution.** Unpaid care can reproduce gender,
   caste, class, ethnic and migration hierarchies while appearing as family duty.
4. **Migration connects care geographies.** Recruitment can improve staffing in
   destination regions while creating separation, remittance dependence and
   care gaps in origin households.
5. **Technology can redistribute control.** It may extend independence, but it
   can also make the recipient legible to a provider while making the person
   less able to refuse, contest or shape care.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A generous care entitlement can fail when providers are absent, travel is
  unpaid, authorizations expire, or the service cannot match language and
  cultural preferences.
- Community care can preserve ordinary life for one person while shifting
  night-time lifting, medication and coordination burdens to an exhausted
  family caregiver.
- A caregiver allowance can recognize work while being too small, conditional
  or administratively difficult to replace lost wages or buy respite.
- More care-worker recruitment can increase nominal capacity while insecure
  contracts, unsafe workloads and migration dependence reduce continuity.
- An assistive or AI device can improve safety for one user while increasing
  surveillance, false alarms, exclusion or loss of human contact for another.
- Institutional care is not uniformly harmful, and ageing in place is not
  uniformly good; safety, preference, relationship quality and remedy must be
  measured for the named person.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, ILO, OECD
and UN sources support the care-demand, community, unpaid-care, workforce,
migration and technology mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2**
because the public evidence rarely follows the same person, household, worker
and provider through daily continuity, autonomy, caregiver relief, cultural
fit, labour-market effects, technology refusal and remedy.

## Next test

Follow one named older or disabled person and their household caregiver through
formal home/community support, one comparable person with a different care
arrangement, and the paid workers/providers serving them. Add one migrant-care
or rural/remote comparison where relevant. Observe ordinary days and a
disruption: missed visit, hospitalization, heat event, worker absence, benefit
renewal, device failure or caregiver illness.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the care-demand, community-support, unpaid-care,
workforce, migration and technology mechanisms. It does not close named daily
care continuity, autonomy, safety, caregiver health or income, cultural fit,
worker retention, institutionalization, technology consent or remedy outcomes.
Those remain acquisition targets.
