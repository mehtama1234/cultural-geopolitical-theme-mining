# Infrastructure bargains: capacity, burden, voice, and realized benefit

**Checked:** 2026-09-21

## Plain-language finding

Across the infrastructure, firm, public-system, geopolitical, and household
themes, the important question is not simply whether a project or capability
exists. It is whether the project creates a bargain that survives contact with
users, ratepayers, workers, residents, allies, and the state.

The current evidence shows four different stages that are often collapsed:

```text
capacity or capability
  -> governance and cost allocation
  -> public or affected-party contestation
  -> operation and actual receipt
  -> durable benefit, acceptable burden, or practical exit
```

The atlas has meaningful evidence in the first three stages. The fourth stage
is still the major gap.

## Four examples, kept separate

| Case | What is visible | What is not yet established |
|---|---|---|
| Virginia large-load/data-center governance | Regulators moved toward minimum charges, long contract obligations, collateral, direct-connect cost assignment, and public monitoring; projected residential rate effects differ by allocation method. | Actual household bills, full cost recovery, facility-level payment, local service burden, and whether projects entered, stayed, or exited under the rules. |
| Prince William data-center growth | County-reported data-center revenue reached $465.9 million in TY2025, alongside reported capacity growth. | Net public benefit after service, power, water, land, and environmental costs; household incidence; resident acceptance; and whether revenue improves lived services. |
| New York transmission investment | A completed/energized Smart Path Connect project reports 45 miles of new lines, three new substations, corridor upgrades, and projected annual savings. | Retail bill pass-through, which users receive the savings, local burden, reliability improvement in practice, and whether the savings persist. |
| NATO medical and defense readiness | Exercises and procurement records show coordination, equipment, training, medical evacuation, civilian-system participation, and allied posture. | Crisis patient continuity, accepted/fielded capability, maintenance, public burden, deterrence, adversary response, and durable public consent. |
| DC Solar for All / community solar | Public materials report income-qualified household reach, average bill savings, a free subscription route, Pepco bill-credit implementation, and project rows where listed subscribers matched monthly-credit recipients. | Customer-level bill posting, credit amount and timing, offsetting charges or arrears, persistence after eligibility or account changes, and whether savings become durable energy security. |
| PJM large-load operating response | A real Northern Virginia large-load disconnection moved operators toward proposed ride-through and connection controls, while FERC separately raised tariff, cost-allocation, and reliability concerns. | Facility-level causal attribution, final rule adoption, payment and compliance, residential interruption or bill effect, backup-generation burden, and local acceptance. |
| Champlain Hudson Power Express | A named 339-mile Quebec-to-New York project reached commercial operation and created a cross-border delivery route expected to supply New York's Zone J. | Monthly delivery, rate-class cost and benefit, household bill or reliability change, host-community acceptance, substitution, and durable cross-border leverage. |
| NATO ISR Force | A named multinational ISR force reported repeated operations across Norway, Finland, and Italy, about 180 flight hours, more than 500 intelligence products, and decision-support use during NATO activities. | Product adequacy, which decisions changed, operator/host burden, public consent, adversary response, deterrence, and durable alliance advantage. |

These are not one pooled outcome. They are comparable as governance problems:
the institutional record is stronger for announcing, funding, approving, or
testing capacity than for proving who ultimately receives protection or value.

## The deeper cross-theme pattern

### 1. Public value is usually recorded before user value

Revenue, projected savings, capacity, readiness, and contract value are easier
to document than a household bill, a completed service, a resident's reduced
risk, a worker's durable security, or a patient's uninterrupted care. This
creates a systematic temptation to call institutional output a social outcome.

### 2. Cost allocation is a political and cultural act

The Virginia large-load record shows the practical issue: a rate design can
change who is expected to carry system costs before anyone can observe the
final household result. The dispute is therefore not only technical. It asks
whose growth counts as development, whose risk counts as acceptable, and who
has standing to contest the bargain.

### 3. Voice is an input, not proof of influence

Public hearings, comments, commission proceedings, and disclosure requirements
create routes for contestation. They do not by themselves show that residents,
ratepayers, workers, or allies changed the decision, received compensation, or
trusted the result. The program should code participation, decision change,
remedy, and later acceptance separately.

### 4. Dependence changes the meaning of exit

For a household, utility, hospital, firm, or alliance, leaving may be costly or
impossible. A project can therefore be formally contestable while remaining
practically unavoidable. “No exit observed” is not evidence that the bargain
was acceptable; it may indicate dependence, switching cost, or lack of an
alternative.

### 5. Resilience can shift hidden labor

Grid upgrades, large-load controls, civilian medical coordination, and defense
readiness can increase system capacity while moving labor, staffing, privacy,
land, environmental, or fiscal burdens onto households and public institutions.
The right question is who supplies the slack when the system is stressed.

## What this adds to the 14-theme atlas

This synthesis links five themes without collapsing them:

- **Infrastructure and dependency:** capacity, reliability, replaceability,
  and technical concentration.
- **Firm and market power:** who receives a preferential connection, contract,
  or operating advantage.
- **Household room and consumption:** bill exposure, service quality, and the
  ability to absorb delays or price changes.
- **Public systems and political action:** cost allocation, hearing routes,
  disclosure, enforcement, and legitimacy.
- **Geopolitical/state consequences:** accepted capability, allied burden,
  external response, and the difference between readiness theater and usable
  power.

The recurring societal insight is that infrastructure is not background. It is
a distribution system for money, time, safety, attention, land, and political
voice.

## Next decisive evidence test

The next high-value record should follow one identifier-bearing project or
incident through:

1. approval or contract;
2. construction, acceptance, or operation;
3. named affected users or public systems;
4. actual cost, service, or risk change;
5. contestation, compensation, or substitution; and
6. durability, maintenance, exit, or later public response.

That test should be applied to one project at a time. Another aggregate
capacity announcement would add breadth but not resolve the program's main
uncertainty.

## Evidence boundary

The examples above are deliberately bounded. They support a cross-domain
research lens, not a single estimate of infrastructure welfare or public
opinion. Several figures are reported or modeled rather than observed at the
household level. The missing middle—actual receipt, burden distribution,
durability, and practical alternatives—remains the central acquisition target.

## Latest realization contrast

The new energy and large-load records make the bargain visible from opposite
directions. Solar for All starts with an eligibility and infrastructure route
and reaches a reported household-level program endpoint: nearly 10,000
households served and about $500 in average annual bill savings. Its customer
terms also expose the practical gates—documents, Pepco enrollment, an estimated
60-day activation period, bill-credit rather than cash delivery, and continuity
that depends on an active account and continuing eligibility. A DCPSC filing
goes one step further at project level by reporting matching subscriber and
monthly-credit counts on selected rows, but still does not show the dollar
amount, net bill, arrears, or later energy security of any customer.

The PJM route starts from the other end: a large load actually disconnected,
operators managed the system response, and proposed reliability controls
followed. That is stronger than a capacity announcement, but it still does not
identify the facility-level cause, the final rule, who paid, or whether any
household experienced an interruption or bill change. Together these cases
show why infrastructure needs two clocks: benefit delivery to the dependent
user and stress response by the shared system. Neither clock alone proves a
durable public bargain.

The cross-border and strategic cases show the same principle at a higher
level. Commercial power delivery and repeated intelligence production are real
operating outputs, not merely plans. But operation is still an intermediate
stage: it must be connected to the affected user's bill or reliability, the
host institution's burden, or an attributed change in another actor's behavior
before it becomes evidence of durable public or geopolitical power.
