# Rotation CA — Informal settlements, climate resilience and tenure security

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** informal settlements / urban climate adaptation / tenure and land rights / basic services / livelihoods / displacement / gender and spatial justice  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 resilience-investment-to-secure-urban-life question

## Research question

When cities upgrade informal settlements, build resilient infrastructure, regularize
land, relocate households or finance climate adaptation, what converts a plan or
project into safer housing, secure tenure, affordable services, protected
livelihoods, social cohesion and resident authority without displacement or green
gentrification?

## Conversion chain

```text
informal housing, tenure insecurity and hazard exposure
  -> risk map, upgrading/regularization, infrastructure, finance or relocation plan
  -> resident participation, tenure/legal protection, water/sanitation, drainage, energy, transport and livelihood safeguards
  -> safer home, continuous services, affordable cost, stable work, reduced hazard and remedy
  -> dignity, belonging, health, social cohesion and legitimate climate-resilient city
  -> durable inclusion without displacement
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN-Habitat World Cities Report 2026 | Up to 3.4 billion people lack secure, safe and adequate housing, including more than 1 billion in informal settlements exposed to insecure tenure, hazards, overcrowding and weak services; climate-resilient housing needs justice safeguards. | global scale, risk and housing/climate policy framing | named settlement tenure, service reliability, affordability, livelihoods and resident outcomes |
| UN-Habitat proposed recommendations on informal settlements | Tenure insecurity increases eviction and limits investment and infrastructure finance; evictions cause financial/livelihood loss, and climate-displaced people often settle in marginal urban areas. | tenure mechanism, displacement and investment barrier | implementation, upgrading quality, resident consent, return/retention and service outcome |
| UN-Habitat 2025 housing-policy recommendations | Recommends twin-track upgrading plus affordable alternatives, protection of formal/informal/customary/communal tenure, community-based data and transparent local capacity. | policy architecture and participation/finance requirements | completed upgrading, cost, tenure security, displacement, access and voice |
| UN-Habitat urban-regeneration evidence | Regeneration should combine physical, economic, social, environmental and governance dimensions with inclusion, affordability, participation and long-term finance. | integrated intervention model | whether investments remain affordable, locally controlled and beneficial after implementation |
| OHCHR right-to-adequate-housing climate guidance | Adaptation and mitigation should protect adequate housing and must not increase costs, energy poverty, homelessness or inequality for informal and marginalized residents. | rights standard and safeguard boundary | enforcement, remedy, household safety, service affordability and anti-displacement result |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** informal housing, overcrowding, flood/heat/fire exposure, insecure
  tenure, poor services and displacement create concentrated urban risk.
- **Mechanism:** land law, tenure type, eviction threat, infrastructure, drainage,
  water, sanitation, energy, transport, rent, credit, income, informal work,
  gender, disability, migration and local governance determine vulnerability and
  ability to invest.
- **Operation:** residents are included in diagnosis and design, receive legal or
  practical tenure protection, infrastructure and services, can afford ongoing
  costs, retain livelihoods and access remedy when works or relocation affect them.
- **Material consequence:** flooding, heat, disease, housing cost, commute,
  eviction, debt, service continuity, income, care and health can change
  independently.
- **Meaning:** settlement is home, network, livelihood location, identity and
  social protection—not only an illegal structure. Formalization can bring
  dignity and services, but can also change belonging, rent, surveillance and
  who is allowed to remain.
- **Power:** city agencies, landowners, developers, utilities, lenders, donors,
  police, landlords, community organizations and residents distribute land,
  information, project benefits, relocation choices and remedy.

## Bounded finding

> **A climate-resilience plan, settlement-upgrading project or tenure document
> is not secure urban life.** A hazard map, drainage project, slum-upgrading
> announcement, land title, relocation site, green-finance package or improved-
> services claim does not establish that named residents remain safely housed,
> have affordable reliable services, retain livelihoods and social networks,
> avoid eviction or green gentrification, and possess meaningful participation
> and remedy.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Informality is a social and economic system, not simply a housing deficit. The
same alley can be a home, workshop, childcare network, market, religious space
and route to work. Moving residents to safer buildings may reduce flood exposure
while increasing rent, commute, surveillance, debt or loss of informal income.

Tenure should be measured as practical security, not only a formal title. Renters,
occupiers, customary and communal users, migrants, women, people with disability
and households with incomplete documents may need protection even when they
cannot receive individual ownership. Climate action must track who is displaced,
who benefits from land-value increases, and whether residents can return or stay.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A title or certificate can improve security while taxes, rent, service charges
  or market pressure make the home unaffordable.
- Infrastructure can reduce physical risk while triggering eviction,
  gentrification, land speculation or loss of informal livelihoods.
- Relocation can provide a formal unit but worsen access to work, schools, care,
  transport, culture and social networks.
- Improved water or sanitation access may be intermittent, expensive or unsafe
  for renters and undocumented residents.
- Community consultation can occur after land and design decisions are fixed.
- A settlement may be omitted from official maps or service data, making missing
  outcomes look like absence of need.
- Climate resilience can be uneven within one settlement; women, renters,
  migrants, disabled people and home-based workers may experience different
  costs and benefits.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: hazard, tenure, resident authority, upgrade,
infrastructure, service quality, affordability, livelihood, displacement,
health, social networks, cultural meaning and remedy are separate stages. Keep
resilience-investment-to-secure-urban-life claims in Tier 2 until a named
settlement ledger joins resident status, intervention, tenure, service, cost,
livelihood, displacement and later safety/retention outcomes.

## Next test

Select one flood-prone informal settlement, one heat-exposed settlement and one
upgrading or relocation project. Record tenure type, residents/renters, hazard,
eviction history, project design, participation, water/sanitation/drainage/
energy/transport, price, work and care access, gender/disability/migration,
relocation/return, land value, grievance and 6/12/24-month retention, safety,
health and livelihood outcomes. Include residents who left, were excluded from
lists or could not afford the post-project costs.
