# Rotation BU — Indigenous and minority-language vitality, digitization and AI

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** language diversity / Indigenous and minority languages / AI and language technology / education / cultural rights / data governance  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 digital-language-to-everyday-vitality question

## Research question

When language datasets, AI translation, speech tools, digital archives, mother-
tongue education and multilingual public services are introduced, what converts
technical representation into everyday language use, intergenerational
transmission, safe access, community control and cultural vitality?

## Conversion chain

```text
language exclusion, low-resource data and intergenerational decline
  -> documentation, encoding, dataset, tool, education or service intervention
  -> community governance, consent, quality, access, usability and local language content
  -> repeated use at home, school, work, media, public service and online
  -> transmission, dignity, participation, knowledge control and language vitality
  -> durable multilingual digital and social infrastructure
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** language endangerment, unequal digital presence, limited corpora,
  weak keyboards/fonts, inadequate public services and intergenerational shift
  are visible in policy and technology evidence.
- **Mechanism:** orthography and encoding, data quality, dialect variation,
  speech resources, model architecture, moderation, education, media, economic
  incentives, privacy, consent, device/connectivity access and institutional
  language policy determine whether representation becomes usable.
- **Operation:** a speaker can find, create, understand, correct and repeatedly
  use content or a service in the language; community stewards control sensitive
  material and can withdraw, restrict or revise it.
- **Material consequence:** school participation, health/legal/service access,
  employment, information safety, time, error burden, cultural production and
  income can change separately from model benchmarks.
- **Meaning:** language carries identity, kinship, land, ceremony, humor,
  memory, worldview and authority. A fluent-looking translation can still erase
  a concept, flatten a relationship or violate a cultural boundary.
- **Power:** governments, platforms, model companies, researchers, funders,
  schools, media and community language authorities decide what is collected,
  standardized, exposed, monetized, corrected and treated as legitimate.

## Bounded finding

> **Digital language presence or AI language support is not language vitality.**
> A corpus, translation model, keyboard, archive, speech recognizer, mother-
> tongue policy or multilingual-service commitment does not establish that named
> speakers can use the language reliably in daily life, that children are
> transmitting it, that a public service was completed safely, or that the
> community retains control over its language and knowledge.

### Named intervention bridge

The new named cases show three different conversion routes. Wales is a state
strategy trying to move from language technology to everyday use in workplaces,
education and services. FirstVoices places curation and sharing inside a
community-managed platform. Waikato-Maniapoto demonstrates a dialect-specific
voice technology whose stated purpose is cultural and intergenerational
connection. These are materially stronger than generic “AI can preserve
languages” claims, but none of the three sources alone proves repeated use,
child transmission, safe service completion or durable vitality. The next
measurement unit must therefore be the named language site, classroom,
workplace, service encounter or media deployment—not the existence of the tool.

The Māori case also makes readiness itself auditable. The source exposes who
contributed speech, the role of language experts, the stated purpose of the
funding and the fact that the model was still in testing. That is evidence of a
community-informed development process, not evidence that the voice was
released, authorized for unrestricted use, adopted by rangatahi or changing
transmission. A responsible protocol is a control surface whose operation and
enforcement still need evidence.

The research abstract adds process-level specificity: a named external partner,
an iwi speaker, a planned recording workload, testing, documentation and a
research poster. This improves provenance and reproducibility, but it also makes
the remaining gap precise. The public record still does not show the recording
consent terms, speaker authority, final evaluation, release license or repeated
use by learners/media. The project is therefore a high-quality readiness and
governance lead, not a completed vitality intervention.

The adjacent Tua o te Pae pathway reaches further into real use. TalkLink
documents a voice operating on an AAC device, a named user using it for a mihi,
and later testing by a small group of Māori AAC users. This closes the
technology-to-user-operation stage for a specific accessibility context and
shows cultural participation as a concrete use case. It still does not establish
large-scale rollout, long-term daily use, standardized pronunciation or
language-vitality effects; the sample and feedback remain small and device
compatibility work is still expanding.

The Welsh outcome evidence supplies a useful counterexample to easy success
narratives. A population survey reports substantial daily use, but its methods
note warns about uncertainty and divergence from census results. The post-16
evaluation finds progress in practitioner capability and small amounts of
Welsh-medium activity while higher-intensity provision remains flat and causal
attribution is absent. Technology and training can therefore move early
capability indicators without yet producing consistent institutional use or
transmission.

The new deployment records close more of the operational stage. Welsh language
technology is not only being funded: it has generated training data, named
transcription services, government workflows, a Senedd map, and business-facing
translation support. Yet the evidence remains provider-reported and mostly
describes outputs or examples rather than denominator-based adoption. A million
translated words, 30,000 place names or 1,000 businesses can demonstrate reach
without showing repeat use, quality across dialects, retention, customer
behavior or intergenerational transmission.

The latest APS refresh does not justify a simple “vitality is rising” claim.
The survey estimate moves to 843,700 speakers and 446,800 daily speakers, but
the Welsh Government says the change is not statistically significant, the APS
has increased uncertainty, and the census—not APS—is the measure for the
million-speaker target. This makes the measurement problem part of the theme:
technology outputs can be documented faster than population-level language
change can be attributed or reliably observed.

The new cross-case evidence sharpens the geography-to-service distinction. The
Māori Google Maps voice puts culturally guided pronunciation into an everyday
navigation surface and creates a visible correction loop, but it is an English
voice for place names, limited to one product and explicitly outside dialect
variation. The Welsh health and social-care review moves in the opposite
direction: it defines the service-outcome indicators needed for an Active Offer
but shows that fragmented administrative data and limited user-experience
measurement prevent routine system-level evaluation. Geographic visibility and
service accessibility are therefore separate conversion stages, even when both
are described as language inclusion.

The historical Say It Tika record is a necessary counterexample to the current
Google Maps launch. More than 67,000 corrections demonstrate substantial public
attention and a rich error-discovery channel, but the earlier campaign did not
produce the promised pronunciation repair. The current Commission page shows a
different governance design—Commission-led kaitiakitanga, licensed data, a
feedback form and an explicit statement that dialect variation is out of scope—
but it does not yet publish correction counts, resolution time, error rates or
the promised long-term kaitiaki group. The lesson is not simply “community
feedback works”; it is that a correction loop must be audited from report to
technical change, authorization and sustained quality.

The NZGB/LINZ record prevents an overly simple failure narrative as well. The
campaign did produce formal orthographic action for 629 names and 24 corrigenda,
while the authority deferred roughly 5,700 names where analysis, jurisdiction,
possible objection or Treaty-settlement issues required more work. That is
institutional conversion of a community signal, but it is not the same as
pronunciation correction: spelling, official naming, audio, map display and
navigation voice are separate artifacts with different owners and acceptance
criteria. A named pipeline can therefore be partly successful and still leave a
large unresolved tail.

The 10,000-clip LINZ release adds an important public-resource stage. It makes
pronunciation auditable and potentially reusable beyond one commercial product,
but the public announcement does not establish how many names are covered by
region or dialect, how clips are corrected, whether APIs or licenses support
reuse, or whether people actually use the clips in schools, navigation or
everyday conversation. The appropriate next test is crosswalking a sample of
clips to NZGB authority, Google Maps output and learner/service usage rather than
counting clips as revitalization.

The Gazetteer documentation closes part of the reuse problem: names have stable
records and status/history fields, downloadable datasets exist, the place-name
layer is exposed through APIs, and the NZGB reports a bulk audio-download
function. This creates a plausible public infrastructure for crosswalking
authoritative names, audio and downstream products. It still does not prove that
the audio files are discoverable or reused outside the Gazetteer, that updates
propagate to partners, or that users receive consistent pronunciation across
regions and dialects. Infrastructure interoperability is therefore an enabling
condition, not evidence of cultural or behavioral impact.

The University of Auckland pronunciation coach provides a complementary
education route. Its design is more culturally and pedagogically specific than a
generic speech score: it starts from pepeha learning, treats pronunciation as an
acceptable range and gives constructive remediation. The public account closes
the problem-definition, partner and prototype-design stages, but its one-million
user goal is an ambition, not adoption evidence. A future audit should test
whether feedback improves learner pronunciation and confidence, whether the
acceptable range is governed across dialects, and whether educators and learners
actually use the tool repeatedly.

The Sámi case adds a third conversion route: community-led service capability.
Ábo is reported as a functional public platform, so it is stronger than a plan
or corpus announcement, but the public description does not provide user
counts, error rates, dialect coverage or service outcomes. In parallel, the
same lab treats clinical assessment, cultural generation and evaluation as
separate workstreams. That separation is analytically important: an Indigenous
institution can control the agenda and still need independent evidence that a
tool is safe, accurate, repeatedly used and beneficial in high-stakes settings.

The Basque Euskorpus case adds a funded ecosystem-building route between
infrastructure and deployment. Its three-phase design explicitly names
planning, corpus/model development and transfer to firms, institutions and
citizens, while the sector list makes potential public-service conversion
visible. That is stronger than an abstract preservation claim but still a
planned pathway: the public announcements do not establish released artifacts,
sector-level adoption, user outcomes, dialect performance or rights settlement.
The atlas should therefore record funding and coalition design as enabling
evidence, not as proof of digital vitality.

The follow-up record strengthens the Basque audit without closing the outcome
gap. The state agreement makes ownership and a ministry reuse license explicit,
and Euskorpora’s 2026 staffing signals show that corpus and language-technology
capacity is being built. Those are stronger implementation controls than a
funding announcement alone. They still do not prove that a corpus or model has
been released, that source-work permissions are settled across the corpus, or
that a Basque public service, company or citizen is using the resulting tools.

Ireland adds an upstream governance pattern that is not visible in the Māori,
Sámi or Basque deployment records. Its technical working group brings the
language requirement into system design, procurement, identity and AI planning
before a public service is released. The action plan also names the “active
offer”—service provision without the citizen first requesting Irish—as a design
and implementation problem. This is a strong conversion mechanism on paper,
but its evidence burden is operational: the atlas must find changed systems,
portable language choices, procurement clauses, user feedback and completed
service encounters rather than treating the group’s existence as delivery.
The activity update closes one narrower question: the mechanism is active and
has a traceable meeting cadence. It does not close the delivery question; six
meetings and linked minutes remain process evidence until a system, contract,
feedback record or completed service can be named.

The African evidence adds a regional-systems layer. AfriEval operationalizes
the missing evaluation middle—native-speaker collection, domain review,
consent/provenance, safety tests and reproducible benchmark traces—while the
EAC declaration frames language models, regional storage, compute, finance and
procurement as sovereignty infrastructure. These are potentially reinforcing
mechanisms: evaluation can make systems safer and regional ownership can make
them more accountable. Neither source yet proves that a model is deployed at
scale, improves a public service, benefits smaller language communities or
changes everyday language use. The next audit should connect platform records
and regional commitments to named models, contracts, deployments and users.

The EAC follow-through closes more than a declaration alone. The proposed
Kiswahili Trust Mark names evaluation dimensions and a public-register mechanism,
while the university network and AI4EAC campaign create institutional and human
capacity for delivery. The evidence still stops before certification, because
the standard, assessment results and certified systems are not yet publicly
identified. This is a useful geopolitical conversion sequence—regional agenda
to standard to talent network to deployed system—but each arrow remains
auditable rather than assumed.

Vambo’s MORENA provides the first clearly shipped model artifact in the African
comparison: weights, a model card and headline evaluation results are publicly
available. That closes “model release” more convincingly than a strategy or
funding announcement, but not “trusted deployment.” Independent replication,
training-data rights, dialect and cultural-safety evaluation, fine-tuned users
and public-service outcomes remain open. The separate API page is even earlier
on the adoption chain because it is a developer preview with a waitlist. Model
availability and product access must therefore remain separate ledger stages.
The model card also supplies a useful counterexample to benchmark triumphalism:
the same release reports weak grounded generation, substantial benign-request
over-refusal, no native-speaker answer rating and no inference-provider
deployment. Open weights and strong aggregate metrics can coexist with poor
task reliability, incomplete cultural evaluation and limited access to a
production service.
The named case studies move one stage further by identifying organizations and
intended domains, but their “results” remain vendor-reported. They do not yet
show denominators, baseline comparisons, language-specific quality, safety
incidents, retention or independently measured comprehension, financial access
or health outcomes. This is customer/use-case evidence, not customer-impact
evidence. The investor metrics should be treated similarly: API volume and
partner counts are useful leads, but without definitions and time series they
cannot establish durable adoption or equitable coverage.

Jacaranda supplies a stronger service-side comparison than the vendor case
studies because its impact report names a live maternal-health workflow, daily
question volume, a triage function and human escalation. It still does not make
the 70% figure a clinical-outcome result: the report does not provide a
language-by-language confusion matrix, independent safety review, false-negative
rate, maternal morbidity outcome or counterfactual. Its five-language rollout
also closes model availability and intended reuse more than it closes safe,
equitable service delivery. This is an operational deployment with a measurable
workflow, not yet a fully validated health impact claim.

The open-source and ASR records make the reuse boundary more precise. Jacaranda
has published model artifacts and a domain-adaptation route, but automated
metrics on 2,500 questions are not equivalent to native-speaker or clinician
review. The speech model’s reported WER and explicit dialect/code-switching
warning also show why “Swahili support” is not a single quality level. A
service can be operational for a dominant or represented variety while failing
in another accent, noisy environment or mixed-language interaction.

The Q2 voice result closes a further behavioral gate: a voice call appears to
have converted some previously unengaged mothers into PROMPTS users. It remains
a prototype result, not a causal or health-outcome finding. Without the sample
size, denominator construction, comparison group, follow-up retention and
language/dialect breakdown, “30% opted in” cannot be generalized to all
low-literacy or sight-impaired mothers. It is nevertheless stronger evidence of
access conversion than a model card or product description alone.

BHASHINI adds a public-digital-infrastructure route at a much larger declared
scale. The sovereign-cloud architecture, model inventory and cultural-event
deployment show that language technology can be positioned as national
infrastructure rather than as a single product. The railway MoU shows a route
into recurring public-service workflows. Yet platform inferences, supported
languages and a successful event assistant still do not prove that a passenger
completed a transaction accurately, that a rural dialect user was served
equally, or that language access improved a durable public outcome. The India
case therefore separates infrastructure scale, event service operation and
ordinary public-service realization.

The Ghana and South Africa records add a different conversion gate: public
language-AI intent is becoming an investment and procurement surface. Ghana’s
named partners and focus on non-standard speech are more specific than a generic
commitment to “local languages,” but the public record still describes a
pathway, not a production service or measured user benefit. South Africa’s call
is stronger as an institutional mechanism because it specifies official-language
coverage, public-service domains, accessibility and a multi-year funding window;
it is still an input-side signal until awards, tools, deployment and outcomes are
auditable. These cases show why a country can have a coherent language-technology
strategy while language-specific quality and service completion remain unknown.

Rori is a useful adjacent counterexample because it closes an outcome gate
without proving a language-vitality gate. A WhatsApp tutor working on basic
devices and low-bandwidth networks has a reported positive learning signal in a
school evaluation, so access channel and learning effect can be measured. That
does not establish that the intervention operated in a Ghanaian language, that
local-language interaction caused the result, or that the result will replicate
outside the original school and implementation conditions. The correct inference
is narrower: low-bandwidth delivery plus a measured learning outcome is a stronger
service-efficacy lead than a model or policy claim, while language inclusion must
still be evaluated separately.

## Cross-case synthesis

| Case | Strongest evidence currently closed | Conversion stage still unproven |
|---|---|---|
| Māori / Te Hiku and TalkLink | Community-linked synthetic voices operate in a named AAC device and have reached small-cohort testing; a user used the voice for a mihi. | Scale, repeat use, device coverage, safety, pronunciation quality and wider cultural participation. |
| Māori / Te Taura Whiri and Google Maps | A public place-name voice exists with Commission-guided pronunciation, a correction route, named data stewardship and a product boundary. | Usage, correction performance, dialect coverage, durable kaitiaki arrangements and changed language behavior. |
| Māori / Say It Tika | A 2017 campaign generated more than 67,000 corrections, exposing demand and a large error-discovery stream. | Conversion of corrections into a functioning, governed and sustained pronunciation improvement. |
| Māori / NZGB-LINZ | Official naming records show 629 standardized approvals and 24 corrigenda, with roughly 5,700 names deferred. | Completion of the deferred tail, pronunciation quality, community acceptance and downstream voice/map updates. |
| Māori / LINZ audio resource | 10,000 place-name audio clips are publicly announced alongside a 54,000-plus-name Gazetteer. | Coverage, reuse, correction, dialect policy and actual learning/navigation use. |
| Māori / Gazetteer reuse route | Downloadable names, APIs, stable geographic records, a CC BY 4.0 layer and a reported bulk-audio function create a machine-readable reuse pathway. | Stable audio index, external reuse, update propagation, dialect coverage and user outcomes. |
| Māori / Auckland Pronunciation Coach | A community-linked learner tool defines targeted feedback and an acceptable pronunciation range. | Released product, learner improvement, dialect governance, retention and increased speaking/use. |
| Wales | Technology outputs, mapping, translation, transcription and a proposed health/social-care indicator framework are documented. | Consistent service completion, user experience, adoption denominators and causal language-vitality effects. |
| Sámi | An Indigenous-led lab reports a functional public Ábo platform and separates clinical, cultural-generation and benchmark workstreams. | Public telemetry, error/dialect evidence, validated high-stakes tools and outcomes for Sámi users. |
| Basque / Euskorpus | Public funding, a public-private coalition, corpus/model infrastructure and an explicit sector-transfer plan are named. | Released artifacts, sector pilots, rights settlement, adoption and citizen outcomes. |
| Ireland | Technical governance, active-offer design, language-choice portability and procurement requirements are institutionalized in plans and a working group. | Changed systems, enforceable procurement, portable language choice and completed Irish-language encounters. |
| Africa / AfriEval and EAC | Evaluation infrastructure and a regional sovereignty agenda connect language, data, compute, finance and governance. | Publicly verified deployments, smaller-language inclusion, regional infrastructure delivery and service outcomes. |
| India / BHASHINI | National sovereign language infrastructure, a named cultural-event assistant and a railway integration pathway are documented. | Language-specific quality, completed public transactions, rural/dialect equity and durable user outcomes. |
| Ghana / local-language speech AI | Named partners, target languages and a non-standard-speech accessibility rationale are documented. | Production tools, language/dialect quality, user uptake and completed education or public-service tasks. |
| South Africa / human-language technology | A multi-year official funding and commercialization route covers all official languages, public services and accessibility. | Funded projects, released tools, deployment, benefit sharing and user outcomes. |
| Ghana / Rori | A low-bandwidth WhatsApp tutoring channel has a reported preliminary learning effect in a named school evaluation. | Language-specific contribution, implementation fidelity, replication, retention and durable learning outcomes. |

The cross-case pattern is not “AI preserves minority languages.” It is a
conversion problem with distinct gates: community authority, usable data,
quality/evaluation, deployable product, institutional workflow, repeated user
behavior and intergenerational or service outcomes. Different cases close
different gates. A strong claim must name the case, gate and outcome; a funding
announcement, benchmark, governance group or public-facing demo cannot stand in
for all downstream gates.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Low-resource is not the same as low-value. A language can be economically small
in a global market while being central to land stewardship, ceremony, family
authority and local political legitimacy. Standardization may improve search and
education while marginalizing dialects or oral forms. A model trained on one
region may fail in another because pronunciation, code-switching, taboo terms,
kinship relations and place names differ.

Digitization also changes the geography of access. A language may be visible in
an archive but absent from the phone keyboard, school, clinic, court, workplace,
media or government portal where people need it. Community control must include
who may access sacred or personal material, how contributors are credited and
paid, whether data can train commercial models, and who can correct harmful
outputs.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A large corpus or benchmark score can coexist with low daily use, poor dialect
  coverage and no language transmission.
- Machine translation may help short exchanges while failing on clinical, legal,
  ceremonial or culturally embedded meaning.
- An archive can preserve a recording while removing community control or
  weakening embodied, intergenerational practice.
- Open data can accelerate tools but expose sacred knowledge, personal voices or
  traditional cultural expressions to extraction and commercial reuse.
- A national mother-tongue policy may exist while schools lack teachers,
  materials, assessment, connectivity or institutional permission to use it.
- AI inclusion can increase visibility while producing hallucinations, stigma or
  false confidence that makes a user less safe.
- A language can be digitally represented without restoring land rights,
  livelihood, political authority or the social conditions needed for speakers to
  keep using it.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: documentation, encoding, model performance,
community governance, usability, repeated use, service completion, education,
transmission, rights and cultural vitality are separate stages. Keep digital-
language-to-everyday-vitality claims in Tier 2 until a named language/cohort
ledger joins tool availability, quality, control, repeated use, correction,
institutional access and intergenerational outcomes.

## Next test

Select one Indigenous language, one African or Asian low-resource language and
one minority language with a public-service or education intervention. Record
orthography and dialect coverage, data provenance and consent, tool availability,
error types, correction route, device/connectivity, school or service encounter,
repeated use, speaker trust, child transmission, content production, governance,
benefit sharing and any sensitive-knowledge restriction. Compare a language with
a community-controlled dataset against one represented mainly through external
collection; do not infer vitality from model or corpus size.
