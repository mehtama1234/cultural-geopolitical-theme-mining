# Rotation BU — Indigenous and minority-language vitality, digitization and AI

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** language diversity / Indigenous and minority languages / AI and language technology / education / cultural rights / data governance  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 digital-language-to-everyday-vitality question

## Research question

When language datasets, AI translation, speech tools, digital archives, mother-
tongue education and multilingual public services are introduced, what converts
technical representation into everyday language use, intergenerational
transmission, safe access, community control and cultural vitality?

## Conversion chain

```text
language exclusion, low-resource data and intergenerational decline
  -> documentation, encoding, dataset, tool, education or service intervention
  -> community governance, consent, quality, access, usability and local language content
  -> repeated use at home, school, work, media, public service and online
  -> transmission, dignity, participation, knowledge control and language vitality
  -> durable multilingual digital and social infrastructure
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** language endangerment, unequal digital presence, limited corpora,
  weak keyboards/fonts, inadequate public services and intergenerational shift
  are visible in policy and technology evidence.
- **Mechanism:** orthography and encoding, data quality, dialect variation,
  speech resources, model architecture, moderation, education, media, economic
  incentives, privacy, consent, device/connectivity access and institutional
  language policy determine whether representation becomes usable.
- **Operation:** a speaker can find, create, understand, correct and repeatedly
  use content or a service in the language; community stewards control sensitive
  material and can withdraw, restrict or revise it.
- **Material consequence:** school participation, health/legal/service access,
  employment, information safety, time, error burden, cultural production and
  income can change separately from model benchmarks.
- **Meaning:** language carries identity, kinship, land, ceremony, humor,
  memory, worldview and authority. A fluent-looking translation can still erase
  a concept, flatten a relationship or violate a cultural boundary.
- **Power:** governments, platforms, model companies, researchers, funders,
  schools, media and community language authorities decide what is collected,
  standardized, exposed, monetized, corrected and treated as legitimate.

## Bounded finding

> **Digital language presence or AI language support is not language vitality.**
> A corpus, translation model, keyboard, archive, speech recognizer, mother-
> tongue policy or multilingual-service commitment does not establish that named
> speakers can use the language reliably in daily life, that children are
> transmitting it, that a public service was completed safely, or that the
> community retains control over its language and knowledge.

### Named intervention bridge

The new named cases show three different conversion routes. Wales is a state
strategy trying to move from language technology to everyday use in workplaces,
education and services. FirstVoices places curation and sharing inside a
community-managed platform. Waikato-Maniapoto demonstrates a dialect-specific
voice technology whose stated purpose is cultural and intergenerational
connection. These are materially stronger than generic “AI can preserve
languages” claims, but none of the three sources alone proves repeated use,
child transmission, safe service completion or durable vitality. The next
measurement unit must therefore be the named language site, classroom,
workplace, service encounter or media deployment—not the existence of the tool.

The Māori case also makes readiness itself auditable. The source exposes who
contributed speech, the role of language experts, the stated purpose of the
funding and the fact that the model was still in testing. That is evidence of a
community-informed development process, not evidence that the voice was
released, authorized for unrestricted use, adopted by rangatahi or changing
transmission. A responsible protocol is a control surface whose operation and
enforcement still need evidence.

The research abstract adds process-level specificity: a named external partner,
an iwi speaker, a planned recording workload, testing, documentation and a
research poster. This improves provenance and reproducibility, but it also makes
the remaining gap precise. The public record still does not show the recording
consent terms, speaker authority, final evaluation, release license or repeated
use by learners/media. The project is therefore a high-quality readiness and
governance lead, not a completed vitality intervention.

The adjacent Tua o te Pae pathway reaches further into real use. TalkLink
documents a voice operating on an AAC device, a named user using it for a mihi,
and later testing by a small group of Māori AAC users. This closes the
technology-to-user-operation stage for a specific accessibility context and
shows cultural participation as a concrete use case. It still does not establish
large-scale rollout, long-term daily use, standardized pronunciation or
language-vitality effects; the sample and feedback remain small and device
compatibility work is still expanding.

The Welsh outcome evidence supplies a useful counterexample to easy success
narratives. A population survey reports substantial daily use, but its methods
note warns about uncertainty and divergence from census results. The post-16
evaluation finds progress in practitioner capability and small amounts of
Welsh-medium activity while higher-intensity provision remains flat and causal
attribution is absent. Technology and training can therefore move early
capability indicators without yet producing consistent institutional use or
transmission.

The new deployment records close more of the operational stage. Welsh language
technology is not only being funded: it has generated training data, named
transcription services, government workflows, a Senedd map, and business-facing
translation support. Yet the evidence remains provider-reported and mostly
describes outputs or examples rather than denominator-based adoption. A million
translated words, 30,000 place names or 1,000 businesses can demonstrate reach
without showing repeat use, quality across dialects, retention, customer
behavior or intergenerational transmission.

The latest APS refresh does not justify a simple “vitality is rising” claim.
The survey estimate moves to 843,700 speakers and 446,800 daily speakers, but
the Welsh Government says the change is not statistically significant, the APS
has increased uncertainty, and the census—not APS—is the measure for the
million-speaker target. This makes the measurement problem part of the theme:
technology outputs can be documented faster than population-level language
change can be attributed or reliably observed.

The new cross-case evidence sharpens the geography-to-service distinction. The
Māori Google Maps voice puts culturally guided pronunciation into an everyday
navigation surface and creates a visible correction loop, but it is an English
voice for place names, limited to one product and explicitly outside dialect
variation. The Welsh health and social-care review moves in the opposite
direction: it defines the service-outcome indicators needed for an Active Offer
but shows that fragmented administrative data and limited user-experience
measurement prevent routine system-level evaluation. Geographic visibility and
service accessibility are therefore separate conversion stages, even when both
are described as language inclusion.

The Sámi case adds a third conversion route: community-led service capability.
Ábo is reported as a functional public platform, so it is stronger than a plan
or corpus announcement, but the public description does not provide user
counts, error rates, dialect coverage or service outcomes. In parallel, the
same lab treats clinical assessment, cultural generation and evaluation as
separate workstreams. That separation is analytically important: an Indigenous
institution can control the agenda and still need independent evidence that a
tool is safe, accurate, repeatedly used and beneficial in high-stakes settings.

The Basque Euskorpus case adds a funded ecosystem-building route between
infrastructure and deployment. Its three-phase design explicitly names
planning, corpus/model development and transfer to firms, institutions and
citizens, while the sector list makes potential public-service conversion
visible. That is stronger than an abstract preservation claim but still a
planned pathway: the public announcements do not establish released artifacts,
sector-level adoption, user outcomes, dialect performance or rights settlement.
The atlas should therefore record funding and coalition design as enabling
evidence, not as proof of digital vitality.

Ireland adds an upstream governance pattern that is not visible in the Māori,
Sámi or Basque deployment records. Its technical working group brings the
language requirement into system design, procurement, identity and AI planning
before a public service is released. The action plan also names the “active
offer”—service provision without the citizen first requesting Irish—as a design
and implementation problem. This is a strong conversion mechanism on paper,
but its evidence burden is operational: the atlas must find changed systems,
portable language choices, procurement clauses, user feedback and completed
service encounters rather than treating the group’s existence as delivery.

The African evidence adds a regional-systems layer. AfriEval operationalizes
the missing evaluation middle—native-speaker collection, domain review,
consent/provenance, safety tests and reproducible benchmark traces—while the
EAC declaration frames language models, regional storage, compute, finance and
procurement as sovereignty infrastructure. These are potentially reinforcing
mechanisms: evaluation can make systems safer and regional ownership can make
them more accountable. Neither source yet proves that a model is deployed at
scale, improves a public service, benefits smaller language communities or
changes everyday language use. The next audit should connect platform records
and regional commitments to named models, contracts, deployments and users.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Low-resource is not the same as low-value. A language can be economically small
in a global market while being central to land stewardship, ceremony, family
authority and local political legitimacy. Standardization may improve search and
education while marginalizing dialects or oral forms. A model trained on one
region may fail in another because pronunciation, code-switching, taboo terms,
kinship relations and place names differ.

Digitization also changes the geography of access. A language may be visible in
an archive but absent from the phone keyboard, school, clinic, court, workplace,
media or government portal where people need it. Community control must include
who may access sacred or personal material, how contributors are credited and
paid, whether data can train commercial models, and who can correct harmful
outputs.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A large corpus or benchmark score can coexist with low daily use, poor dialect
  coverage and no language transmission.
- Machine translation may help short exchanges while failing on clinical, legal,
  ceremonial or culturally embedded meaning.
- An archive can preserve a recording while removing community control or
  weakening embodied, intergenerational practice.
- Open data can accelerate tools but expose sacred knowledge, personal voices or
  traditional cultural expressions to extraction and commercial reuse.
- A national mother-tongue policy may exist while schools lack teachers,
  materials, assessment, connectivity or institutional permission to use it.
- AI inclusion can increase visibility while producing hallucinations, stigma or
  false confidence that makes a user less safe.
- A language can be digitally represented without restoring land rights,
  livelihood, political authority or the social conditions needed for speakers to
  keep using it.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: documentation, encoding, model performance,
community governance, usability, repeated use, service completion, education,
transmission, rights and cultural vitality are separate stages. Keep digital-
language-to-everyday-vitality claims in Tier 2 until a named language/cohort
ledger joins tool availability, quality, control, repeated use, correction,
institutional access and intergenerational outcomes.

## Next test

Select one Indigenous language, one African or Asian low-resource language and
one minority language with a public-service or education intervention. Record
orthography and dialect coverage, data provenance and consent, tool availability,
error types, correction route, device/connectivity, school or service encounter,
repeated use, speaker trust, child transmission, content production, governance,
benefit sharing and any sensitive-knowledge restriction. Compare a language with
a community-controlled dataset against one represented mainly through external
collection; do not infer vitality from model or corpus size.
