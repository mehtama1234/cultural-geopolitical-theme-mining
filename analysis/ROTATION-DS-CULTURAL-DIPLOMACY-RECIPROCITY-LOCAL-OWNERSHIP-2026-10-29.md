# Rotation DS — Cultural diplomacy, reciprocity and local ownership

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** cultural / geopolitical / social / peacebuilding / institutional

## Why this rotation

The atlas contains packets on cultural belonging, language, heritage, sport,
faith, diaspora protection, peacekeeping and intercultural public space. This
rotation isolates the cross-border cultural relationship as a geopolitical
mechanism. Governments and institutions use art, education, museums, cuisine,
sports, language, festivals, creative industries and diaspora networks to build
connection, reputation and influence. These instruments can also be
instrumentalised, extract local culture, marginalise dissent, or substitute
visibility for reciprocal power.

The distinction is between **cultural diplomacy**—often state-led and linked to
foreign-policy objectives—and **cultural relations**—reciprocal interaction
among state and non-state actors. A programme can be attractive and widely
attended without creating mutual understanding, local agency, durable trust or
changed geopolitical behaviour.

## Research question

When a state, city, foundation, university, museum, cultural network or diaspora
organisation funds an international cultural exchange, festival, exhibition,
language programme, sports partnership, creative-industry project or cultural
peacebuilding initiative, under what conditions does it produce reciprocal
relationship, local ownership, cultural rights and durable cooperation—and when
does it become one-way advocacy, branding, extraction or narrative power?

## Conversion chain

```text
foreign-policy objective / cultural encounter / conflict or distance
  -> funding, exchange, exhibition, education, media, festival or diaspora link
  -> local artists, institutions and communities interpret, adapt, contest or host
  -> repeated relationship, shared work, practical benefit, voice and repair
  -> trust, mutual understanding, cultural rights, peace or cooperation
  -> attributed geopolitical influence, refusal, resilience or policy durability
```

The unit must be a named programme, corridor, institution, artist/community,
exchange cohort or local peacebuilding site. Attendance, social-media reach,
positive sentiment and national branding are signals, not proof of reciprocity
or geopolitical leverage.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Cultural relations and cultural diplomacy are not interchangeable

The European Parliament’s review of the New European Agenda for Culture
describes cultural relations as reciprocal, non-coercive, transnational
interaction involving state and non-state actors. It distinguishes cultural
diplomacy as more government-led and potentially instrumentalised for foreign-
policy objectives. It also stresses people-to-people approaches involving
cultural networks, civil society and local organisations.

This distinction is foundational for the atlas. A state can use culture to
communicate values and seek influence; that does not mean the relationship is
mutual or that local actors can alter the agenda.

Source: [European Parliament implementation report on the New European Agenda for Culture](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2024/12/EP%20report%20on%20the%20implementation%20of%20the%20New%20European%20Agenda%20for%20Culture%20and%20the%20EU%20%281%29.pdf).

### 2. Cultural policy is often connected to foreign affairs, trade and industry

UNESCO’s report on participatory and integrated cultural governance finds that
interministerial cultural collaboration is especially connected to foreign
affairs, economy/business and education portfolios. It also emphasizes
participatory governance and the role of civil society in cultural policy.

This reveals the multi-domain mechanism: culture may be a rights and community
field, a diplomatic tool, a trade strategy, a tourism brand and a creative-
industry policy at the same time. Evaluation must not treat these objectives as
one outcome or assume that export value equals cultural wellbeing.

Source: [UNESCO, Participatory and integrated approaches in cultural governance](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2023/01/380474eng.pdf).

### 3. Local ownership is a peacebuilding condition

UN Peacebuilding’s community-engagement guidance calls for community-specific
conflict analysis, mapping of local civil-society actors, respectful and
transparent partnerships, and home-grown solutions. Its Niger example describes
locally identified cultural festivals and events helping communities prepare for
the reintegration of former combatants, alongside peace committees involving
women, youth, local leaders and religious actors.

This is a named mechanism rather than proof of a universal festival effect. The
relevant conversion is whether local actors identified the activity, held
decision power, continued it, and experienced changed relationships or safety.

Source: [UN Peacebuilding community-engagement guidelines](https://www.un.org/peacebuilding/sites/www.un.org.peacebuilding/files/documents/un_community-engagement_guidelines.august_2020.pdf).

### 4. Intercultural contact requires equal-status conditions

The Council of Europe’s Intercultural Cities material describes meaningful
interaction as constructive everyday encounters across differences and notes
that contact is more likely to reduce prejudice when participants have equal
status, common objectives and cooperative relationships. It also warns that
housing, education, sport, commerce, culture and city services all shape whether
interaction is integrated or segregated.

This is important for international exchanges: an event can bring people
together without changing unequal status, voice or resource access. Conflict
and disagreement are not evidence of failure by themselves; the ability to
mediate conflict under rights and rule-of-law conditions is part of the outcome.

Source: [Council of Europe, Intercultural Cities positive interaction checklist](https://rm.coe.int/positive-intercultural-interaction-checklist/1680a48c84).

### 5. Culture-for-peace frameworks foreground local agency and rights

UNESCO’s Culture for Peace independent expert report identifies local
governments and communities as frontline actors in translating cultural
diversity into peace outcomes, and civil society as an anchor for cultural
rights, justice and local agency. It recommends locally led cultural
peacebuilding, memory work, oral history, intergenerational learning and ethical
digital infrastructure.

These are governance principles and mechanisms. They do not establish that
international cultural programmes have caused peace, reduced violence or
changed state behaviour without named longitudinal evidence.

Source: [UNESCO Culture for Peace independent expert group report](https://articles.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2025/11/C4P_Report-of-the-Independent-Expert-Group-on-Culture-for-Peace.pdf).

## Bounded proposition 124

**A cultural-diplomacy strategy, international exhibition, language programme,
sports exchange, festival, museum partnership, creative-industry grant, diaspora
network or culture-for-peace project is not by itself reciprocal understanding,
local ownership, cultural rights, social cohesion, peace or geopolitical
influence.** Attendance, reach, positive sentiment, media coverage, artist
mobility, cultural export, partnership agreement or diplomatic visibility does
not establish that named local actors shared agenda-setting power, received fair
benefit, could contest representation, sustained the relationship, changed
everyday interaction, obtained remedy, or influenced later policy or behaviour.

## Cultural and geopolitical interpretation

1. **Soft power is received, translated and sometimes refused.** The sender’s
   intention is not the recipient’s meaning; local actors may reinterpret,
   appropriate, resist or ignore the signal.
2. **Reciprocity requires material and institutional symmetry.** Exchange of
   performers is not equivalent to exchange of budgets, rights, audience access,
   intellectual property, safety and decision power.
3. **Representation is a power allocation.** Who speaks for a culture, whose
   history is displayed, and who can correct an inaccurate portrayal affects
   legitimacy and belonging.
4. **Diaspora networks can be bridges and contested political spaces.** They may
   transfer skills and memory while also facing surveillance, regime pressure or
   internal disagreement.
5. **Cultural cooperation can precede policy cooperation but cannot be presumed
   to cause it.** A relationship is stronger when repeated collaboration,
   practical exchange and accountable institutions connect the cultural signal
   to later behaviour.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A high-attendance national festival can improve visibility while remaining
  one-way advocacy with no local agenda-setting power.
- A museum exchange can increase curiosity while reproducing unequal ownership,
  repatriation or provenance disputes.
- A cultural project can create real local peacebuilding benefits without
  changing national diplomatic relations.
- A government may fund artists abroad while restricting dissenting artists at
  home; cultural reach is not proof of cultural rights.
- A diaspora partnership can strengthen knowledge transfer for some members and
  expose others to political pressure or exclusion.
- Conflict or criticism during an exchange is not automatically failure; a
  transparent remedy and continued dialogue may be the more legitimate outcome.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. UNESCO, UN
Peacebuilding, Council of Europe and cultural-relations evidence support the
definition, local-ownership, contact and governance mechanisms. Keep the
integrated outcome as **Tier 2** because public sources rarely connect the same
programme and participants to repeated interaction, material reciprocity,
cultural-rights remedy and later geopolitical behaviour.

## Next test

Select one state-led cultural exchange, one city-to-city or diaspora programme,
and one locally led culture-for-peace initiative. Track:

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | foreign-policy objective, cultural concern, conflict or invitation |
| Mechanism | funding, exchange, language, exhibition, media, mobility or local partnership |
| Operation | named institution/artist/community/cohort, decision rights, representation and benefit |
| Material consequence | pay, skills, access, safety, local service, relationship continuation or remedy |
| Meaning | identity, dignity, mutuality, historical memory, trust and attribution |
| Power | agenda-setting, refusal, correction, policy response, cooperation, exit and durability |

Follow participants and host communities beyond the event, and include cases
where exchange generated contestation, withdrawal, misrepresentation or no
observable policy effect.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the distinction between cultural diplomacy and
cultural relations, the local-ownership mechanism, the intercultural-contact
conditions and the culture-for-peace governance architecture. It does not close
reciprocal benefit, cultural-rights remedy, durable local ownership, social
cohesion, peace outcomes or geopolitical leverage for named programmes.
