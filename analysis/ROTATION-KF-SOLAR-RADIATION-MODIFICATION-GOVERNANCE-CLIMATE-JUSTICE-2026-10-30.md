# Rotation KF — Solar radiation modification, governance and climate justice

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** As solar radiation modification (SRM) research and investment grow, can societies study possible climate-intervention risks transparently and inclusively—or will a speculative, transboundary technology create unilateral power, regional winners and losers, mitigation lock-in, liability gaps and intergenerational legitimacy failures?

## Why this packet matters

SRM is distinct from carbon dioxide removal. It would seek to reflect sunlight and temporarily cool the Earth, while leaving the greenhouse-gas cause of climate change in place. The central conversion is therefore not a project’s tonnes removed or permanence, but whether research and any future decision can manage uncertain, geographically uneven effects and prevent powerful actors from making unilateral or irreversible choices on behalf of people who did not consent.

```text
model / laboratory / outdoor experiment / proposed deployment
  -> atmospheric, ecological, health and regional-impact evidence
  -> governance, consultation, liability, monitoring and decision authority
  -> temperature / precipitation / crop / ecosystem / geopolitical effect
  -> mitigation and adaptation incentives, compensation, legitimacy and exit
```

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Climate intervention turns atmosphere into a contested political space. A global average temperature effect can coexist with regional precipitation, crop, ecosystem or health changes. The people most exposed to those differences may have the least influence over research agendas and no practical route to compensation. Climate justice therefore requires more than inviting observers to a workshop; it requires meaningful participation in what is studied, where, with which models, under whose authority and with what exit conditions.

The technology also creates a temporal problem. If cooling were initiated and then stopped, rapid warming could produce severe disruption; if research becomes politically or financially entrenched, mitigation may be weakened even without deployment. Early institutional choices can therefore create lock-in before technical feasibility is established. Transparency, registries, independent review and explicit exit ramps are not administrative details; they are safeguards against path dependency.

The geopolitical risk is unilateral action and asymmetric capacity. A state, consortium or private actor may have greater ability to conduct experiments or influence the agenda than climate-vulnerable countries. The new jurisdictional evidence shows a more bounded pattern than a simple “deployment race”: the UK separates research from deployment, its Met Office confines its contribution to modelling, and the Montreal Protocol brings ozone, UV, health and ecosystem questions into an existing environmental assessment regime. These are useful institutional guardrails, not a global decision rule. Conversely, a blanket refusal to study risks could leave decision-makers less prepared if an actor acts unilaterally. A legitimate research pathway must hold both possibilities: do not present SRM as a solution, and do not let uncertainty remove the need for governance, monitoring, dialogue, liability and accountability.

The UK’s five-trial arrangement makes the research boundary operational enough to audit. “Small-scale” is not the same as harmless: the assessment, publication, reversibility, community challenge, data-sharing and post-experiment monitoring stages all matter. The 2026 experiment typology strengthens this point by identifying intermediate cases that can be technically feasible yet cross legal or legitimacy thresholds. Governance should therefore classify an experiment by material release, atmospheric persistence, geographic reach, reversibility, affected interests and decision authority—not by a single label such as small or outdoor.

The Nunavut RASI work adds a place-based test. Community collaboration and staged scaling are stronger than a generic promise of public engagement, but they still do not show who holds decision authority, whether local ecological knowledge changes the design, or whether benefits and risks are distributed fairly across Arctic residents, ecosystems and global climate beneficiaries.

## Counterexample and source boundary

An SRM model, laboratory result, outdoor experiment, investment, policy dialogue, research registry, governance recommendation, national non-deployment position, pre-experiment assessment, reversibility requirement, ozone-assessment mandate, cooling scenario or public-opinion signal is not by itself viable climate action, regional safety, ecological protection, equitable participation, compensation, mitigation continuity, public consent or legitimate deployment authority. Better modelling can reveal more uncertainty; a national research boundary can coexist with global governance gaps; an independent assessment can improve procedure without resolving value conflict; and a community-engagement record or staged experiment can still leave decision power, ecological impact and global distribution unresolved.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** SRM has a consequential research and governance problem, not an established climate solution; scientific uncertainty, regional distribution, lock-in, unilateral action and legitimacy remain central. A credible research portfolio can be bounded by explicit non-deployment policy, model-only institutional roles and cross-regime ozone/ecosystem assessment, but these do not amount to global authorization or consent.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **SRM-research-to-inclusive-risk-knowledge-governance-authority-climate-justice-and-reversible-decision**. The strongest next acquisition is a named research/experiment/jurisdiction chain linking method, location, material release, scale classification, model/observation, affected region, consultation, consent, data, regulator, independent assessment, monitoring, liability/compensation, mitigation/adaptation interaction and later decision or exit. The UK ARIA trials should be tracked as an implementation test, not as proof that global SRM governance exists.
