# Rotation HU — Menstrual health, participation and dignity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When products, WASH facilities, education, health services and norm-change programmes address menstruation, do they create safe, affordable and dignified participation in school, work and public life—or only provide inputs without reliable use, bodily autonomy, attendance, health or belonging?

## Why this packet matters

Menstrual health is a cultural and geographical access problem as well as a health problem. The relevant unit is not a distributed pad, a toilet, a curriculum lesson or a campaign. It is a person who can understand what is happening, obtain a suitable product, change or wash privately, manage pain or heavy bleeding, dispose of materials safely, seek care without stigma, and continue school, work, care and civic participation.

```text
menstrual need / pain / bleeding
  -> knowledge and culturally safe conversation
  -> affordable suitable material and private WASH
  -> safe change, washing and disposal
  -> health support and pain/bleeding care
  -> attendance, work, mobility, agency and belonging
  -> durable norm change and institutional accountability
```

The conversion is especially sensitive to geography (water, sanitation, distance, climate and displacement), culture (taboo, purity, gender roles and language), and household economics (product price, emergency replacement and the cost of missing work or school).

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| HU1 | WHO’s 2026 menstrual-health fact sheet estimates that about 500 million people who menstruate lack the full range of materials and facilities needed for menstrual health; it identifies stigma, pain, heavy bleeding, disability, homelessness, migration and displacement as relevant contexts. | Global problem framing and affected-population heterogeneity. | A comparable person-level measure of product use, privacy, care, attendance or durable dignity. |
| HU2 | WHO says menstrual health includes access to information, materials, water and sanitation, disposal, health care and an environment free from stigma and discrimination. | Multi-component definition and rights/participation frame. | Whether a named programme delivers all components together. |
| HU3 | World Bank reports that Ethiopia’s One WaSH programme reached 1,074 schools and built 227 menstrual-hygiene-management rooms, with reported improvement in school attendance. | Named WASH investment, institutional route and an attendance signal. | Same-student baseline, attendance measurement, product affordability, learning, safety or attribution. |
| HU4 | UNICEF’s 2025 Mexico national survey reports that 67% of respondents lacked menstrual products at school or work for an unexpected need, up from 56% in 2022. | A named country-level emergency-access and change signal. | Why the gap changed, who bears it, actual absence, health, workplace response or policy effectiveness. |
| HU5 | UNICEF Mozambique documents young people challenging the belief that menstruation is a disease and identifying privacy, clean water, materials and social support as needs. | Youth-led norm articulation and culturally specific meaning. | Whether the norm change persisted or changed service use and participation. |
| HU6 | UNICEF Mali describes school MHM training across Koulikoro, Bamako, Mopti, Kayes and Sikasso, including student testimony about impurity beliefs. | Multi-place education and norm-change operation. | Product access, facility condition, attendance, pain care or longer-term belief/behavior change. |
| HU7 | World Bank describes Bangladesh women entrepreneurs using training and local enterprise to make menstrual products easier to access and less socially difficult to purchase. | Local economic and distribution mechanism. | Product quality, affordability for low-income users, consistent supply, uptake and health outcomes. |
| HU8 | UNICEF/WHO school-WASH reporting shows menstrual-health education varies substantially; a Papua New Guinea survey found 53% of schools taught menstrual hygiene management, but only 5% taught it to both girls and boys. | Institutional education coverage and gendered inclusion gap. | Lesson quality, comprehension, boys’ behavior, stigma, facilities, attendance or later participation. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

The same input can have different effects by place. A private toilet without water may not support washing; a product distribution without disposal may create a new burden; a lesson delivered only to girls can leave peers, teachers and household decision-makers unchanged; and a school intervention may not reach informal workers, displaced people, disabled people or people outside school. Local women’s enterprises can improve reach and reduce embarrassment, but supply, quality and price still require audit.

## Counterexample and source boundary

Mexico’s 2025 emergency-access result is a useful counterexample to assuming that visibility or policy discussion equals practical availability: a majority of surveyed respondents reported no product at school or work for an unexpected need. The Ethiopia case is a useful positive-but-bounded case: rooms and attendance improvement are observable, but the public record does not establish a same-student causal chain through product use, safety, learning, health and durable participation.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Menstrual-health inputs and norm-change activity are separate from dignified participation. Products, toilets, rooms, lessons, campaigns or enterprise support do not by themselves prove affordable use, privacy, health care, attendance, work continuity, autonomy or belonging.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **menstrual-health-system-to-dignified-participation**. The strongest next acquisition is a named school, workplace or displaced-person setting linking need, product/material choice, WASH condition, pain or bleeding support, cost, privacy, absence/presence, learning/work, complaint and later norm change, with disability and language disaggregation.
