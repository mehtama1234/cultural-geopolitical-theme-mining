# Rotation IS — Repatriation, post-return custody and living cultural continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** After an object, staff or ancestral belonging is returned, does community-directed custody produce ceremony, teaching, identity, spiritual care and cultural continuity—or does the return stop at a transfer into another institution’s temporary storage?

## Why this packet matters

The IR packet established the restitution framework and its limits. This packet follows the object after transfer and looks for post-return operations: who holds it, who decides access, whether ceremony occurs, whether children and descendants encounter it, and whether care protocols are community-controlled.

```text
return / repatriation agreement
  -> custody and destination decision
  -> care protocol, ceremony, access and interpretation
  -> family/child/community encounter and teaching
  -> identity, spiritual relationship and cultural practice
  -> durable authority, continuity and reconciliation
```

## Evidence ledger

|---|---|---|---|
| IS1 | Canada reports that 62 Indigenous objects transferred from the Vatican in November 2025 arrived in Canada in December 2025, entered temporary care at the Canadian Museum of History, and are to be researched and repatriated with Indigenous partners; the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation led negotiations. | Current named return, interim custody and Indigenous-led destination process. | Final community custody, ceremony, access, care, teaching, spiritual use or continuity. |
| IS2 | British Columbia records the Haisla Nation’s return of the G’psgolox totem pole in 2006 after prolonged negotiation; a replacement pole remained in Sweden and the original returned to Kitamaat Village, with community statements linking return to children, teachings, language and identity. | Named completed return, negotiation, destination and symbolic/educational meaning. | Long-term condition, everyday use, child learning, community governance, cultural practice or measurable reconciliation. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Post-return custody is a power question. The state, museum, church, national Indigenous organization, local nation, family, clan and descendants may not be the same claimant or custodian. A “temporary” museum can provide conservation capacity while delaying community control; a community can hold an object while lacking resources for climate-safe storage, documentation or ceremonial care.

The Haisla case shows that return can generate a living relationship among original object, replacement object, place, children and language. The Poundmaker case highlights the importance of family and ceremonial authority. The Vatican case shows that a politically significant return can still be incomplete when objects remain in interim custody. These cases caution against treating handover date as the endpoint.

## Counterexample and source boundary

Arrival in a country is not arrival in the community; temporary museum care is not community custody; a ceremony is not sustained practice; a replacement object is not the original; and a government statement about reconciliation is not evidence of reconciliation. The evidence closes named transfer, interim custody, descendants’ authority, ceremony, community-care options and implementation barriers, but no public source here closes a comparable long-term chain through everyday access, teaching, spiritual care, material preservation, healing and intergenerational continuity.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** A completed repatriation, arrival, ceremony, descendant handover, temporary custody arrangement, replacement object or conservation protocol is not by itself community control, spiritual safety, cultural teaching, healing, reconciliation or durable living continuity.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **repatriation-to-community-controlled-custody-ceremony-teaching-and-living-continuity**. The strongest next acquisition is a named object/family/community chain linking return, final custodian, decision rights, care protocol, access restriction, ceremony, language, child/youth encounter, teaching, physical condition, spiritual relationship, grievance and repeated cultural practice.
