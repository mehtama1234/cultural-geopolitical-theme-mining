# Rotation BY — Migrant care workers, care economies and cross-border household resilience

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** care economy / migrant domestic and care workers / gender / ageing and disability / social protection / labor migration corridors / sending and receiving households  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 care-policy-to-worker-and-household-resilience question

## Research question

When countries invest in childcare, eldercare, disability support, labor-migration
pathways, recruitment systems and social protection, what converts a care-policy
commitment into accessible quality care, decent work for care workers, migrant
rights, portable protection, gender equity and resilience in both sending and
receiving households?

## Conversion chain

```text
care need, ageing, disability, workforce shortage and unpaid-care burden
  -> care policy, public investment, migration pathway, recruitment and training
  -> worker status, contract, pay, hours, safety, skills recognition, social protection and care-service quality
  -> reliable care encounter, worker income/rights, family remittance and reduced unpaid burden
  -> health, dignity, gender equality, household resilience and sustainable care system
  -> durable cross-border care and social protection institutions
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** ageing, disability, childcare gaps, unpaid-care inequality, worker
  shortages and cross-border recruitment expand demand for paid and migrant care.
- **Mechanism:** wages, contracts, migration status, recruitment fees, employer
  power, live-in work, working time, training, language, recognition, social
  insurance, portability, service financing and household norms determine care
  quality and worker security.
- **Operation:** a worker crosses or remains in a labor market, receives a fair
  contract, can change employer or seek remedy, delivers care safely, is paid and
  covered, and can carry entitlements across borders; the recipient receives
  reliable, appropriate care.
- **Material consequence:** recipient health and dignity, worker income, safety,
  debt, remittances, family separation, unpaid care, labor-force participation,
  pension/maternity/disability protection and household resilience can diverge.
- **Meaning:** care is intimate, relational and culturally situated. Language,
  food, religion, ageing norms, disability expectations, privacy and family
  authority shape whether a formally available service feels safe and respectful.
- **Power:** states, employers, agencies, platforms, families, health providers,
  unions and migrant-worker organizations decide recruitment, status, pricing,
  working time, service standards, data and access to justice.

## Bounded finding

> **A care-economy plan, migrant-worker pathway or care investment is not decent
> work, quality care or household resilience.** A recruitment agreement, training
> certificate, care facility, social-protection commitment, portability rule or
> job forecast does not establish that a named worker has fair pay, safe hours,
> legal status, freedom from debt or abuse, portable benefits and remedy; or that
> a named child, older person or person with disability receives affordable,
> reliable, culturally appropriate and continuous care.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Care is a transnational infrastructure. A receiving country may solve a staffing
shortage by recruiting women from a sending country, while the sending household
loses a caregiver and gains remittance income. A policy that counts filled jobs
can miss family separation, unpaid care shifted to another woman, recruitment
debt, return insecurity or the recipient’s quality of life.

The unit must follow both sides of the corridor: worker, recipient, sending
household, employer, recruiter, training provider, insurer, social-security
agency and destination/return place. Care quality cannot be separated from
worker rights. Nor should migrant workers be treated only as labor supply;
language, race, gender, disability, legal status and cultural authority shape
their ability to refuse, correct, organize and obtain justice.

## Counterexamples and limits

- More formal care jobs can coexist with low wages, long hours, surveillance,
  unsafe live-in conditions or poor recipient care.
- A legal migration channel can still be unusable because of fees, debt,
  document retention, language, employer tie or lack of complaint access.
- Social-protection coverage in the destination does not prove portability to the
  origin country or actual payment of maternity, disability or pension benefits.
- A care facility can increase capacity while excluding rural, poor, migrant,
  disabled or culturally distinct households through price, distance or rules.
- Remittances can improve household income while care deficits, family separation
  or unpaid labor increase.
- Training and skills recognition can improve mobility but also create selection
  that leaves lower-paid domestic workers without protection.
- Care investment job forecasts are models, not evidence of completed services,
  decent jobs or better recipient outcomes.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: care need, investment, recruitment, legal
route, contract, training, working time, safety, pay, social protection,
portability, care quality, recipient outcome, household burden, gender, voice and
remedy are separate stages. Keep care-policy-to-worker-and-household-resilience
claims in Tier 2 until a named corridor/cohort ledger joins worker and recipient
entry, service encounter, rights, payment, protection, family effects and later
well-being.

## Next test

Select one South Asia–Gulf domestic-care corridor, one ASEAN corridor and one
formal care-work route in Europe or North America. Record care need, worker
status, recruitment cost/debt, contract, hours, wage, accommodation, safety,
language, training/recognition, employer change, complaint/remedy, social-
protection contribution and portability, remittance, family-care substitution,
recipient quality/continuity and outcomes at 6/12/24 months. Include workers who
returned early, changed status or were excluded from administrative data.
