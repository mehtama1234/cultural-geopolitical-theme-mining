# Rotation EG — Urban stormwater, drainage and everyday flood-risk justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** urban infrastructure / climate / water / public health / geography

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers disaster recovery, warning-to-safe-outcome, informal settlements,
public space, wastewater, groundwater and climate adaptation. This rotation
isolates everyday urban stormwater: drains, culverts, retention ponds, wetlands,
permeable surfaces, floodplains, waste blockage, green infrastructure and
operation and maintenance.

The distinction is important because a flood project may protect a central road,
while runoff is shifted to a low-income settlement; a wetland may deliver flood
storage, while land values and displacement rise; a drain may be built but not
maintained; and a “sponge city” label may not reduce household water depth,
contamination, lost work or repeated repair burden.

## Research question

When a city introduces drainage, retention, floodplain zoning, wetlands,
permeable surfaces, rain gardens, green roofs, river restoration, waste controls
or digital flood monitoring, under what conditions does it reduce actual
household disruption and health risk without shifting water, land, maintenance
cost or displacement pressure onto less powerful communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
rainfall / runoff / land cover / drainage / waste / floodplain / vulnerability
  -> zoning, grey-green infrastructure, maintenance, warning and governance
  -> street, home, school, workplace, market and watershed operation
  -> water depth, duration, contamination, access, repair, income and care result
  -> health, mobility, housing retention, livelihood and ecological resilience
  -> local authority, maintenance power, accountability, remedy and continuity
```

The unit must be a named catchment, drain, street, household, school,
settlement, wetland, project or institution, with rainfall, water depth,
duration, maintenance and outcome dates. Project completion, storage capacity,
green hectares, modelled risk or warning coverage are intermediate signals, not
proof of household protection.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Urban flood risk is a system, not a pipe count

World Bank guidance identifies drainage capacity, land use and catchment
geography as interacting determinants of urban flood risk. Community- and
nature-based solutions can collect, store and infiltrate water, delay peak runoff
and complement grey systems. The effective unit is the catchment, not one asset.

This closes the hydraulic mechanism while preserving the need to test who is
protected, where water is redirected, and whether systems remain functional.

Sources: [World Bank community and nature-based urban flood handbook](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/508581601532989979/pdf/Community-and-Nature-Based-Solutions-for-Integrated-Urban-Flood-Risk-Management-Mini-Studios-for-Water-Sensitive-Urban-Design-A-Handbook-for-Organizers-and-Facilitators.pdf)
and [World Bank green-infrastructure flood-risk evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/841391474028584058/pdf/108291-WP-P156654-PUBLIC-ABSTRACT-SENT-WBUFCOPKnowledgeNoteGreenInfrastructureSolutions.pdf).

### 2. Resilience requires data, governance, investment and community engagement

UNDRR’s 2026 urban-resilience report finds that resilient cities combine risk
data, coordinated governance, sustained investment and community engagement.
It highlights nature-based solutions, water reuse, digital monitoring and
integrated planning, while recommending long-term finance and collaboration.

The conversion test is whether risk information changes land-use, maintenance,
warning, evacuation, household protection and accountability—not whether a
dashboard or plan exists.

Source: [UNDRR Waves of Change](https://www.undrr.org/publication/documents-and-publications/waves-change-urban-resilience-pathways-flood-and-water).

### 3. Green infrastructure can provide real protection and co-benefits

UNEP identifies floodplain reconnection, wetlands and nature-based storage as
ways to absorb, purify and manage water. World Bank examples include restored
stormwater drainage and urban wetlands that provided flood protection, air
purification, livelihoods and recreational benefits. These are useful positive
cases, but the reported beneficiary counts do not close same-household damage,
retention, maintenance or distributional outcomes.

Sources: [UNEP working with nature](https://www.unep.org/en/what-we-do/fresh-water/nature-based-solutions/working-nature)
and [World Bank adaptation examples](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2025/05/20/smart-development-in-action-through-adaptation).

### 4. Maintenance and revenue are part of the infrastructure

World Bank integrated flood-management evidence recommends local stormwater
institutions, operation and maintenance, and a revenue stream. It also documents
that retention ponds can deteriorate when sewage and garbage enter them, and
that channel-only approaches can increase risk. A built asset can therefore
create a false sense of protection if maintenance, sanitation, waste control,
land-use regulation and budget authority are weak.

This is the infrastructure-to-service conversion: the question is whether a
drain, wetland or pond remains open, safe, funded and connected to the catchment
when the next storm arrives.

Source: [World Bank integrated urban flood-risk management](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/d67ef885fc1c5b4ca43cf4465b3e7241-0310012025/original/Urban-Resilience-India.pdf).

### 5. Community-responsive adaptation can produce measurable evidence

UNDRR’s Nairobi case describes community co-designed flood interventions in
high-risk informal settlements, combining constructed protection, preparedness
and financial resilience. It used intervention and control sites, repeated
household surveys and flood modelling across multiple years.

This is a stronger research design than a project completion report because it
can connect intervention to household disruption, income and local knowledge,
while still requiring attention to maintenance and who was excluded.

Source: [UNDRR community-responsive adaptation in Kenya](https://www.undrr.org/resource/community-responsive-adaptation-planning-project-kenya).

### 6. Flood protection can become land and displacement policy

Wetland restoration, floodplain protection, parks and drainage can increase
amenity and property value. Without tenure, anti-displacement and participation
mechanisms, the people who lived with flood risk may be priced out or moved,
while the project is reported as a resilience success. Conversely, refusing
development in a floodplain may protect safety but reduce housing or livelihood
options if no fair alternative exists.

The conversion chain must include land, rent, tenure, work, access, repair,
return and host-community effects—not only water depth.

## Bounded proposition 138

**A drain, levee, retention pond, wetland restoration, permeable surface, green
roof, floodplain rule, sponge-city programme, flood dashboard, warning system or
community adaptation project is not by itself reduced household flood risk,
health protection, reliable mobility, housing retention, livelihood continuity,
ecological recovery or climate justice.** Construction completion, storage
capacity, green hectares, modelled risk reduction, warning coverage, project
attendance or restored wetland area do not establish that named households,
schools, workers and informal settlements experienced lower water depth and
duration, less contamination, affordable repair, maintained access, protected
income, retained tenure, equal protection, effective warning or remedy when
water was displaced or the system failed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Drainage is an invisible public service until it fails.** Its value appears
   in avoided disruption, not only visible structures.
2. **Water follows catchments, not administrative boundaries.** Protection in one
   street can create burden downstream or across a jurisdiction.
3. **Maintenance is local power.** Clearing drains, managing waste, preserving
   wetlands and funding repair determine whether infrastructure remains real.
4. **Green resilience can change land value.** Ecological improvement can bring
   pride, recreation and health while threatening tenure and affordability.
5. **Flood memory is cultural infrastructure.** Residents hold route, warning,
   drainage and safe-place knowledge that models may miss.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A new drain can reduce flooding locally while increasing downstream flow or
  contamination.
- A wetland can provide real storage and livelihood benefits while raising land
  prices or restricting customary access.
- Permeable surfaces and rain gardens can work in one soil and rainfall context
  but fail under clogging, poor maintenance or extreme events.
- A warning system can improve response while households lack transport, safe
  shelter, documents, cash or authority to leave.
- Floodplain zoning can reduce future risk while displacing present residents if
  compensation, tenure and alternatives are inadequate.
- Community participation can improve design and legitimacy but does not prove
  that decision power or long-term maintenance funding was transferred.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. UNDRR, UNEP and
World Bank sources support the catchment, green-grey, maintenance, governance,
community and displacement mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2**
because public sources rarely follow the same households and infrastructure
through flood depth, contamination, repair, income, tenure, maintenance and
post-project power.

## Next test

Compare one community-responsive catchment, one major grey-infrastructure
project, and one green/wetland intervention. Track named households, drains,
schools, markets, workers and downstream areas across at least two rainy seasons,
including a storm exceeding design assumptions and a maintenance disruption.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the catchment, green-grey, maintenance, governance,
community and displacement mechanisms. It does not close named household flood
depth, contamination, repair, income, tenure, warning, ecological recovery,
maintenance or remedy outcomes. Those remain acquisition targets.
