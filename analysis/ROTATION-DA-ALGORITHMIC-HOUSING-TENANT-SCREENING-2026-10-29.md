# Rotation DA — Algorithmic housing access, tenant screening and neighborhood exclusion

**Date:** 2026-10-29
**Lane:** tenant screening / algorithmic housing allocation / credit and eviction records / criminal records / rental applications / adverse action / correction / fair housing / displacement / neighborhood geography / household stability
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 algorithmic-housing-to-secure-tenancy-and-neighborhood-belonging question

## Research question

When landlords, property managers, housing authorities and screening vendors use
data, scores or algorithms to decide who may rent, how do those systems alter
access to housing, application cost, displacement, neighborhood sorting and
household stability across race, disability, age, family status, language,
migration, income and place—and what evidence would show a corrected and
contestable route to tenancy?

## Conversion chain

```text
housing vacancy, applicant, household, credit, eviction, criminal, income, identity and location data
  -> data matching, score, recommendation, risk flag, pricing, targeted listing or waitlist rank
  -> landlord/property-manager decision, adverse-action notice, application fee,
     deposit, lease, denial, appeal, correction or alternative screening
  -> move, delay, temporary housing, rent burden, eviction risk, displacement,
     neighborhood access, school/work/health continuity and household coping
  -> secure affordable tenancy, fair opportunity, privacy, remedy, belonging and legitimate housing governance
```

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** a housing provider promises instant screening, “objective” risk,
  fraud prevention, flexible pricing, automated waitlists or faster leasing.
- **Mechanism:** credit, eviction, criminal, income, identity, address and
  inferred household data are matched, weighted and converted into a score,
  recommendation, rent, deposit or rank.
- **Operation:** a landlord receives the output and accepts, rejects, prices,
  delays, asks for more documentation, issues an adverse-action notice or
  considers a dispute; the applicant pays fees and searches for alternatives.
- **Material consequence:** the household obtains a lease or experiences delay,
  denial, higher cost, temporary shelter, displacement, school/work disruption,
  eviction risk or repeated screening across the market.
- **Meaning:** screening may be described as prudent risk management by owners
  and as opaque class sorting, racialized exclusion, punishment for old records
  or loss of neighborhood belonging by residents.
- **Power:** vendors, landlords, housing authorities and data brokers control
  inputs, models and notice; applicants often face time, money, language,
  disability and documentation barriers when challenging an output.

## Bounded finding

> **A tenant-screening report, rental score, eviction record, criminal-record
> search, automated waitlist or rent recommendation is not by itself fair
> housing access or secure tenancy.** A data match, risk score, adverse-action
> notice, correction portal, human-review promise, faster leasing claim or
> fair-housing policy does not establish that named households received a fair
> chance, understood the reason, avoided discriminatory burden, corrected an
> error, obtained a lease, retained housing or remained connected to their
> neighborhood.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Housing screening turns place into a portable judgment. An address may encode
  historic segregation, policing, flood risk, landlord practices, lack of
  transit or data quality rather than a household’s ability to pay rent. An
eviction filing may reflect a landlord’s action, not a completed eviction; a
criminal record may be old, incomplete or unrelated to tenancy; a thin credit
file may reflect migration, cash work or exclusion from banking. Algorithmic
screening can therefore reproduce neighborhood boundaries while presenting the
result as individualized objectivity. Families with children, disabled people,
survivors, migrants, older adults and people with limited English or digital
access may carry different correction burdens. Secure housing legitimacy is
experienced through a complete route from application to affordable retention,
not through screening accuracy alone.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Accurate, relevant screening can help reduce fraud or match households to
  housing when lawful criteria, notice, alternatives and correction are real.
- A denial correlated with race, disability or geography does not alone prove
  unlawful discrimination; data, model, landlord discretion and market supply
  must be separated.
- Correcting a report does not prove a landlord reconsidered the application or
  that a comparable unit remained available.
- Tenant-screening harm may be driven by shortage, rent levels, deposit policy,
  landlord practice or eviction law rather than an algorithm alone.
- A fair-housing policy or vendor audit is not evidence of household lease,
  retention, safety or neighborhood belonging.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: housing signal, data mechanism, screening
operation, resident exposure, tenancy consequence, neighborhood meaning and
remedy are separate stages. Keep the algorithmic-housing-to-secure-tenancy-and-
neighborhood-belonging claim in Tier 2 until a named provider joins score
exposure, subgroup, notice, dispute, correction, reconsideration, lease, price,
retention, displacement and geographic outcome.

## Next test

Select one housing provider, screening vendor or public-housing waitlist and one
market. Record data sources, matching rules, score or recommendation, price and
deposit, notice, language/disability access, dispute, correction timing,
reconsideration, lease, move-in, rent burden, renewal, eviction and household
continuity. Compare households by race, ethnicity, disability, age, family
status, migration, income, neighborhood and application channel, while coding
vacancy supply, landlord policy and local eviction law.
