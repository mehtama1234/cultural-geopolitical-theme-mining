# Rotation IE — Respectful maternity care, referral and autonomy

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When maternal-health systems expand skilled attendance, facilities, emergency obstetric care and respectful-care training, do women and families receive safe, timely, culturally responsive and autonomous care—or does the chain stop at facility contact, training or mortality statistics while mistreatment, delay and referral failure persist?

## Why this packet matters

Maternal care is a clinical and social encounter. The practical chain is:

```text
pregnancy / warning sign / labor
  -> information, trust and birth preparedness
  -> reachable facility and respectful first contact
  -> consent, communication, companion and pain support
  -> skilled clinical assessment and emergency referral
  -> treatment, birth and newborn care
  -> postnatal continuity, safety, dignity and family functioning
```

Facility delivery can coexist with disrespect, coercion, non-consensual procedures, cost barriers, delayed referral or inadequate medicines. Respectful care is not a soft add-on: it can shape whether people seek care, disclose symptoms, accept referral and return for postnatal support.

## Evidence ledger

| ID | Named evidence | What it closes | What it does not close |
|---|---|---|---|
| IE1 | WHO’s 2025 compendium defines respectful maternal and newborn care as person-centred care that protects dignity, prevents harm and supports informed choice for women, gender-diverse people, newborns, parents and families. | Normative care definition and inclusive unit. | Facility-level implementation, encounter quality or outcome. |
| IE2 | WHO reports a 2019 study in four countries in which over 40% of women experienced abuse or discrimination during labor or childbirth, including shouting, slapping or forced restraint. | Named mistreatment signal and scale boundary. | Current prevalence in every setting, reporting safety, referral, remedy or health outcome. |
| IE3 | WHO’s 2025 maternal-health messages identify chosen companions, clear communication, pain relief, mobility and choice of birth position as rights-linked components of a positive experience. | Concrete encounter requirements. | Availability at a named facility, cultural fit, staffing or clinical outcome. |
| IE4 | WHO Africa reports Senegal reduced maternal mortality from 392 per 100,000 live births in 2015 to 153 in 2023 and introduced a respectful-care initiative with birth preparedness, a labor-management guide and psychological support. | Country outcome trend and named service model. | Attribution to respectful care, subgroup equity, mistreatment reduction, referral completion or postnatal continuity. |
| IE5 | UNFPA reports a Paraguay model emphasizing dignity, emotional state, beliefs, consent, autonomy and family engagement in maternity care. | Named culturally responsive and autonomy mechanism. | Coverage, facility practice, emergency referral, safety or maternal/newborn outcome. |
| IE6 | UNFPA reports Dhaka urban-slum facility delivery at 53.7%, compared with 77.3% in non-slum areas, and describes a hospital model promising respectful, free deliveries close to home. | Urban inequality and proximity/respectful-care response. | Whether the model changes access, referral, quality, cost, safety or outcome at scale. |
| IE7 | UNFPA’s Serbia example describes a study of women’s maternity-hospital experiences followed by guidelines emphasizing respectful care, communication and informed consent, with planned training and public consultation. | Experience-to-guideline/accountability route. | Training completion, behavior change, patient receipt, complaint resolution or clinical outcome. |
| IE8 | WHO’s 2025 sickle-cell pregnancy guideline emphasizes individualized respectful care, medical history, preferences and informed decisions about treatment and complications. | Intersectional clinical-personalization requirement. | Provider competence, availability of medicines, referral, consent in emergencies or outcome equity. |

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Respect is experienced through language, privacy, family/companion norms, caste/class/race, age, marital status, disability, migration, religion and prior mistreatment. Rural distance and urban-slum barriers affect the first encounter; conflict and humanitarian settings amplify cost, referral and safety risks. Standardized clinical protocols matter, but local trust and meaningful consent determine whether they become usable care.

## Counterexample and source boundary

The Senegal mortality trend is encouraging but cannot be attributed to respectful care alone. A training or guideline can exist without changed behavior. A facility can be geographically close but unaffordable, unsafe or disrespectful. The 40% mistreatment finding prevents facility contact from being treated as quality. Public evidence closes definitions, mistreatment, encounter requirements, country models, inequality, guideline pathways and individualized-care standards, but not a comparable woman/family chain through consent, referral, treatment, safety and postnatal continuity.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 bounded finding:** Facility delivery, skilled-attendance coverage, respectful-care training, maternal-mortality improvement, clinical guideline, birth companion policy or referral plan is not by itself respectful encounter, informed consent, timely treatment, safety, autonomy or durable trust.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** The next high-value conversion is **respectful-maternity-system-to-consented-safe-referral-and-postnatal-continuity**. The strongest next acquisition is a named facility/community chain linking pregnancy/labor need, language and trust, arrival, consent, communication, companion/pain support, clinical assessment, referral, treatment, cost, birth/newborn care, complaint, postnatal follow-up and maternal/family outcome.
