# Rotation DY — Small-scale fisheries, coastal livelihood justice and blue-economy conversion

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** social / geographical / food systems / labour / culture / climate

## Why this rotation

The atlas already contains ocean-governance, port-resilience, Arctic-corridor,
Indigenous-food and coastal-livelihood themes. This rotation narrows the unit to
small-scale fisheries and the people who harvest, process, trade and consume
aquatic foods. It asks whether marine protected areas, fisheries reform,
traceability, port and cold-chain investment, blue-economy projects, climate
adaptation and conservation finance improve ecological condition while preserving
food security, safety, income, gender equity, cultural knowledge and practical
decision power.

The distinction matters because a national “blue economy” success, export value,
stock-recovery indicator or protected-area designation can coexist with lost
access to landing sites, poorer local diets, unsafe work, lower women’s control
of income or displacement of customary practices.

## Research question

When a government, donor, conservation body or investor changes fishing rules,
creates a protected area, invests in ports/cold chains, introduces traceability,
promotes tourism or offshore development, or funds climate adaptation, under
what conditions does the change produce healthier ecosystems and reliable food
and income without transferring risk and control away from named coastal
communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
stock/ecosystem/climate/food and income pressure
  -> access, tenure, conservation, labour, market and infrastructure policy
  -> vessel/landing site/processor/trader/household/community operation
  -> catch, price, safety, food quality, work, access and participation result
  -> nutrition, livelihood, health, cultural continuity and ecological outcome
  -> tenure security, gender equity, voice, remedy, bargaining and adaptive power
```

The unit must be a named fishing community, landing site, fishery, vessel,
worker group, women’s post-harvest organization, household, protected area,
market or coastal project, with dates and a counterfactual or comparison where
possible. Catch volume, export earnings, registration, protected-area coverage
and training attendance are intermediate signals, not proof of local wellbeing.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Small-scale fisheries are food, livelihood and safety-net infrastructure

The World Bank’s blue-social-protection brief reports that small-scale fisheries
produce at least 40% of global fish catch, employ more than 90% of fishers and
support hundreds of millions of people through livelihoods and subsistence.
It also emphasizes that seasonal volatility, informality and weak registration
leave many workers outside social protection and national statistics.

This closes the scale-and-vulnerability mechanism but not a named community
outcome. The measurement problem is part of the issue: dispersed, informal,
subsistence and women-led work can disappear from the administrative record.

Source: [World Bank blue social protection brief](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/f434c2f1cb2af90b55be9dd042d4384f-0320072025/related/Blue-SP-Brief.pdf).

### 2. Tenure and participation are causal mechanisms, not consultation slogans

FAO’s Voluntary Guidelines advise that small-scale fishing communities need
secure, equitable and socio-culturally appropriate tenure to fishing grounds,
landing areas and adjacent land. They warn that tourism, industrial fishing,
power production and other competing uses can push communities aside. The
guidelines also call for affected small-scale fishers to participate in the
design and implementation of protected areas and management measures.

The relevant test is therefore not whether a consultation occurred. It is
whether a named group retained access, influenced rules, could challenge a
decision, and could continue or adapt its livelihood after ecological or market
conditions changed.

Sources: [FAO responsible governance of tenure](https://www.fao.org/voluntary-guidelines-small-scale-fisheries/key-thematic-areas/responsible-governance-of-tenure/en)
and [FAO small-scale fisheries guidelines](https://www.fao.org/fishery/static/ssf/SSF_guidelines/ZeroDraftSSFGuidelines_MAY2012.pdf).

### 3. Women’s work is often the hidden conversion layer

FAO reports that women are heavily represented in processing, marketing, trade
and other post-harvest activities, while often lacking equal access to finance,
technology, infrastructure, education, information, leadership and decent work.
FAO’s implementation work also finds that infrastructure and technology projects
are not automatically sustained after funding ends; appropriate design,
organizational capacity and women’s actual decision power matter.

Therefore, a landing-site upgrade or cold-chain intervention can improve food
quality yet reduce women’s control of income if ownership, access, fees or market
rules shift to larger actors. “Women participated” is not sufficient evidence
of voice or benefit.

Sources: [FAO gender in fisheries](https://www.fao.org/gender/learning-center/thematic-areas/gender-in-fisheries-and-aquaculture/1/en?tabInx=3)
and [FAO women’s small-scale fisheries implementation evidence](https://www.fao.org/voluntary-guidelines-small-scale-fisheries/in-action/implementation/norad-ssf-project/).

### 4. Decent work and ecological sustainability must be measured together

ILO Convention No. 188 establishes standards concerning safety training, risk
assessment with fisher participation, medical care, injury protection and social
security. The ILO’s current fisheries partnership frames decent work as part of
an inclusive and sustainable blue economy, not as a separate labour issue.

This provides a minimum rights floor, but ratification or a training programme
does not establish that a particular vessel, landing site or worker actually
received safe equipment, rest, written terms, compensation or social protection.

Sources: [ILO Work in Fishing Convention No. 188](https://www.ilo.org/dyn/normlex/en/f?p=NORMLEXPUB:12100:2036832942107618::NO:12100:P12100_ILO_CODE:C188:NO)
and [ILO Partnership for Decent Work in Fisheries](https://www.ilo.org/about-partnership).

### 5. Blue-economy transition assumptions can fail

World Bank fisheries-project evidence warns that projects often assume livelihood
assistance will move communities out of fishing or reduce pressure on resources,
without testing whether the proposed alternative is viable. It specifically
notes that small grants alone may not support such transitions and that project
theories may be unclear about whether assistance is resilience-building or job
substitution.

This is an important counterexample to “diversification” as an automatic good.
Tourism, aquaculture or alternative employment may be useful for some households
but may also be seasonal, debt-bearing, culturally disruptive or inaccessible to
women, older workers, undocumented workers or people without assets.

Source: [World Bank Making Waves fisheries portfolio review](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099431408192429035/pdf/SECBOS-98bcbc58-d8fb-4d8d-af0b-1e4bead5702b.pdf).

### 6. Ocean knowledge is cultural and operational infrastructure

UNESCO describes Indigenous and local ocean knowledge as including navigation,
tides, currents, habitat conservation, biodiversity, governance systems,
cultural values and intergenerational memory. The Saloum Delta case shows how
women’s shellfish harvesting can be both living heritage and resource-management
practice.

The conversion test is whether projects protect knowledge holders’ authority,
data rights and transmission to younger generations, rather than extracting
local knowledge while transferring control to a consultant, platform or external
conservation institution.

Sources: [UNESCO Indigenous knowledge of the ocean](https://www.unesco.org/en/links/ocean)
and [UNESCO Saloum Delta shellfish heritage](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ancestral-shellfish-harvesting-living-heritage-women-saloum-delta).

## Bounded proposition 130

**A marine protected area, fisheries-management plan, vessel-registration
programme, traceability system, blue-economy investment, port or cold-chain
upgrade, climate-adaptation project, tourism programme, conservation-finance
deal or alternative-livelihood package is not by itself ecological recovery,
food security, decent work, women’s economic agency, cultural continuity or
coastal justice.** Protected-area coverage, catch or export value, vessel
registration, training attendance, new infrastructure, alternative-job counts or
community consultation do not establish that named fishers and post-harvest
workers retained secure access, received safe and stable income, improved local
nutrition, gained social protection, influenced rules, protected knowledge,
avoided displacement, or obtained remedy when conditions changed.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **The landing site is a civic institution.** It is simultaneously workplace,
   food system, market, social network, gendered space and intergenerational
   knowledge site.
2. **Tenure is practical belonging.** A legal map can recognize a protected area
   while removing the physical access, seasonal route or shoreline space needed
   to live the fishery.
3. **Women’s value-chain work is often hidden by the sea-facing metric.** Catch
   and vessel data can miss processing, trade, care, gleaning and unpaid work.
4. **Conservation has distribution.** Ecological gains may be real while access,
   price, food availability or safety burdens move to poorer households.
5. **Knowledge is not free data.** Local ecological knowledge can improve
   monitoring and adaptation, but extraction without authority, attribution,
   benefit sharing or refusal rights can weaken legitimacy.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A protected area can improve stocks over time while restricting customary
  access or shifting fishing pressure to less safe waters.
- Vessel registration can improve monitoring and eligibility for support while
  excluding undocumented, seasonal or women-led activity.
- Cold-chain investment can reduce spoilage and improve nutrition while fees,
  ownership or procurement shift value to larger traders.
- Tourism or aquaculture diversification can stabilize some incomes while
  increasing land prices, seasonal dependence or cultural displacement.
- Traceability can reward verified sustainable products while making informal
  fishers invisible to formal markets.
- Community co-management can improve compliance and legitimacy, but only if
  decision power, enforcement accountability and remedy are real rather than
  ceremonial.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. FAO, ILO, World
Bank and UNESCO sources support the scale, tenure, labour, gender, transition
and knowledge mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at **Tier 2** because the
public record rarely follows the same community through ecological change,
access, nutrition, income, safety, cultural continuity and post-project power.

## Next test

Compare one co-managed fishery, one conservation or port project with restricted
access, and one blue-economy diversification project. Follow named fishing and
post-harvest households through at least one seasonal cycle and one disruption.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the scale, tenure, gender, labour, transition and local-
knowledge mechanisms. It does not close named ecological recovery linked to
household nutrition, income, safety, cultural continuity, women’s control or
post-project bargaining power. Those remain acquisition targets.
