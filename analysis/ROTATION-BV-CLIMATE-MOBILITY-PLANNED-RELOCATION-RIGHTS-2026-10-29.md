# Rotation BV — Climate mobility, planned relocation and rights-based continuity

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** climate mobility / internal migration / displacement / planned relocation / receiving places / human rights / livelihoods and cultural continuity  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 climate-risk-to-safe-mobility-and-continuity question

## Research question

When climate models forecast migration or governments plan adaptation and
relocation, what converts a risk map or policy commitment into safe, voluntary
where possible, dignified movement; secure land and housing; services,
livelihoods, social cohesion, rights and cultural continuity for both moving and
receiving communities?

## Conversion chain

```text
climate hazard, slow-onset loss and livelihood stress
  -> exposure, immobility, displacement or mobility choice
  -> information, finance, legal route, adaptation-in-place or planned-relocation decision
  -> land/housing, documentation, transport, services, jobs, social protection and host-community preparation
  -> safe movement or safe staying, livelihood recovery, rights, belonging and cultural continuity
  -> durable resilience in sending, moving and receiving places
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| World Bank Groundswell | Models potential internal climate-migration hotspots and emphasizes a life-cycle approach: adapt in place, enable mobility, and prepare after migration; planned relocation should be a last resort and participatory. | scenario scale, hotspot logic and policy architecture | actual household movement, consent, land, services, jobs, integration and outcomes |
| World Bank Groundswell Africa | Highlights weak preparedness in many places and the need for skills, information, legal support, services, livelihood opportunity and migrant inclusion in planning. | regional mechanism and receiving-place requirements | named corridor, completed route, tenure, income, service use, host relations and return |
| IOM Climate Mobility and Childhood | Treats migration, displacement and planned relocation as distinct climate-mobility forms and identifies data/evidence gaps affecting children on the move. | conceptual distinctions and child-risk evidence gap | child protection, education, family unity, voice, durable solution and long-term wellbeing |
| OHCHR climate change, migration and human-rights messages | Planned relocation should generally be last resort, with meaningful participation, housing, employment, social protection, living-standard restoration, self-determination, culture and remedy. | rights standard and accountability requirements | implementation, consent, grievance, remedy, receiving-community impacts and actual restoration |
| World Bank forced-displacement evidence | Records a large global forcibly displaced population and shows how host-community projects connect cash, services and local inclusion to displacement response. | scale and host-community policy mechanism | climate-specific attribution, durable service access, social cohesion, livelihoods and agency |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** hazard projections, slow-onset environmental change, displacement
  events, immobility and changing livelihood conditions indicate mobility pressure.
- **Mechanism:** water, land, crop and ecosystem change, income, assets, gender,
  age, disability, documentation, borders, legal status, networks, transport,
  housing, services and host-place capacity determine who moves, stays or is
  trapped.
- **Operation:** people receive usable information, choose or are forced to
  move, obtain documents and land/housing, cross or remain within legal routes,
  access services and work, and participate in decisions affecting origin and
  destination communities.
- **Material consequence:** safety, income, food/water, education, health,
  housing tenure, debt, social protection, family unity, return and cultural
  practice can diverge sharply from a migration forecast.
- **Meaning:** leaving can be understood as survival, loss, opportunity,
  dispossession or betrayal; staying can be resilience or immobility under
  constraint. “Resettlement” has different legitimacy depending on land,
  ancestry, graves, language, customary authority and belonging.
- **Power:** governments, landlords, employers, border systems, donors,
  infrastructure agencies, host communities and customary authorities decide
  who can move, who receives land and services, whose loss counts and whether
  affected people can refuse or challenge a plan.

## Bounded finding

> **A climate-migration projection, displacement count or relocation plan is not
> safe mobility or durable adaptation.** A hotspot map, early warning, cash
> program, legal pathway, managed-retreat announcement or resettlement site does
> not establish that a named person or community moved by choice or with
> protection, obtained secure housing and land, restored livelihoods and living
> standards, accessed services, maintained family/cultural continuity, or that
> receiving communities were prepared and fairly supported.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Climate mobility is not one event. It includes temporary evacuation, seasonal
movement, urban migration, displacement, circular livelihood movement, staying
under constraint, return and planned relocation. A household may be mobile while
still insecure, or physically safe while losing land tenure, social identity,
language, customary work and political voice.

The unit of analysis must follow the whole corridor: origin, route, destination,
return and the institutions that connect them. Receiving places are not empty
containers. Land and housing competition, service pressure, labor-market
segmentation, xenophobia and host-community exclusion can convert a climate
adaptation policy into a new inequality. Conversely, well-planned secondary
cities and migrant inclusion can create opportunity when newcomers have rights,
services and voice.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A modelled hotspot is a scenario, not a count of people who will move; climate
  effects interact with poverty, conflict, markets, policy and adaptation.
- More movement can reflect greater capacity to move, while less movement can
  reflect successful adaptation or inability to escape.
- Planned relocation can reduce physical hazard while causing land conflict,
  debt, cultural loss, gender-based risk or host-community resentment.
- Cash or housing delivery does not prove secure tenure, service continuity,
  livelihood recovery or meaningful consent.
- A legal migration route can remain unusable because of cost, documentation,
  discrimination, distance, language or lack of networks.
- Climate attribution is often mixed with conflict, development, labor and
  household decisions; do not claim a single climate cause without evidence.
- Return may be unsafe or impossible even when a program reports “durable
  solutions”; absence of return is not itself evidence of failure or success.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: hazard, forecast, mobility choice,
immobility, displacement, legal route, consent, land, housing, services,
livelihood, host capacity, rights, culture, return and remedy are separate
stages. Keep climate-risk-to-safe-mobility-and-continuity claims in Tier 2 until
a named corridor/cohort ledger joins origin conditions, movement, destination
rights and services, livelihood, social cohesion, cultural continuity and later
security or return.

## Next test

Select one slow-onset rural-to-urban corridor, one disaster-displacement route
and one planned-relocation or managed-retreat case. Record hazard and livelihood
conditions, forecast uncertainty, who can stay or move, information, consent,
documents, transport, land/tenure, housing, income, services, education and
health, gender/age/disability, host-community support, cultural practice,
grievance, return and outcomes at 6/12/24 months. Include people who stayed or
were unable to move, and compare modeled migration with observed trajectories.
