# Rotation DO — Public space, safety, inclusion and belonging

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** cultural / social / geographical / urban governance

## Why this rotation

The atlas already contains packets on transit, sport, libraries, extreme heat,
refugee integration and practical mobility. This rotation isolates the public
place as the unit of analysis: a street, park, playground, plaza, market route,
waterfront, courtyard or other shared space. It asks whether investment and
participation produce usable access across gender, age, disability, class,
informality and time of day.

The distinction matters culturally and geographically. A place can be formally
open while being socially closed through fear, harassment, traffic, heat, poor
lighting, inaccessible surfaces, policing, cost, informal exclusion or lack of
maintenance. A design that improves daytime appearance may still fail a woman
walking home, a child going to school, an older person seeking shade, a disabled
person needing a continuous route, or a street vendor whose livelihood depends
on tolerated presence.

## Research question

When a city, donor, developer or community invests in a public-space upgrade,
safety audit, lighting program, play area, green/blue space, surveillance
system, universal-design intervention or co-design process, under what
conditions does it become safe, reachable, inclusive and meaningfully used by
named groups—and who is displaced, monitored, excluded, or burdened by the
intervention?

## Conversion chain

```text
need / exclusion / unsafe route
  -> mapping, audit, co-design, capital or operating intervention
  -> physical change + rules + stewarding + social response
  -> actual arrival, movement, play, work, care, gathering or civic use
  -> perceived and experienced safety, inclusion, health and belonging
  -> maintenance, accountability, non-displacement and durable right to use
```

The unit must remain a named place and subgroup. Citywide counts, renderings,
consultation attendance and self-reported intention are discovery evidence, not
proof of completed access or durable belonging.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Child-centred public-space guidance makes access multidimensional

WHO, UNICEF and UN-Habitat’s 2026 *Guide to creating urban public SPACES for
children* frames public space as linked to children’s health, development,
learning and social ties. Its six principles are Safety, Play, Access, Child
Health, Equity and Sustainability. The accompanying release reports that only
44% of urban residents live near an open public space, falling to 30% in
low- and middle-income countries. It recommends traffic calming, safe routes,
shade and cooling, clean air, universal design, community participation and
attention to climate hazards.

This closes the architecture-to-design stage. It does not establish that a
named child or caregiver can reach, use and repeatedly return to a specific
space, or that the space is safe after construction and across seasons.

Source: [WHO/UNICEF/UN-Habitat guide](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240116313)
and [WHO release](https://www.who.int/news/item/21-01-2026-new-guide-promotes-safe--inclusive-and-child-friendly-public-spaces).

### 2. Gendered safety is temporal, spatial and behavioural

UN-Habitat’s public-space work states that women and girls do not use urban
spaces in the same way as boys and men and promotes gender-responsive planning,
participation and management. Its Jericho assessment records a concrete
place/time signal: women reported greater night-time unsafety than men, with
lighting, visibility and security presence among the factors affecting safety.

This is culturally important because “access” includes the ability to move
without changing route, time, clothing, companion, work pattern or care
arrangement. It also warns against treating CCTV or security presence as an
automatic safety outcome: these may protect some users while increasing
surveillance or exclusion for others.

Sources: [UN-Habitat gender-responsive urban planning](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/download-manager-files/Gender%20Responsive%20Urban%20Planning%20and%20Design.pdf)
and [Jericho public-space assessment](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2021/07/jericho_city-wide_public_space_assessment_report-_english_1.pdf).

### 3. Assessment and management are part of the intervention

UN-Habitat’s Public Space Assessment, Design and Management Methodology
combines citywide inventories with site-specific interventions and a process
for local-government and community use. The Palestinian testing emphasizes
mapping physical and social characteristics, identifying women’s safety and
access barriers, and linking assessment to management rather than stopping at
design.

The mechanism is transferable: a capital project can fail because no actor is
responsible for cleaning, lighting repair, conflict response, accessibility or
the rules governing informal use. A place is an operating institution, not just
an asset.

Source: [UN-Habitat methodology](https://unhabitat.org/the-public-space-assessment-design-and-management-methodology).

### 4. Programme scale is not the same as durable use

UN-Habitat reports that its Global Public Space Programme has supported more
than 1.85 million people’s access to over 134 spaces in 88 cities and engaged
more than 20,000 people in co-design and co-building. These figures are useful
for scale and programme architecture, but they are not a common outcome
measure. They do not by themselves show repeat use, subgroup safety, operating
cost, displacement, maintenance, or whether the people who co-designed a place
retain influence after handover.

Source: [UN-Habitat Global Public Space Programme](https://unhabitat.org/programme/global-public-space-programme).

### 5. Green space can support health, but the social mechanism matters

WHO’s evidence review finds that urban green-space interventions can produce
health, social and environmental benefits, with particular potential for lower
socioeconomic groups. It also identifies a design and evidence limitation:
physical improvement works best when combined with social engagement and
outreach, and health and equity outcomes are not consistently monitored.

This supports a bounded proposition about coupling physical access with social
permission, programming and governance. It does not support a universal claim
that every park or green-space investment improves health or equality.

Source: [WHO review of urban green-space interventions](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/m/item/urban-green-space-interventions-and-health--a-review-of-impacts-and-effectiveness.-full-report).

### 6. Indian Safe City infrastructure shows the intervention bundle

UN Women’s India measure page describes a Safe City Project implemented in
eight major cities, combining high-risk-zone mapping, street lighting, CCTV,
panic buttons, police patrols and gender-sensitive public transport access.
This is useful as an intervention bundle and an accountability route. The
public description does not establish that each named user experienced reduced
harassment, completed more trips, trusted the reporting channel, or avoided
surveillance and exclusion.

Source: [UN Women India Safe City Project](https://data.unwomen.org/global-database-on-violence-against-women/country-profile/India/measures/Safe%20City%20Project%20for%20Women%E2%80%99s%20Urban%20Safety).

## Bounded proposition 120

**A park, plaza, playground, street-lighting project, safety audit, green-space
upgrade, surveillance system, co-design workshop or public-space grant is not
by itself safe inclusive access, belonging, health or a durable right to the
city.** A completed asset, consultation, mapped risk, participation count,
lighting installation, CCTV deployment or reported satisfaction does not
establish that named women, children, older people, disabled people, informal
workers or minority groups could reach and use the place at the times they
needed, experienced less harassment or exclusion, retained livelihood and
cultural access, influenced management, received remedy, or returned after
maintenance and policing practices changed.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

1. **The public place has a time geography.** Day/night, school hours, care
   journeys, religious gatherings, market cycles, heat and seasonal weather can
   create different “cities” for different groups.
2. **Safety is relational, not only physical.** Lighting, sightlines and
   crossings matter, but so do harassment norms, police conduct, surveillance,
   language, disability access, social recognition and the ability to ask for
   help.
3. **Co-design can redistribute voice without redistributing control.** The
   test is whether participants influence rules, budgets, maintenance and
   conflict response after the ribbon-cutting event.
4. **Beautification can create a displacement trade-off.** A safer or greener
   space may raise rents, remove vendors, criminalize informal gathering or
   narrow culturally meaningful uses. Inclusion requires tracking who leaves as
   well as who arrives.
5. **Belonging is observable through repeated use and attribution.** A place
   becomes socially meaningful when people can use it without extraordinary
   precautions, recognize themselves in its rules and design, and identify an
   accountable steward when harm occurs.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A high-quality park can remain unused by girls if access routes, norms or
  harassment remain unchanged.
- More CCTV can increase detection or perceived safety for some users while
  deterring migrants, protesters, young people or informal workers.
- A community meeting can be inclusive in attendance but not in decision
  power if land, budget and management rules are already fixed.
- A green-space upgrade can improve shade and health while displacing vendors
  or raising nearby costs; these are separate outcomes, not a single score.
- A documented safety perception can improve without objective incident decline,
  and incident counts can decline because people stop reporting or stop using
  the place. Both routes must be measured.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition above. The
architecture, subgroup mechanism and intervention bundle are well supported by
multilateral guidance and named place assessments. Keep the later outcome as
**Tier 2** because the public sources do not provide a consistent
place-by-subgroup chain from investment to repeated use, incident exposure,
livelihood/cultural continuity, maintenance and post-intervention governance.

## Next test

Select one named public-space intervention in each of three contrasting
geographies (dense low-income/informal settlement, middle-income city, and
conflict- or climate-affected place). Build a six-clock ledger containing:

| Stage | Minimum fields |
|---|---|
| Signal | policy, complaint, audit or safety concern; date and source |
| Mechanism | route, lighting, shade, traffic, policing, norm, cost or exclusion pathway |
| Operation | named place, subgroup, time of day, visit/route, service or management action |
| Material consequence | completed access, incident/harassment, health, livelihood, displacement or care burden |
| Meaning | safety, dignity, belonging, cultural fit, attribution and trust |
| Power | complaint, correction, management voice, refusal, exit, return and maintenance durability |

Do not promote until the same place and subgroup can be followed across at
least two seasons or a meaningful pre/post period, with non-users and displaced
users recorded as counterexamples.

## Source boundary

The current packet closes the evidence at public-space principles, programme
scale, gendered place assessment and intervention architecture. It does not
close individual-level safety, repeated use, causal health effects,
displacement, cultural continuity or long-run maintenance. Those remain named
acquisition targets rather than implied outcomes.
