# Rotation LF — Strategic-sector investment, industrial policy and local value

**Date:** 2026-10-30  
**Research question:** When capital concentrates in AI infrastructure, semiconductors, critical minerals and energy-transition technologies, what turns foreign investment and industrial policy into local productive capacity, decent work, technology transfer and resilient value chains rather than enclave growth and geopolitical dependence?

## Why this packet matters

UNCTAD's 2026 investment evidence shows a structural shift: strategic sectors
now attract a much larger share of greenfield investment while traditional
manufacturing entry points for lower-income economies decline. The relevant unit
is not an announced project alone. It is the chain from investor and incentive
through land, energy, water, suppliers, skills, technology, jobs, tax, ownership,
environmental burden and later value retention.

```text
strategic priority / subsidy / screening / investor decision
  -> site, capital, infrastructure, energy, water and supplier network
  -> construction, operation, skills, jobs, technology and tax
  -> local value retention, environmental burden, bargaining power and resilience
  -> reinvestment, spillover, exit, stranded asset or geopolitical dependence
```

## Cultural, geographical and geopolitical reading

Industrial policy is experienced in places. A data centre can bring construction
work and connectivity while competing for water and electricity; a mine can bring
roads and revenue while disrupting land, culture and ecological systems; a
semiconductor plant can create high-skill jobs while importing its suppliers and
leaving local workers in lower-value services. “Strategic” from a national
security perspective may be extractive or exclusionary from a community's point
of view.

The geography of value matters. Project announcements are often counted where the
capital is booked, while minerals, energy, water, labour, tax concessions and
environmental risk are distributed across places. A destination can capture
investment without capturing design, IP, procurement, skills or bargaining power.
Conversely, a smaller economy may use regional specialization and supplier
development to retain more value than its project count suggests.

Geopolitical competition changes the investment bargain. Subsidies, export
controls, investment screening and economic-security alliances can create
opportunities for trusted partners while narrowing access for others. Strategic
dependence can move from oil to chips, cloud, minerals or batteries. The atlas
must retain both the security rationale and the distributional effects.

## Counterexample and source boundary

An announced greenfield project, subsidy, investment screening decision, data
centre, mine, factory, jobs target, supplier programme, technology-transfer
clause, local-content rule or strategic-sector share does not prove completion,
productive capacity, decent work, local ownership or public value. Capital can be
reallocated, projects can be delayed, benefits can be repatriated, and local
content can be low quality or temporary. Concentration statistics describe risk
and opportunity but do not establish country-level causality.

## Promotion decision

**Tier 1 deepened finding:** Strategic-sector investment is increasingly
concentrated and shaped by industrial policy and geopolitics, while traditional
development entry points narrow. Development impact depends on the local chain
from site and infrastructure to skills, suppliers, rights, value retention and
resilience.

**Tier 2 mechanism candidate:** **strategic-investment-to-local-productive-capacity-decent-work-technology-transfer-and-shared-value**. The next acquisition should follow one named project from announcement and incentive through land/energy/water, construction, suppliers, employment, skills, technology/IP, tax, community consent, environmental burden, operation, reinvestment and exit, with a non-strategic or alternative-location comparator.
