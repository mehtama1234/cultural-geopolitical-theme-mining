# Rotation GV — science diplomacy, reciprocal cooperation and geopolitical trust

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** science diplomacy, international research cooperation, strategic autonomy, global commons and geopolitical trust

## Why this rotation exists

Science diplomacy is being elevated from a specialist practice to a strategic
instrument. Scientific cooperation can create channels between actors whose
official relations are strained, support shared global goods and strengthen
capacity. It can also project national interests, protect strategic technology,
condition access, reproduce unequal partnerships or turn openness into leverage.
The key distinction is between a meeting, agreement, network or shared facility
and a durable change in cooperation, local capability, crisis management or
geopolitical behaviour.

## Research question

When states, universities, firms, international organisations and communities
use scientific cooperation or scientific evidence in diplomacy, under what
conditions does the arrangement produce reciprocal capacity, trusted dialogue,
shared public goods and legitimate geopolitical cooperation rather than symbolic
exchange, dependency, knowledge extraction, securitisation or one-sided power?

## Conversion chain

```text
shared question / expertise / data / facility / researcher network / diplomatic channel
  -> reciprocal rules, access, safeguards, financing, interpretation and sustained contact
  -> partners solve a bounded problem or manage a shared risk together
  -> local capability, public-good delivery, crisis communication or policy alignment improves
  -> cooperation survives political change with fair benefit, autonomy and legitimate trust
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. UNESCO's 2025 Global Ministerial Dialogue frames science diplomacy as a
   response to geopolitical tension and technological change, seeking a global
   framework for dialogue and sustainable development. The event and its
   participation demonstrate agenda formation, not evidence that diplomatic
   cooperation changed after the event.
2. The European Commission's 2025 framework was co-created by 130 scientists,
   diplomats and experts and proposes strategic, operational and enabling
   instruments. This shows institutionalisation and a design architecture, but
   recommendations do not establish reciprocal benefit or durable diplomatic
   outcomes.
3. The EU Council's 2026 recommendation defines science diplomacy as using
   science, evidence and cooperation to support foreign policy, and diplomacy to
   support international scientific progress. It stresses openness alongside
   research security, reciprocity, academic freedom and institutional autonomy.
   The explicit balance is itself an important governance tension: cooperation
   can be both public-good practice and strategic statecraft.
4. OECD's Science, Technology and Innovation Outlook 2025 reports that
   geopolitical competition is reshaping international STI cooperation through
   research-security measures, critical-technology policy and science diplomacy.
   It describes cooperative, competitive and hybrid forms, including Track 2
   networks. Examples of dialogue or capability do not automatically close the
   chain to influence, trust or local public value.
5. OECD documents examples in which scientific links supported arms-control
   dialogue, weather-data cooperation, public-health exchange and cross-border
   environmental discussion. These are useful mechanism cases, but they are
   selected examples and do not establish a general success rate or prove that
   benefits were equally distributed among local communities.
6. UNESCO's 2026 CUSP project explicitly aims to bring perspectives from Africa,
   Latin America and the Caribbean, Asia and the Pacific, and Arab States into
   science-diplomacy ecosystems. This responds to a geographical representation
   gap; participation in a global project still needs to be traced to agenda
   power, funding, capability, benefit and later policy influence.

## Bounded proposition

**A science-diplomacy dialogue, memorandum, research partnership, shared
facility, expert network, data exchange, fellowship, standards coalition or
international project is not by itself reciprocal cooperation, local capacity,
shared public-good delivery, crisis de-escalation, strategic autonomy,
geopolitical trust or legitimate influence.** A state may sign an agreement,
scientists may collaborate or a facility may open without establishing that
partners had equal agenda power, received durable capability, retained data and
research autonomy, solved a shared problem, protected affected communities or
changed later diplomatic behaviour.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Science diplomacy is not placeless. Research infrastructures, field sites,
data, languages, environmental risks and community knowledge sit in particular
territories. A partnership headquartered in a wealthy state may count local
institutions as implementers while retaining funding, data, authorship and
decision power. Conversely, regional centres and diaspora networks can create
meaningful reciprocal capacity when they train, retain and empower local actors.

The cultural question is whose knowledge is treated as credible and whose
security or development priorities define the problem. The geographical question
is whether a global facility or agreement changes capability and protection in
the places exposed to the risk. Trust should be measured through repeated
cooperation, transparent rules, correction, benefit sharing, crisis behaviour
and the ability to refuse or renegotiate—not only through diplomatic language.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Scientific channels can remain open during political conflict and create
  valuable communication or humanitarian links; the proposition does not deny
  these possibilities.
- Research security can protect people, knowledge and critical infrastructure,
  but broad restrictions may reduce academic freedom, Global South access and
  shared problem-solving.
- A facility may generate excellent science while producing limited local
  employment, capability or public benefit; scientific output and territorial
  value are separate clocks.
- A partnership can be formally reciprocal but practically unequal because of
  funding, language, visas, equipment, IP, data or publication asymmetries.
- Geopolitical influence may be real without being legitimate or durable. The
  atlas records leverage, legitimacy, benefit and autonomy separately.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official sources show rapid
institutionalisation of science diplomacy, strategic tension between openness
and security, and mechanisms for cooperative and Track 2 engagement. Promote as
**Tier 2 conversion question**: science-diplomacy-to-reciprocal-cooperation-and-
geopolitical-trust, requiring named evidence from partnership design through
capacity, public-good effect, crisis use, benefit distribution and later
behaviour.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | dialogue, framework, agreement, facility, network, fellowship or shared data initiative | Official evidence is strong for policy expansion and strategic attention |
| Mechanism | access, reciprocity, finance, data, security, visas, interpretation, governance and sustained contact | Mechanisms are documented; partner-level incidence varies |
| Operation | named partners use the channel or facility to conduct research, solve a problem or manage a risk | Examples exist; comparable cross-case operating records are limited |
| Material consequence | local capability, health, environmental protection, crisis response, standard or public-good delivery | Same-place downstream effects are rarely joined to diplomacy records |
| Meaning | trust, dignity, recognition, epistemic respect, legitimacy and belonging | Statements and participation are visible; lived legitimacy is under-measured |
| Power | agenda setting, autonomy, refusal, benefit, leverage, alignment and durable policy behaviour | Strategic intent is explicit; reciprocal realized power remains open |

## Next test

Select three contrasting cases: a shared research facility, a cross-border
global-commons partnership and a Track 2 scientific network operating amid
political tension. For each, trace one partner institution and one affected
locality from agenda setting through funding, access, data, training, output,
public-good use, crisis communication, benefit sharing and later diplomatic or
policy behaviour. Record who could set the agenda, who retained the data and
infrastructure, what local capability remained after funding, and whether the
relationship survived a political shock.

## Named facility, capability and regional-trust refresh

### 7. SESAME: an operating shared facility across political divides

SESAME is an intergovernmental synchrotron in Jordan established under
UNESCO's auspices and modelled partly on CERN. Its full members include
Cyprus, Egypt, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Pakistan, Palestine and Türkiye. CERN
reports that the facility opened to users in 2017, Iraq later joined as an
associate member, and an initial Iraqi proposal linked the Natural History
Museum in Iraq with the British Museum to study geological formations. A 2025
SESAME publication reports more than 1,000 proposals from 45 countries and
growing peer-reviewed output.

This closes more than a meeting: it shows a shared facility, membership
architecture, access and scientific output operating across geopolitical
divides. It does not establish equal beam-time access, local retention of
skills, national research autonomy, community benefit, crisis use or a change
in diplomatic behaviour. Output and cooperation are real, but trust and
territorial public value remain separate measures.

Sources: [CERN on SESAME and Iraq](https://home.cern/sesame-growing-focal-point-regional-scientific-collaboration/) and [SESAME 2025 operating results](https://sunstone.sesame.org.jo/news-events/nuclear-physics-news-features-sesame-science-without-borders/).

### 8. ICTP: a long-running capability channel with alumni evidence

UNESCO describes the International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Trieste
as a joint UNESCO–Italy–IAEA initiative created in 1964 to strengthen scientific
expertise, particularly where researchers lacked access to continuing
education. UNESCO reports that over the decades ICTP provided scientists from
developing countries with continuing education and skills, and that many alumni
went on to teach at universities, lead research centres and contribute to
science in their countries.

This is a stronger capability signal than a one-off fellowship because it links
training to alumni roles and institutional leadership over time. The public
account still does not provide comparable country-level counterfactuals, funding
and visa access, retention versus migration, agenda power, gender or language
distribution, or evidence that alumni relationships altered later diplomacy.

Source: [UNESCO, cooperation through science diplomacy](https://www.unesco.org/en/scientific-research-cooperation-why-collaborate-science-benefits-and-examples?hub=171411).

### 9. Open Quantum Institute: inclusive global-public-good design before outcome

CERN describes the Open Quantum Institute as a multilateral science-diplomacy
initiative designed and incubated with about 180 experts from academia,
technology, diplomacy, philanthropy and civil society. Its pilot focuses on
SDG-related quantum-computing use cases in health, energy, climate, clean water
and food security, with an explicit ambition to include underserved regions.

This is a useful emerging-technology case because the problem selection and
access question are built into the diplomatic design rather than added after
deployment. It remains pre-outcome evidence: selected use cases, access
arrangements, computational capability, local ownership, usable public goods
and geopolitical trust have not yet been demonstrated at scale.

Source: [CERN, Open Quantum Institute pilot](https://home.cern/open-quantum-institute-launches-its-pilot-phase-cern/).

### 10. UNESCO Levant science-diplomacy project: geography and incompleteness are explicit

UNESCO's project portal lists an ongoing 2026–2027 science-diplomacy project
covering Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, Iraq and Palestine. Its stated purpose is to
consolidate science-diplomacy capacity for peace, human rights and sustainable
development; the portal reports a USD 4,614 budget, zero incurred expenditure
and 0% progress at the stated benchmark.

This is important negative evidence against premature success language. The
project names a conflict-sensitive geography, youth and gender priorities, but
the current record closes only intent, scope and administrative design. It does
not establish participant access, safe contact, research output, local
capability, public-good delivery, crisis communication or post-project trust.

Source: [UNESCO Core Data Portal, Levant science-diplomacy project](https://core.unesco.org/en/project/A210293031).

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for the rise of science diplomacy, its strategic
and cooperative forms, the tension between openness and security, and examples
of scientific channels supporting dialogue. It is weaker for comparable,
same-place evidence that partnerships created reciprocal local capability,
shared public-good outcomes or durable geopolitical trust. This remains a Tier 2
research opportunity, not a promoted causal finding.
