# Rotation FI — Post-quantum cryptography, secure continuity and digital trust

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 post-quantum-migration-to-secure-continuity-and-equitable-digital-trust conversion question  
**Research lane:** post-quantum cryptography / cryptographic agility / critical infrastructure / public services / long-lived data / standards / supply chains / interoperability / geopolitical technology / digital trust / equitable transition

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers cyber service continuity, cloud sovereignty, digital identity,
space weather and geopolitical technology. This packet isolates a different
conversion: whether organizations can migrate from quantum-vulnerable
cryptography before it is broken, while preserving authentication, privacy,
interoperability, service continuity and public trust.

Standards published, algorithms selected, inventories created, migration dates,
hybrid pilots, vendor claims, compliance mandates or quantum-threat headlines
are important signals. They do not by themselves establish that a named service
can upgrade safely, that old records remain confidential, that devices and
vendors interoperate, or that people retain access and remedy during transition.

## Research question

When governments, firms and critical-infrastructure operators migrate to
post-quantum cryptography, under what conditions do they maintain secure and
accessible services, protect long-lived data, preserve interoperability and
local capability, and distribute migration cost and security benefit fairly
across regions, institutions and users?

## Conversion chain

```text
quantum threat / harvest-now-decrypt-later / vulnerable cryptography
  -> inventory, prioritization, standards, procurement and crypto agility
  -> algorithm replacement, hybrid operation, testing and interoperability
  -> authenticated data, secure transactions, service continuity and recovery
  -> user confidence, privacy, institutional capability and remedy
  -> geopolitical resilience, standards influence and durable digital trust
```

The minimum unit is a named system, cryptographic asset, protocol, device,
vendor, data class, service, user group and time period. A migration roadmap or
algorithm standard is a capability signal—not proof that a specific workload,
device or resident-facing service completed a safe transition.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Standards are available, but migration is an operational project

NIST says three post-quantum standards are ready for implementation and urges
organizations to identify vulnerable algorithms and plan replacement across
products, services and protocols. The standards create a common technical
baseline; they do not prove inventory completeness, implementation quality or
user-facing continuity.

Source: [NIST, Post-quantum cryptography](https://www.nist.gov/pqc).

### 2. The threat date is uncertain, but stored data may be exposed now

NIST’s migration guidance describes “harvest now, decrypt later”: adversaries
can collect encrypted data today for possible future decryption. NIST’s 2026
transition roadmap sets federal milestones for high-value and high-impact
systems while emphasizing that migration differs by organization and use case.
This supports early prioritization without pretending that a cryptographically
relevant quantum computer has a settled arrival date.

Source: [NIST, Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/10/nist-report-provides-guidelines-transition-post-quantum-cryptography).

### 3. Crypto agility is the bridge between standards and continuity

NIST’s crypto-agility guidance defines the capability to replace algorithms in
protocols, applications, software, hardware, firmware and infrastructure while
preserving security and ongoing operations. The migration problem therefore
includes architecture, testing, dependency mapping, rollback and governance,
not only choosing an algorithm.

Source: [NIST, Considerations for Achieving Crypto Agility](https://csrc.nist.gov/News/2025/considerations-for-achieving-crypto-agility).

### 4. Migration is an interoperability and supply-chain problem

NIST’s migration project works with industry, standards organizations and
customer organizations on discovery, prioritization, interoperability and
performance. NIST also identifies HQC as a backup algorithm to ML-KEM, showing
that standardization and algorithm diversity remain active rather than fully
closed.

Sources: [NIST PQC project](https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/Post-Quantum-Cryptography) and [NIST HQC announcement](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2025/03/nist-selects-hqc-fifth-algorithm-post-quantum-encryption).

### 5. Quantum capability is geopolitical as well as technical

The OECD’s 2025 science and technology outlook describes quantum technologies
as a matter of geopolitical contention and links them to dual-use technology,
defense policy and strategic cooperation. Standards, talent, components,
cryptographic libraries and procurement can therefore shape dependence and
sovereignty across states and firms.

Source: [OECD, Science, Technology and Innovation Outlook 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/10/oecd-science-technology-and-innovation-outlook-2025_bae3698d/full-report/reconfiguring-scientific-co-operation-in-a-changing-geopolitical-environment_73f32116.html).

### 6. The transition creates unequal operational burdens

NIST’s migration materials make clear that organizations must discover where
cryptography is used and replace or update products, services and protocols.
Large institutions may have inventories, staff and procurement leverage; small
providers, municipalities, hospitals, schools and legacy-device owners may not.
The public record supports a capacity and interoperability mechanism, but not a
claim that benefits or costs are equally distributed.

Source: [NIST NCCoE migration FAQ](https://pages.nist.gov/nccoe-migration-post-quantum-cryptography/).

## Bounded proposition 165

**A post-quantum standard, migration roadmap, cryptographic inventory, algorithm selection, hybrid deployment, vendor assurance, compliance deadline or quantum-readiness claim is not by itself secure continuity, privacy protection, interoperability, accessible digital service, supply-chain resilience, equitable transition or durable public trust.** Algorithms standardized, systems inventoried, certificates issued, products updated or milestones announced do not establish that named workloads, devices and users can authenticate, transact, communicate and recover safely across legacy dependencies, failures, vendor changes, algorithm revisions or unequal migration capacity.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Security is a time-and-place relationship.** Data that must remain confidential for decades has a different priority from a short-lived transaction; rural or low-capacity institutions may face different migration barriers from national agencies.
2. **Trust is invisible until failure.** Users rarely see cryptographic transitions, but experience them through login, payment, identity, health, education and communication success or failure.
3. **Standards can create inclusion or exclusion.** New devices, certificates, bandwidth, software and support may improve protection while making older equipment, low-resource providers or offline users harder to serve.
4. **Crypto agility is institutional memory.** Asset inventories, dependency maps and skilled operators determine whether a future algorithm change is routine or disruptive.
5. **Geopolitics enters through standards and supply chains.** Algorithm choices, hardware, libraries, certification and talent shape strategic dependence and interoperability among states.
6. **Equity requires migration visibility.** A national completion percentage can hide particular hospitals, municipalities, languages, disability-access systems or community networks that remain vulnerable.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A published standard may be secure in theory while implementation, random-number generation, key management or side-channel protection fails.
- A hybrid cryptographic mode can hedge algorithm risk while increasing computation, latency, complexity and failure modes.
- An inventory can be complete for owned systems but miss suppliers, embedded devices, certificates, backups or long-lived archives.
- An aggressive deadline can reduce exposure while causing rushed migrations, service outages or exclusion of small vendors.
- A quantum-safe product claim can improve procurement confidence while concealing proprietary lock-in or weak interoperability.
- A system can be cryptographically upgraded while users still lack accessible recovery, identity correction or privacy remedy.
- A future quantum threat may arrive later than forecast; that uncertainty does not erase the present confidentiality, migration and supply-chain questions.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for standards readiness, harvest-now-
decrypt-later exposure, crypto agility, interoperability, supply-chain and
geopolitical mechanisms. Keep the integrated post-quantum-migration-to-secure-
continuity-and-equitable-digital-trust outcome at **Tier 2** because public
sources rarely follow one workload and user group through inventory, migration,
failure, recovery, accessibility, cost and long-term trust.

## Next test

Select one long-lived public record or critical service, one cryptographic
dependency chain and one small or resource-constrained operator. Map algorithms,

## Source boundary

The packet closes the standards, urgency, crypto-agility, interoperability,
supply-chain and geopolitical mechanisms. It does not close named workload
completion, user continuity, accessibility, privacy preservation, recovery,
small-operator capacity, vendor dependence, cost, algorithm failure, legal
conflict or durable digital trust. Those remain acquisition targets.

The fourth and sixth stages—material consequence and power—remain open across
most post-quantum sources. Standards and roadmaps are kept separate from safe,
equitable, user-visible continuity.
