# Rotation EH — Urban tree canopy, cooling and greening justice

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction; Tier 2 later-outcome conversion question  
**Research lane:** urban ecology / heat / public health / public space / land value

## Why this rotation

The atlas covers extreme heat, public space, urban ecosystem adaptation and
stormwater. This rotation narrows the unit to urban trees and canopy: planting,
survival, shade, evapotranspiration, species, water, maintenance, safety,
street-vendor and worker exposure, biodiversity, air quality and land value.

Tree programmes can create real cooling, health, biodiversity and social benefits.
They can also fail through mortality, poor species selection, water stress,
root damage, weak maintenance, uneven distribution, policing, rent increases or
green gentrification. The meaningful outcome is not tree count or canopy target;
it is durable shade and wellbeing for named people and places.

## Research question

When a city, developer, utility, donor or community plants trees, creates green
corridors, restores parks or funds urban forestry, under what conditions does it
produce durable cooling, health, biodiversity, mobility, public-space use and
climate resilience without shifting water, maintenance, safety, rent or
displacement burdens onto less powerful residents?

## Conversion chain

```text
heat / pollution / runoff / biodiversity loss / shade and public-space need
  -> tree planting, species, design, water, land, maintenance and governance
  -> street, park, corridor, building, worker and household encounter
  -> canopy survival, shade, radiant heat, air, water, use, cost and access result
  -> health, work, mobility, belonging, ecology, resilience and land value
  -> stewardship, tenure, maintenance power, accountability and continuity
```

The unit must be a named street, park, corridor, tree cohort, household,
worker, school, vendor, species or neighbourhood, with canopy, shade, radiant
temperature, survival, maintenance, water and outcome dates. Planting counts,
canopy targets, modelled cooling or park creation are intermediate signals, not
proof of lived cooling, health or justice.

## Evidence snapshot

### 1. Trees can produce local cooling and economic benefits

World Bank analysis finds that even small increases in urban tree canopy can
matter, while large-scale efforts can be transformative. Cooling depends on
canopy density, built form and wind; street trees can create meaningful local
temperature differences. A Medellín green-corridor programme reportedly planted
8,800 trees and reduced local temperatures by about 2°C while training people
from disadvantaged backgrounds to care for green spaces.

These are promising mechanisms and named cases, but reported city or programme
results do not close same-household heat exposure, health, maintenance or
distributional outcomes.

Sources: [World Bank urban heat and tree-canopy evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099091925132542945/pdf/P504085-d53bd008-c83a-446f-9f07-0fc363794cfb.pdf)
and [UNEP urban ecosystem adaptation](https://www.unep.org/en/what-we-do/cities/cities-nature/urban-ecosystem-based-adaptation).

### 2. Cooling is highly local and depends on design

World Bank evidence emphasizes that canopy quality, size, density, leaf traits,
spacing and distance from people affect cooling. A Johannesburg study notes that
tree cooling is often local, especially through shade, and that wider air
cooling can dilute rapidly unless vegetation is extensive and strategically
placed.

The outcome should therefore be measured at the pedestrian, dwelling, school,
vendor and worker level, including radiant temperature and shade duration—not
only citywide air temperature.

Source: [World Bank Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni urban heat evidence](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099030626090162085/pdf/P173517-42ab-40eb-87d2-79eaa418dc01.pdf).

### 3. Urban green-space interventions can improve health but evidence needs equity monitoring

WHO’s review finds that urban green-space interventions can deliver positive
health, social and environmental outcomes, particularly for lower socioeconomic
groups. It also identifies a need for better inclusion of health and equity
outcomes, local management monitoring and community engagement.

This is a useful distinction between potential benefit and verified conversion:
green space may exist without repeated safe use, shade, health improvement or
resident control.

Source: [WHO urban green-space interventions and health](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/m/item/urban-green-space-interventions-and-health--a-review-of-impacts-and-effectiveness.-full-report).

### 4. Maintenance, species and water are the long clock

World Bank urban-forestry evidence identifies species selection, survival during
the first year, pruning, root competition, infrastructure conflicts and community
support as major implementation challenges. Trees can fail from drought, poor
soil, pests, vandalism, construction or absent budgets; mature canopy benefits
may take years.

The conversion test must follow cohorts after planting and account for watering,
maintenance, shade continuity, safety and replacement—not treat planted trees as
surviving trees.

Source: [World Bank green-infrastructure and urban-heat evidence](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/e439ea308b9e83f47648a01f049f5845-0090012024/original/P177113003ae610c809ca607b855651972e.pdf).

### 5. Greening can connect ecology, public space and regeneration

UN-Habitat’s 2025 nature-based-solutions work frames urban regeneration as a
co-governed process linking climate risk, biodiversity, inequality, jobs, health,
drainage, mobility and public space. It recommends multi-scale planning and
community-level data to manage trade-offs.

The positive pathway is real, but regeneration can also raise land value,
increase rent, restrict informal livelihoods or make the original residents
unable to enjoy the improved environment. Anti-displacement and tenure must be
part of the greening design.

Source: [UN-Habitat nature-based solutions in urban regeneration](https://unhabitat.org/nature-based-solutions-in-urban-regeneration).

### 6. Equity must guide heat-resilience placement

World Bank heat-resilience work notes that marginalized communities often face
converging risks from income inequality, inadequate housing, health-care access
and lack of shade or cooling. It presents Medellín as an example of targeting
green corridors in low-income neighbourhoods, while cautioning that adaptation
choices can create different benefits and emissions burdens.

The conversion question is whether tree canopy reaches people with the highest
heat exposure and least ability to buy cooling, and whether they retain access
when land and amenities become more valuable.

Source: [World Bank heat vulnerabilities in marginalized communities](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099091325152516228/pdf/P177201-38bce580-6d47-40eb-87d2-79eaa418dc01.pdf).

## Bounded proposition 139

**A tree-planting programme, canopy target, green corridor, urban forest,
park-restoration project, cooling plan, biodiversity initiative or nature-based
regeneration project is not by itself durable cooling, lower heat illness,
health equity, biodiversity recovery, safe public-space use, livelihood support
or greening justice.** Trees planted, canopy percentage, modelled temperature
reduction, park area, survival claims, maintenance contracts, jobs created or
community consultation do not establish that named residents, workers, vendors,
children and older people experienced sustained shade and lower radiant heat,
better health, affordable access, surviving canopy, protected income, retained
tenure, equal maintenance or remedy when greening changed land value or safety.

## Cultural, geographical and social interpretation

1. **Canopy is a daily service.** Shade changes walking, waiting, selling,
   working, caregiving, play and social life at particular hours.
2. **Trees are place relationships.** Species, water, soil, memory, ecology,
   street design and cultural meaning matter as much as count.
3. **Maintenance is distributed power.** Residents, workers and municipalities
   decide who waters, prunes, repairs, protects and benefits from trees.
4. **Greening can revalue land.** Cooling and amenity may improve wellbeing while
   increasing rents, surveillance or displacement.
5. **Cooling and biodiversity can conflict.** Water-intensive species, roots,
   allergens, invasive plants or dense vegetation may create new burdens if not
   matched to place and users.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- A tree count can rise while survival and canopy remain low.
- Trees can cool a street while roots damage sidewalks, pipes or accessibility.
- Dense planting can provide shade and habitat while increasing allergens,
  perceived insecurity or maintenance costs.
- A green corridor can improve health and public space while accelerating rent
  increases and displacement.
- Watering trees can protect canopy while competing with household, ecosystem or
  drought needs.
- Canopy equity measured by neighbourhood average can miss shade at bus stops,
  schools, informal markets, care routes and worker locations.

## Evidence status and promotion decision

Promote to **Tier 1 bounded distinction** for the proposition. WHO, UNEP,
UN-Habitat and World Bank sources support the cooling, health, biodiversity,
maintenance, equity and regeneration mechanisms. Keep the integrated outcome at
**Tier 2** because public sources rarely follow the same tree cohort and people
through survival, shade, health, maintenance, land value, tenure and remedy.

## Next test

Compare one targeted low-income canopy programme, one high-amenity regeneration
project and one under-canopied comparison area. Follow named streets, bus stops,
schools, vendors, workers, households and tree cohorts through hot and rainy
seasons, including maintenance interruption and rent or land-value change.
