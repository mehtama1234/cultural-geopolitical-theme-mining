# Rotation BZ — Small-scale fisheries, blue economy and coastal livelihood security

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Lane:** small-scale fisheries / blue economy / coastal and island communities / food security / marine conservation / gender / climate resilience / cultural livelihoods  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 ocean-governance-to-coastal-livelihood question

## Research question

When marine conservation, fisheries management, blue-economy investment, climate
adaptation and fisheries-improvement programs are introduced, what converts
policy and capital into secure access to fishing grounds, food, income, women’s
value-chain work, cultural continuity, ecosystem health and legitimate community
authority?

## Conversion chain

```text
fish stocks, coastal ecosystem, climate hazard and community dependence
  -> fisheries rule, protected area, market/traceability, blue-economy investment or adaptation project
  -> tenure/access, co-management, landing/cold chain, finance, safety, skills, value-chain participation and enforcement
  -> catch, food availability, income, fair prices, safety, women’s work and ecosystem recovery
  -> cultural continuity, nutrition, dignity, resilience and legitimate ocean governance
  -> durable coastal livelihood and marine sustainability
```

## Evidence snapshot

| Source | Direct observation | Stage closed | Stage still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| FAO Sustainable Small-Scale Fisheries gateway | Small-scale fisheries contribute about 40% of global catch and support 90% of the capture-fisheries workforce; about 500 million people rely on them, while declining stocks, sector competition and weak voice persist. | global scale, livelihood/food mechanism and governance gap | named community access, catch, price, nutrition, safety and ecosystem outcome |
| FAO Asia-Pacific 2025 progress | Small-scale fisheries support about 46 million people in Asia-Pacific, with women representing 40% of participants and the sector contributing nearly half of regional catch; climate and policy/finance gaps remain. | regional economic, gender, food, culture and resilience framing | distribution of value, women’s control, climate adaptation, rights and lasting livelihood |
| FAO SSF Guidelines food-security evidence | Fish from small-scale fisheries supplies protein and micronutrients and is critical to local food systems, but marginalized communities do not fully realize these benefits. | nutrition pathway and inclusion problem | local affordability, distribution, household consumption, market/export trade-off and health |
| UNDP Indonesia seaBLUE | A 2025–2026 project in remote Morotai and Tanimbar combines community-driven solutions, clean technologies, gender inequality, food security, income and inclusive governance. | named blue-economy intervention and intended mechanism | trained fisher adoption, income, food, gender power, ecosystem and durable local ownership |
| UNDP Barbados Coastal Fisheries Resilience Project | Project launched after Hurricane Beryl to support livelihood recovery and disaster preparedness in coastal fishing communities. | named climate-resilience intervention and recovery objective | restored catch/income, access, insurance, safety, ecosystem, women’s work and community governance |

## Stage audit

- **Signal:** fish-stock decline, warming, storms, coastal erosion, pollution,
  conservation restrictions, competition, market volatility and dependence on
  marine food systems affect coastal communities.
- **Mechanism:** tenure and access rights, gear, safety, weather information,
  landing sites, cold chain, credit, fuel, market power, processing, gendered
  value-chain work, enforcement, customary governance and ecosystem health
  determine who benefits.
- **Operation:** fishers and fish workers can access grounds legally and safely,
  land and process catch, obtain finance and equipment, sell at fair terms,
  participate in rules, receive warnings/insurance and adapt without losing
  food, culture or authority.
- **Material consequence:** catch, income, prices, nutrition, occupational
  safety, debt, migration, women’s earnings, ecosystem function and community
  resilience can change separately.
- **Meaning:** fishing is livelihood, food, identity, kinship, seasonal rhythm,
  customary law and place attachment. A marine protected area or blue-economy
  project can be experienced as stewardship, dispossession, opportunity or
  exclusion depending on who decides and who receives value.
- **Power:** states, industrial fleets, tourism, ports, conservation bodies,
  investors, seafood buyers, donors, customary authorities, fisher organizations
  and women processors distribute access, data, finance, enforcement and remedy.

## Bounded finding

> **A marine-protection measure, fisheries agreement or blue-economy investment
> is not coastal livelihood security.** A quota, protected area, traceability
> platform, resilience grant, training program, clean-technology package or
> conservation target does not establish that named fishers and fish workers have
> secure access, safe work, fair income, affordable nutritious food, women’s
> value-chain power, cultural continuity, ecosystem recovery or meaningful voice
> in ocean governance.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

The relevant unit is the fishing system, not only the boat. It includes gleaners,
crew, processors, traders, transporters, vendors, household consumers, women’s
work, youth, customary authorities, landing sites and the sea/shore ecology. A
catch statistic can rise while prices, debt, food access or women’s control
decline. Export growth can coexist with local nutrition loss.

Geography matters at the coast, landing site, market and marine boundary. A
protected area can protect biodiversity but displace customary access; a digital
traceability requirement can open premium markets while excluding fishers
without connectivity or formal documents. Climate adaptation may need boats,
insurance, early warning, mangrove protection, alternative income and safe
harbors—not only a stock-management rule.

## Counterexamples and limits

- A sustainable catch or stock indicator does not prove fair prices, household
  nutrition, safe work or women’s income.
- Marine conservation can improve ecological indicators while restricting
  customary fishing or shifting effort and hardship to poorer communities.
- Blue-economy investment can create enterprises while concentrating ownership,
  displacing small operators or increasing dependence on external buyers.
- Training or clean technology does not prove adoption if credit, spare parts,
  fuel, safety, weather, landing infrastructure or market access are missing.
- Export value can rise while local fish availability and affordability fall.
- Participation in a co-management meeting is not decision power, tenure security,
  benefit sharing or an effective complaint route.
- A storm-recovery grant may restore boats without restoring reefs, mangroves,
  insurance, household debt capacity or cultural continuity.

## Promotion decision

Promote the distinction to Tier 1: ecological signal, rule, access/tenure,
co-management, infrastructure, finance, safety, catch, market, nutrition,
gendered value-chain work, ecosystem outcome, culture, remedy and authority are
separate stages. Keep ocean-governance-to-coastal-livelihood claims in Tier 2
until a named community/cohort ledger joins intervention, access, catch, price,
food, safety, gender, ecosystem and later resilience outcomes.

## Next test

Select one remote island fishery, one urbanizing coastal landing site and one
community inside or adjacent to a marine protected area. Record customary and
legal tenure, species/season, rules, access, catch, effort, price, debt, fuel,
safety, weather warning, landing/cold chain, processing and women’s work,
household consumption, conservation effects, climate losses, finance, local
authority, grievance and 6/12/24-month livelihood and ecosystem outcomes.
Compare formal and informal fish workers and people excluded from official catch
data.
