# Rotation HE — climate school continuity, learning and child agency

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question; distinct from general school attendance, climate relocation and climate mental-health packets  
**Research lane:** climate hazards, school closure, heat-safe learning, infrastructure, displacement, learning continuity, dropout, teachers, child voice and education recovery

## Why this rotation exists

Climate disruption is often counted as a school closure or a number of affected
students. That signal matters, but the lived chain continues through shortened
school days, unsafe classrooms, damaged buildings, transport, household work and
care burdens, migration, remote-learning access, teacher capacity, reenrolment,
learning loss and child agency. This packet isolates whether education systems
keep children learning and connected before, during and after climate shocks.

## Research question

When heat, floods, storms, drought, wildfire, haze or displacement disrupt
schooling, under what conditions do resilient infrastructure, anticipatory action,
teacher support, safe schedules, remote or alternative learning, social
protection and reenrolment convert a closure or shortened school day into
continued learning, belonging, progression and child voice rather than dropout,
learning loss, unsafe attendance or unequal digital exclusion?

## Conversion chain

```text
climate hazard / forecast / damaged school / unsafe heat or air / displacement
  -> closure, shortened timetable, absence, disrupted transport or household coping
  -> safe infrastructure, anticipatory action, teacher/community support, continuity route and protection
  -> child reaches a usable learning environment and remains connected to school
  -> learning, progression, belonging, agency and recovery persist after the shock
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. UNICEF's 2026 global snapshot reports that at least 171 million students
   experienced climate-related school disruptions in 2025 across 106 countries
   or territories; 75% were in low- and lower-middle-income countries. The
   estimate establishes scale and inequality, not same-child learning loss or
   intervention effect.
2. UNICEF and Save the Children's 2026–2027 El Niño guidance calls for climate
   risk in education planning, identification of schools and learners at risk,
   contingency and learning-continuity arrangements, forecast triggers,
   coordination and financing, including response and recovery. Guidance is a
   mechanism, not proof of implementation or learning recovery.
3. UNICEF's Eastern and Southern Africa assessment estimates that climate
   disruptions affected 130 million children between 2005 and 2024, with direct
   education-system damage and projected lifetime-income losses. Regional loss
   estimates reveal distributional stakes but are not an individual child's
   measured learning trajectory.
4. World Bank education analysis describes direct pathways from heat, floods,
   storms, wildfire and other hazards to unsafe learning environments, school
   destruction or temporary closure, and indirect pathways through health,
   nutrition, food security, economic shocks, conflict, migration and
   displacement. It recommends resilience planning, infrastructure, continuity
   and teachers/students as change agents.
5. UNICEF's Malaysia 2026 haze account shows why closure is not the endpoint:
   air pollution closed schools across several states, while children at home
   still needed safety, care and learning support. This is a concrete case of
   health-protective closure with unresolved home-learning and household-capacity
   questions.
6. UNESCO education-in-emergencies evidence identifies displacement, language,
   documentation, damaged schools, overcrowding and teacher/material shortages
   as barriers. Climate continuity therefore has cultural and geographical
   dimensions beyond infrastructure alone.

## Bounded proposition

**A climate-related school closure, shortened timetable, damaged classroom,
heat-safe schedule, contingency plan, remote-learning platform, teacher training,
learning kit, reopened school, enrolment, attendance count or climate-finance
commitment is not by itself learning continuity, progression, safety, belonging,
child agency, equitable access, recovery or future income.** A closure can protect
health while interrupting learning; reopening can occur with unsafe heat, damaged
facilities or absent teachers; and remote provision can preserve contact for some
children while excluding those without devices, connectivity, quiet space,
language access, disability support or adult help.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

School is simultaneously a learning site, safety route, meal and health access
point, social world, language community and pathway into future work. Climate
disruption can break seasonal rhythms, exams, ceremonies, peer relationships,
care arrangements and trust in institutions. A rural flood, urban heatwave,
island cyclone, wildfire evacuation or haze closure creates different routes and
meanings.

Child agency means more than asking children about climate. It includes whether
children can influence safe schedules, communication, recovery priorities and
learning formats, while adults retain responsibility for protection and system
repair. Education continuity should not shift infrastructure and adaptation
burdens onto children, teachers or caregivers.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Closure can be the correct health and safety decision; it is not automatically
  an education failure.
- Reopening can restore routine while exposing children to heat, smoke, unsafe
  buildings or transport risk.
- Remote learning can preserve continuity for some learners but widen inequality
  where connectivity, devices, electricity, language or disability support fail.
- Attendance is not learning, and learning loss is not identical to dropout,
  wellbeing loss or lifetime-income loss.
- School infrastructure investment can reduce risk, but maintenance, water,
  cooling, teacher capacity and household affordability determine use.
- Aggregate disruption estimates can conceal repeated shocks, children already
  out of school, informal learning, migration, gendered care work and children
  with disabilities.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official evidence supports the scale
and geographical inequality of climate school disruption, direct and indirect
pathways, anticipatory continuity mechanisms, and system-level loss and damage.
Promote as **Tier 2 conversion question**:
climate-disruption-to-learning-continuity-and-child-agency, requiring named
school/community/cohort records through exposure, safety, continuity, learning,
belonging, progression, recovery and remedy.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | hazard, forecast, closure, timetable change, damage or displacement | Global scale and hazard pattern are documented |
| Mechanism | resilient infrastructure, early action, teacher support, continuity, finance and protection | Guidance and investment routes exist; implementation varies |
| Operation | named school/teacher/child uses a safe learning route during and after shock | Closure and affected-student counts exist; same-child routes are limited |
| Material consequence | learning, attendance, progression, health, safety, meals, care and reduced dropout | Longitudinal child-level evidence is sparse |
| Meaning | belonging, language, identity, dignity, hope and child agency | Cultural and child-voice principles are visible; durable meaning is under-measured |
| Power | children/teachers/families shape recovery, data, schedule, resource allocation and remedy | Participation is documented; realized decision power and remedy remain open |

## Next test

Trace three place-bound cohorts: a heat-affected urban school, a flood/cyclone-
affected rural or island school and a displaced or haze-affected community.
Follow forecast/impact → closure or shortened day → transport, infrastructure,
teacher, device, meal and care route → learning contact and safety → attendance,
assessment and wellbeing → reenrolment/progression → recovery investment and
complaint. Compare children with and without continuity access, preserving
gender, disability, language, income, migration and geography.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for global disruption scale, unequal geography,
loss-and-damage framing, direct and indirect education pathways, anticipatory
continuity guidance and system-resilience mechanisms. It is weaker for
comparable longitudinal evidence joining a named child or cohort to safe
learning access, actual learning, belonging, progression, family burden, agency,
dropout prevention and remedy across repeated climate shocks. This remains a
Tier 2 research opportunity, not a promoted causal finding.
