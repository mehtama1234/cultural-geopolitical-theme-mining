# Geopolitical and state-power subtheme synthesis v1

**Checked:** 2026-09-22  
**Scope:** state capacity, alliances, defense, infrastructure dependence,
public legitimacy, and external response  
**Status:** broad cross-source synthesis; not a measure of national power

## The subtheme

The current geopolitical evidence shows that state power runs on several
different clocks. A government can announce a commitment, fund a project,
buy equipment, accept a system, operate it, and still not have demonstrated
deterrence, public consent, replaceability, or influence over another actor.

The working chain is:

```text
commitment or investment
  -> procurement, staffing, construction, or agreement
  -> accepted and operating capability
  -> local and domestic legitimacy
  -> alliance cooperation or public durability
  -> changed external or adversary behavior
```

The evidence is strongest in the middle—formal commitment, operation,
staffing, and coordination—and weakest at the final step: proof that another
actor changed its choices.

### Export controls have an implementation clock before a leverage clock

The [GAO advanced-semiconductor export-control record](records/us-gao-advanced-semiconductor-export-control-compliance-2022-2024.json)
adds a measured implementation stage to the technology-dependence route. GAO's
review of three 2022–23 advanced-semiconductor and manufacturing-equipment
rules found Commerce working with six other agencies on technical review,
licensing, foreign-partner outreach, border verification, investigations, and
enforcement. Companies updated compliance programs and self-reported potential
violations; GAO identified ten reported compliance challenges, including rule
clarity, and documented BIS feedback, definition changes, briefings, seminars,
and later rule updates.

This is stronger than a control announcement but still stops before strategic
effect. The review does not establish foreign-partner adoption, Chinese
substitution, a change in advanced-computing capability, domestic incidence,
deterrence, or U.S. leverage. The next record should join one named control to
partner alignment, shipment or substitution behavior, and an independently
attributed change in the other actor's choices.

The [BIS foreign-fab VEU record](records/us-bis-foreign-fab-veu-export-control-closure-2025.json)
adds a sharper control-to-capability boundary. On August 29, 2025, BIS stated
that foreign-owned semiconductor fabs in China previously covered by the
Validated End-User route would need licenses; BIS intended to allow existing
fabs to operate, while not intending to license capacity expansion or
technology upgrades. That separates continuity of an existing facility from
permission to increase or modernize capability. It still does not show which
firms applied, which licenses were granted, whether shipments or production
changed, whether sourcing moved, or whether the policy changed PRC capability,
partner alignment, deterrence, or U.S. leverage.

The follow-up [BIS final-rule summary](https://www.bis.gov/90fr-42321-revocation-validated-end-user-authorizations-peoples-republic-china)
and Federal Register text make formal implementation more concrete: Intel
Semiconductor (Dalian), Samsung China Semiconductor, and SK hynix Semiconductor
(China) were removed from the VEU list effective December 31, 2025. This is a
verified authorization change, not proof that the named fabs stopped operating
or that the affected firms changed sourcing. Individual licenses, shipments,
production, and external response remain open.

Two company-side certification surfaces provide a bounded continuity signal
after the effective date. Samsung's Xi'an certificate lists 12-inch flash-memory
manufacturing, packaging/testing, and SSD assembly through October 14, 2026;
SK hynix's current quality page lists Wuxi 12-inch integrated-circuit
manufacturing through August 2, 2027. Neither source reports output, utilization,
individual license decisions, technology upgrades, or an effect attributable to
the VEU change. Formal authorization loss and certified operating scope can
therefore coexist without proving either shutdown or strategic continuity.

The [Chinese Ministry of Commerce response](https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/News/SpokesmansRemarks/art/2025/art_267bddaf8d52497289627453b3d5636a.html)
adds the first directly attributed external-response stage. China opposed the
VEU removals, warned of serious harm to global semiconductor supply-chain
stability, urged U.S. correction, and stated that it would take necessary
measures to protect its enterprises. The statement is a real diplomatic
response, but it does not identify an enacted countermeasure, changed shipment
or production pattern, negotiated concession, or strategic effect.

The [MOFCOM analog-IC investigation record](records/us-china-mofcom-analog-ic-antidumping-investigation-2025.json)
adds a formal institutional response in the broader semiconductor dispute. On
September 14, 2025, MOFCOM opened an anti-dumping investigation into specified
U.S. commodity-interface and gate-driver IC imports after a domestic-industry
application. The ministry reported preliminary applicant evidence of a 37%
cumulative import increase and 52% cumulative import-price decline from 2022 to
2024, but those figures are not final findings. This advances the chain from
official opposition to a legal trade-remedy process; it does not establish
dumping, a direct causal response to the VEU removals, final duties, firm
behavior, or strategic leverage.

The [MOFCOM anti-discrimination investigation record](records/us-china-mofcom-integrated-circuit-antidiscrimination-investigation-2025.json)
adds a broader formal legal channel. On September 19, 2025, China announced an
investigation under Articles 7, 36, and 37 of its Foreign Trade Law into U.S.
integrated-circuit measures, including export controls and Section 301
investigations, and invited affected parties to submit evidence. The formal
process advances state capacity and contestation beyond a press objection, but
the source does not establish a violation, remedy, direct VEU causation, or
changed firm or strategic behavior.

The [EU dual-use list record](records/us-eu-commission-semiconductor-dual-use-control-list-update-2025.json)
adds a partner-regulatory alignment stage. On September 8, 2025, the European
Commission adopted an EU common-list update covering semiconductor
manufacturing/testing equipment and materials and advanced-computing integrated
circuits and assemblies. The Commission grounded the update in multilateral
export-control regime decisions and member-state commitments. This is evidence
of formal list-level coordination, not proof that EU policy mirrors U.S. rules,
that member states enforced the controls, or that firms or external actors
changed behavior.

The Commission's later [implementation-report observation](records/us-eu-commission-semiconductor-dual-use-control-list-update-2025.json)
adds system-level enforcement visibility. For 2024, the Commission reports
€77.6 billion in EU-authorized dual-use exports and 487 transactions denied for
security risks; China represented 25% and the United States 21% of authorized
export value. These aggregate figures show an operating licensing system, but
not semiconductor-specific enforcement, U.S.-EU convergence, a list-update
effect, firm adaptation, or geopolitical leverage.

The underlying Commission dataset now supplies that missing category-level
visibility without closing the downstream arrow. It reports €721.1 million in
2024 individual authorizations for 3EC4 semiconductor manufacturing/testing
equipment across the listed Member State rows, totaling 156 authorizations;
Germany and Austria account for the largest reported values. This is stronger
than the aggregate implementation summary for measuring semiconductor-control
exposure, but it remains authorization data: destination, exporter, denial,
shipment completion, firm response, and strategic effect are not linked.

The same dataset reports a further implementation endpoint: 415 full-time-
equivalent staff directly involved in control administration, 154 outreach
events, 1,353 compliance audits, 270 reported infringements, and 144
administrative or criminal penalties/fines in 2024. These figures make the EU
control system more observable than the authorization totals alone, but they
are not 3EC4-specific and do not establish enforcement rates, deterrence,
remediation, or geopolitical leverage.

The [Applied Materials BIS settlement record](records/us-bis-applied-materials-semiconductor-equipment-settlement-2026.json)
adds a named firm-level endpoint. BIS reported that Applied Materials and
Applied Materials Korea routed ion implanters through Korea to China in
2021–22 without the required license for an Entity-Listed recipient; the
reported merchandise value was approximately $126 million and the penalty was
approximately $252 million. The settlement also requires multiple compliance
audits and annual certifications, and records personnel consequences. This
advances the chain from system capacity to violation, penalty, and remediation,
but it does not establish later audit completion, production or worker impact,
Chinese substitution, deterrence, or U.S. leverage.

The [Cadence BIS/DOJ enforcement record](records/us-bis-doj-cadence-eda-export-enforcement-2025.json)
adds a complementary design-tool route. BIS reported 56 admitted EAR
violations involving EDA hardware, software, and semiconductor design
technology valued at $45.3 million, while DOJ described at least 59 exports
through a PRC front company to NUDT and related entities. The coordinated
resolution included a $95 million BIS penalty, DOJ criminal penalties and
forfeiture, a net resolution above $140 million, account freezes, an export
hold, and compliance remediation. The agency counts are retained separately;
the case does not establish later capability change, future compliance,
deterrence, or leverage.

The [Alpha and Omega BIS order](records/us-bis-alpha-omega-huawei-power-semiconductor-settlement-2025.json)
adds a third semiconductor layer: power-semiconductor samples and related
items were sent from the United States to an AOS China facility for Huawei
testing and transfer after Huawei's Entity List addition. BIS records 15
unauthorized occasions involving approximately 1,650 items and a $4.25 million
civil penalty; timely payment was made a condition of export privileges, and
the order separately notes that the Digital Power business unit was eliminated
in 2021. The source does not attribute that business-unit outcome to the
enforcement action or establish Huawei capability change, future compliance, or
deterrence.

The [Bosch BIS settlement record](records/us-bis-bosch-foreign-produced-mems-huawei-settlement-2026.json)
adds a foreign-produced-item route. BIS reported that Germany-headquartered
Bosch exported approximately $72.4 million in MEMS sensors and automotive
software from abroad to Huawei and affiliates under the Foreign Direct Product
Rule without the required authorization. Bosch self-disclosed and cooperated;
the resolution included a $36.18 million BIS penalty and approximately $3.6
million in DOJ disgorgement, with a corresponding BIS credit. This extends the
enforcement chain across an allied firm's overseas production surface, but does
not establish later compliance, production or consumer effects, substitution,
deterrence, or leverage.

The [BIS H200/MI325X licensing record](records/us-bis-h200-mi325x-case-by-case-china-licensing-2026.json)
adds a conditional-opening stage after restrictive controls. BIS shifted
specified advanced-computing exports to China or Macau toward case-by-case
review, subject to U.S.-supply and foundry-capacity safeguards, a comparative
TPP ceiling, prohibited-end-use certifications, and independent U.S. testing.
The rule does not report an approved application, shipment, customer, or
capability result, so it shows policy conditionality rather than realized
market reopening or strategic leverage.

The [NVIDIA H200 license realization record](records/us-nvidia-h200-china-license-no-revenue-2026.json)
identifies the next administrative stage. NVIDIA disclosed that the U.S.
government granted a February 2026 license for small amounts of H200 products
to specific China-based customers, but reported no H200 revenue under the
program and no known authorization that imports would be allowed into China.
U.S. inspection before shipment and a 25% tariff condition remain part of the
route. This is evidence of license approval, not shipment, Chinese import
approval, capability transfer, or restored competition.

The [NVIDIA partial H200 shipment record](records/us-nvidia-h200-partial-china-shipments-prc-restriction-2026.json)
closes the next part of that chain. NVIDIA later reported that PRC restrictions
prevented all licensed sales, but that a fraction of allowed H200 shipments had
been made; those shipments were less than 1% of Data Center revenue in the
quarter. NVIDIA still described the China data-center market as effectively
foreclosed and included no China compute revenue in its forward outlook. This
is realized but commercially constrained delivery, not restored market access.

The [MOFCOM H200 non-confirmation record](records/us-china-mofcom-h200-shipment-nonconfirmation-2026.json)
adds the Chinese state-side evidence boundary. In a July 16, 2026 regular
press conference, a spokesperson was asked about U.S. claims that a small
number of H200s had been sold to China and said he did not know about the
reported situation. This does not negate NVIDIA's shipment report, but it
means Chinese official confirmation, import clearance, and domestic-use
authorization remain unobserved.

The [SAMR NVIDIA-Mellanox investigation continuity record](records/us-china-samr-nvidia-mellanox-investigation-continuity-2026.json)
adds a parallel Chinese regulatory clock. SAMR opened a further investigation
after a preliminary finding concerning NVIDIA's compliance with the conditions
attached to its Mellanox acquisition, and its April 2026 enforcement review
still listed the NVIDIA investigation. No final decision, penalty, or remedy is
reported in these sources, so the case is an open regulatory stage rather than
proof of completed enforcement or a causal explanation for H200 restrictions.

The [Hygon DCU competitive-adaptation record](records/us-china-hygon-dcu-h200-competition-adaptation-2025-2026.json)
adds a firm-response stage. Hygon described H200 entry as intensifying
domestic high-end-chip competition and said it would respond through
performance benchmarking, ecosystem compatibility, security compliance,
cost-performance, tailored submarkets, and customer reference cases. Hygon
also reported DCU use in important industry and internet applications and
expected 2026 share gains. These are company-reported adaptation and outlook
signals, not evidence of customer switching, displacement, or capability gain.

The [Hygon realized growth and deployment record](records/us-china-hygon-dcu-realized-growth-deployment-2026.json)
adds an observed firm endpoint. Hygon's first-half 2026 report records RMB
9.099 billion in consolidated revenue, up 66.52% year over year, and reports
DCU deployment across key industries and hundreds of application scenarios,
with adaptation to more than 400 mainstream large models. This establishes
realized domestic-firm growth and deployment, but not H200-driven substitution,
customer switching, or a measured capability effect.

The [Hygon large-model cluster realization record](records/us-china-hygon-dcu-large-model-cluster-realization-2026.json)
adds an operational-capability endpoint. Hygon reported preliminary stable
operation of a 10-trillion-parameter model on a cluster containing tens of
thousands of its DCUs, with a DTK software update integrating more than 2,000
operators and supporting more than 100 mainstream framework components. This
is a company-reported infrastructure milestone, not independent validation,
commercial durability, or evidence that NVIDIA capacity was displaced.

The [Hygon Kimi K3 adaptation record](records/us-china-hygon-dcu-kimi-k3-adaptation-deployment-verification-2026.json)
adds a model-specific realization stage. A 2026 listed-company interim report
states that Hygon completed full-process Kimi K3 adaptation, deployment, and
performance verification on its DCU platform. This is a second-party reported
ecosystem claim, not independent benchmarking, named-customer acceptance,
production-duration evidence, or proof of substitution.

The [Hygon Kimi K3 performance-claim record](records/us-china-hygon-dcu-kimi-k3-reported-12-percent-performance-loss-2026.json)
adds a quantitative but non-independent endpoint. A technical-news report
relayed an official Hygon measurement that performance loss on domestic cards
was within 12 percent and described Kimi K3 distributed training and
multi-card inference on a Sugon 8000 cluster using the Hygon hardware stack.
The baseline, metric, configuration, reproducibility, customer, utilization,
and production duration remain undisclosed.

The [Hunan public-system integration record](records/us-china-hunan-compute-platform-hygon-dcu-resource-integration-2026.json)
adds external acceptance beyond the vendor's own cluster report. Hunan's
provincial compute-scheduling platform entered trial operation, adapted to
Hygon DCU among multiple domestic accelerators, and reported first-batch
resource access by the Changsha National Supercomputing Center, operator
compute centers, and Changsha AI Innovation Center. This is public-system
integration, not quantified utilization, procurement, or substitution.

The [Changsha AI-center workload record](records/us-china-hunan-changsha-ai-center-hygon-dcu-model-deployment-2026.json)
adds named workload evidence. Hunan's 2026 AI-supply inventory describes a
200P, two-module Changsha AI Innovation Center: one Ascend-based module and a
second module based on Hygon DCU. The center is reported to support a visual
foundation model, an agricultural Q&A model, and a tourism model. Because the
architecture is mixed and workloads are attributed to the center, this shows
deployment context rather than Hygon-specific utilization or substitution.

**Meaning:** a control can narrow future capability while preserving present
operation. The transition clock is therefore a measurable state-capacity stage,
not evidence of strategic success. The relevant next observation is the named
fab or exporter after the license deadline, with production, sourcing,
investment, and external response kept separate.

## What the sources show

### Alliance support is not the same as support for a specific mission

NATO can remain broadly popular while support varies by country, party, cost,
mission, and perceived reliability of the United States. General alliance
approval does not tell us whether people support a particular deployment,
burden-sharing arrangement, escalation risk, or use of force.

**Meaning:** institutional legitimacy is layered. People can value an alliance
while distrusting a partner or opposing a particular operation.

Sources: [Pew NATO legitimacy record](records/us-pew-nato-public-legitimacy-2026.json),
[Pew US reliability record](records/us-pew-us-reliability-international-2026.json),
[Germany/Lithuania alliance posture record](records/us-nato-germany-hybrid-response-alliance-posture-2026.json).

### Host-country approval is not informed or durable consent

A government can approve a foreign brigade, exercise, or security arrangement
while public knowledge of the specific mission remains limited. Approval also
does not establish how land, infrastructure, families, local services, or risk
will be experienced over time.

**Meaning:** geopolitical legitimacy depends on local knowledge and practical
burden, not only on formal government consent.

Source: [Germany/Lithuania staffing and social-capacity record](records/us-germany-lithuania-brigade-staffing-social-capacity-2026.json).

### Readiness depends on social capacity

Staffing, family support, childcare, training, maintenance, host-nation
relationships, and sustained funding are part of military capability. A force
can have a formal target or public commitment while the people and support
systems needed to sustain it remain under pressure.

**Meaning:** state power is not only equipment. It is also labor, care,
institutional trust, and the willingness of people to keep carrying the system.

Sources: [Germany/Lithuania brigade staffing record](records/us-germany-lithuania-brigade-staffing-social-capacity-2026.json),
[NATO civil-military medical readiness record](records/us-nato-vigorous-warrior-civil-military-medical-readiness-2026.json),
[GAO military childcare workforce record](records/us-gao-military-childcare-workforce-incentives-2026.json).

The deeper subtheme is **state power as social capacity**. The Germany-
Lithuania brigade report says about 80% of positions were filled by volunteers
but that selected mandatory measures were still needed before the 2027 target.
NATO's medical exercise involved civilian health boards, hospitals, rescue,
police, border services, and ambulances across 38 participating countries.
KFOR's posture adjustment likewise depends on coordination with Kosovo Police,
EULEX, Kosovo institutions, religious representatives, and other security
actors. These are not just support details. They are the social and
institutional labor that makes a formal capability usable.

The cultural and political question is who is expected to carry that labor,
who gets protected by it, and who gets a meaningful say in the risk. A force
can become more staffed, an exercise can become more integrated, and a mission
can coordinate with local actors while family burden, civilian resource use,
local acceptance, and actual safety remain unsettled. The records therefore
support a capability-and-legitimacy pattern, not a claim of deterrence or
consent.

### Operation is stronger evidence than procurement, but still not leverage

NATO ISR flights, intelligence products, KFOR coordination, JEF Baltic
operations, and Maven network authorization show that capabilities can cross
from plan to operation. But operating activity does not by itself prove a
successful decision, deterrence, adversary adaptation, public acceptance, or
strategic advantage.

**Meaning:** the important geopolitical question is not “did the system
operate?” but “did its operation change another actor’s options or behavior?”

Sources: [NATO ISR operating record](records/us-nato-nisrf-agile-combat-employment-2026.json),
[NATO Maven integration record](records/us-nato-maven-operational-integration-2026.json),
[JEF LION PROTECTOR operating record](records/us-jef-lion-protector-baltic-express-2026.json),
[KFOR local-security integration record](records/us-nato-kfor-posture-local-security-integration-2026.json).

### Procurement, delivery, acceptance, and use are different events

The Poland defense records keep contracts, scheduled delivery, aircraft
arrival, formal acceptance, pilot training, missile non-delivery, and supplier
structure separate. A contract can create an option without creating an
operational stock, and an accepted platform does not prove sustainment or
mission use.

**Meaning:** national autonomy is specific to an application. A country may
have more equipment and still depend on foreign maintenance, inputs, software,
training, or replacement parts.

Sources: [JASSM-ER delivery non-observation record](records/us-poland-jassm-er-delivery-nonobservation-2026.json),
[JASSM/LRASM realization record](records/us-jassm-lrasm-production-integration-realization-2024-2025.json),
[Poland defense financing and opinion record](records/us-poland-defense-financing-public-opinion-2026.json),
[USAspending supplier route](records/usaspending-jassm-lrasm-multi-facility-recipient-route-2024-2025.json).

### International agreement is not yet usable cooperation

The [DOD international-arrangements finding](projects/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements/findings/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements-001.md)
adds an earlier institutional stage. DOD established 303 acquisition-related
international arrangements associated with $64 billion from FY2021–2025, but
the portfolio declined while review timelines, staffing, dispersed
responsibility, and cost-sharing communication created friction. An agreement
can therefore be politically valuable and financially large without yet being
implemented, delivered, jointly used, or strategically effective.

The [GAO Section 333 partner-capacity record](records/us-gao-section-333-partner-absorptive-capacity-2023.json)
adds a sharper planning-to-use boundary. GAO reviewed 46 selected train-and-
equip proposals and found that 42 did not fully document a sustainment plan,
partner absorptive-capacity analysis, or measurable objectives. The program had
allocated nearly $5.6 billion in FY2018–2022, but the allocation total is not a
delivery or operational-capability measure. GAO also found inconsistent State
participation and, in its April 2026 follow-up, an unresolved joint DOD–State
planning process. This does not show that every project failed; it shows that
funding and proposal approval can precede evidence that a partner can absorb,
sustain, use, and evaluate the capability.

**Meaning:** partner capacity is partly an institutional-design problem. The
route from U.S. intent to external capability requires a named planning owner,
partner-fit assessment, sustainment resources, measurable objectives, and
follow-up evidence—not only a budget line or congressional notification.

The companion [GAO Section 333 delivery record](records/us-gao-section-333-delivery-delay-evaluation-quality-2023.json)
shows what happens after that planning layer. Across Section 333 activities with
estimated delivery dates, 75% of deliveries were late relative to DOD's own
estimates as of the end of FY2022. In six completed project evaluations, four
reported capability improvement and two reported no improvement because
equipment was unsuitable or unreliable; GAO also found that the evaluations
generally failed to meet its quality criteria. The evidence therefore supports
an implementation-friction stage, not a simple success/failure score.

One bounded example is especially useful: a $7.6 million ground-sensor system
functioned for about three months before becoming inoperative, while a $12
million mobile border-security-trailer project was operational for about three
months total during 2019–2022, with maintenance and shipping problems reported
in the evaluation. These cases do not establish a general failure rate, but
they demonstrate why delivery, reliability, maintenance, and partner use must
remain separate from funding and intended capability.

The [GAO human-rights training record](records/us-gao-security-assistance-human-rights-training-oversight-2019-2021.json)
adds a legitimacy and oversight stage. GAO found that DOD initially lacked
systematic tracking of mandated human-rights training and that DOD and State had
not evaluated its effectiveness. Later follow-up documented new tracking fields,
quarterly status reporting, and monitoring-and-evaluation plans. This improves
administrative visibility, but it does not prove changed conduct, civilian
protection, local acceptance, partner legitimacy, or strategic effect. Rights
training must therefore remain distinct from equipment delivery and operational
capability.

The [GAO Northern Triangle police-training record](records/us-gao-central-american-police-training-human-rights-controls-2014-2025.json)
shows how the rights route becomes local and organizational. Across 22 State and
USAID-funded projects in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras, none of the 14
State or 8 USAID project documents reviewed had explicit human-rights
objectives, and State lacked reliable consolidated data on the number of police
trained. Follow-up reported additional human-rights language in Central America
training agreements and new indicators. Those controls improve the formal route
from U.S. assistance to local policing, but they do not establish changed
conduct, civilian safety, complaint resolution, or public trust.

This is not evidence that cooperation failed. Selectivity, consolidation, legal
review, and security safeguards may explain part of the pattern. The safe claim
is narrower: the administrative route from partner agreement to usable joint
capability is itself a state-capacity variable.

### Coordination capacity depends on named responsibility and staffed routes

The public evidence adds a more specific implementation subtheme beneath an
agreement or exercise. GAO's space-cooperation review finds multiple formal
mechanisms alongside staffing gaps, overlapping responsibilities, difficult
points of contact, and missing implementation milestones. Its security-
cooperation workforce review separately finds that DOD's workforce provisions
were at mixed stages of completion and that the relevant position counts and
staffing scopes are not interchangeable. Together with the international-
arrangements review, these sources show that cooperation is carried through
responsibility assignment, contactability, staffing, training, and milestone
tracking—not merely through a signed arrangement or shared strategic intent.

**Meaning:** institutional legibility is a geopolitical capacity. A partner
cannot reliably coordinate through a mechanism whose owner, contact route,
staffing, or implementation clock is unclear.

The sources use different organizational units and do not establish a common
failure rate, a particular mission failure, partner refusal, or strategic
defeat. The supported trend is narrower: formal cooperation can coexist with
an unready coordination layer that constrains whether the capability becomes
dependable shared action.

Sources: [GAO space-cooperation record](records/us-gao-space-allied-coordination-2025.json),
[GAO security-cooperation workforce record](records/us-gao-security-cooperation-workforce-2026.json),
[DOD international-arrangements finding](projects/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements/findings/us-gao-international-defense-arrangements-001.md).

### Domestic infrastructure can create geopolitical dependence before it creates leverage

Energy transmission, data centers, critical materials, defense production, and
cross-border power can expand capacity while leaving ownership, cost,
replaceability, rate allocation, and local acceptance unsettled.

**Meaning:** dependence is not automatically weakness, and capacity is not
automatically leverage. Leverage requires an observed moment when another
actor changes a decision because alternatives have changed.

Sources: [Virginia large-load governance record](records/us-virginia-data-center-large-load-governance-2024-2027.json),
[Virginia residential bill-relief record](records/us-virginia-data-center-residential-bill-relief-package-2026.json),
[JASSM/LRASM supplier-concentration record](records/usaspending-jassm-lrasm-supplier-concentration-2024-2025.json).

### Local security operations can persist while legitimacy remains unsettled

KFOR posture adjustment, Polish border operations, airspace alerts, and allied
security exercises show that states can sustain coordination and adapt through
local institutions. Persistence demonstrates organizational capacity, not
necessarily safety, consent, fairness, or a change in the underlying conflict.

**Meaning:** security policy has a local cultural layer. Residents, migrants,
religious institutions, police, families, and host communities experience the
operation differently from national governments.

Sources: [KFOR posture and local integration record](records/us-nato-kfor-posture-local-security-integration-2026.json),
[NATO hybrid-response posture record](records/us-nato-germany-hybrid-response-alliance-posture-2026.json),
[NATO drone-interception record](records/us-nato-lithuania-drone-interception-alliance-response-2026.json).

### Foreign reputation, domestic politics, and capability can diverge

The international-reception records show that foreign publics, U.S. partisan
groups, and U.S. institutions can hold different views of the country's role.
Domestic support for a capability can coexist with foreign distrust, while
foreign approval does not prove that a policy has durable domestic support.

**Meaning:** legitimacy has multiple audiences. It is not one national trust
score and not a direct substitute for military or economic power.

Source: [Pew US global role and international comparison layer](records/us-pew-us-reliability-international-2026.json).

## The cultural and political meaning

The broad cultural issue is **conditional national capacity**. Americans and
partner populations are not only asking whether the state can build or deploy
something. They are asking:

- who pays for it;
- who is protected;
- who must supply the labor and family support;
- who gets to decide;
- whether the partner is reliable and respectful;
- whether the local burden is fairly shared; and
- whether the capability actually changes the danger or dependence it was
  meant to address.

This creates a recurring political split between support for an abstract goal
and consent to the concrete cost, mission, or risk required to achieve it.

## The next test

The next valuable geopolitical record should connect one named capability or
operation to:

```text
accepted and operating capability
  -> local burden and public knowledge
  -> partner or community response
  -> changed external behavior, deterrence, concession, or refusal
  -> continued legitimacy and sustainment
```

If the record stops at procurement, exercise activity, staffing, or official
claims of readiness, it should remain a capability-stage finding.

## Bottom line

The atlas is finding that geopolitical power is built through domestic social
capacity and legitimacy, not only through money, weapons, technology, or
agreements. A state becomes more powerful when capability is operating,
replaceable, socially supportable, locally accepted, and able to change other
actors’ choices.

The major unresolved question is whether the growing number of formal
capabilities around the United States will produce durable public permission,
alliance cooperation, and actual external leverage—or mainly produce more
systems that require money, labor, and trust without proving the strategic
result.

**Geopolitical stopping rule:** do not add another agreement count, procurement
announcement, exercise, or readiness claim unless it adds a named arrangement
or operation with implementation, delivery, joint use, partner response,
public burden, deterrence, concession, refusal, or other attributed external
effect.
