# Rotation GX — research security, open collaboration and academic autonomy

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Status:** Tier 1 bounded distinction plus Tier 2 conversion question  
**Research lane:** research security, internationalisation, critical knowledge, foreign interference, academic freedom and proportional governance

## Why this rotation exists

Research security is expanding quickly as states seek to protect critical
knowledge, technology and publicly funded research from interference,
unauthorised transfer and coercion. The policy challenge is not simply whether
to protect research. It is whether safeguards are targeted, transparent and
proportionate enough to reduce real risk while preserving open collaboration,
academic freedom, institutional autonomy, mobility, inclusion and global
problem-solving. A policy, training module or restricted list is an upstream
control; it is not evidence of safer research or better cooperation.

## Research question

When governments, funders and research institutions introduce research-security
screening, disclosure, training, export controls or partnership restrictions,
under what conditions do they protect people, knowledge and public investment
without producing unjustified exclusion, self-censorship, discrimination,
administrative burden or loss of valuable international collaboration?

## Conversion chain

```text
critical research / international partner / funding application / data / facility
  -> proportionate risk assessment, disclosure, support, security and appeal
  -> sensitive work is protected while legitimate collaboration remains possible
  -> research quality, integrity, safety and shared public-good capability improve
  -> institutions retain autonomy, researchers retain freedom and affected partners receive fair treatment
```

## Evidence snapshot

1. The EU Council's research-security recommendation identifies undesirable
   knowledge transfer, foreign interference and ethical or integrity violations
   as risks. It also requires responsible internationalisation: academic freedom,
   institutional autonomy, openness, proportionality, non-discrimination,
   fundamental rights and avoidance of protectionism or political
   instrumentalisation. These principles define the design tension, not the
   results of implementation.
2. The European Commission reports that research security is a priority in the
   2025–27 European Research Area agenda and encourages authorities to support
   universities and researchers in making informed partnership decisions. A
   national policy or support service closes institutional intent; it does not
   show that researchers received timely, comprehensible and non-discriminatory
   help.
3. OECD's 2025 STI Outlook reports that research-security policies expanded from
   12 countries in 2018 to 41 in 2025, with more than 250 policy initiatives.
   It also explains that over-securitisation can weaken research quality,
   impede innovation and fragment collaboration, while list- and process-based
   risk assessments can create prejudice, cultural bias and inadvertent
   discrimination. The policy count is a signal, not a measured safety outcome.
4. OECD describes the shift from awareness campaigns to strategies, regulation,
   oversight, guidance, self-service tools and advisory structures. It also
   reports that institutions may receive insufficient or contradictory
   information and that researchers can self-censor or avoid important research
   areas. This closes an operational risk mechanism, but not the incidence or
   remedy for a named researcher.
5. NSF's research-security programme defines the problem as safeguarding
   federally supported R&D from misappropriation, integrity violations and
   foreign-government interference, while explicitly seeking to strengthen
   beneficial international collaboration. Its TRUST framework includes risk
   assessment, disclosure and due-process values. A framework and training
   resource do not establish fair adjudication, retained collaboration or equal
   treatment across institutions and nationalities.
6. The OECD and EU guidance retain “as open as possible, as closed as
   necessary” as a governing idea. This is useful because it rejects both naive
   openness and indiscriminate closure, but it requires a live proportionality
   test: what information is sensitive, who decides, what evidence supports the
   restriction, how can a researcher appeal, and what public value is lost?

## Bounded proposition

**A research-security strategy, partner screen, disclosure form, training
requirement, export-control rule, sensitive-technology list, funding review,
security office, data restriction or due-diligence process is not by itself
safer research, research integrity, academic freedom, institutional autonomy,
fair treatment, open collaboration, public benefit or geopolitical resilience.**
An institution can classify a partner, restrict a dataset or investigate a
disclosure without establishing that the risk was material, the measure was
proportionate, the researcher understood it, legitimate work continued, bias
was avoided, an appeal was available or the protected public value exceeded the
cost of closure.

## Cultural and geographical interpretation

Research security is experienced differently by a well-resourced institution,
an early-career researcher, a migrant scholar, a partner in the Global South, a
minority-language team or a university dependent on international facilities.
Country lists and opaque risk signals can turn nationality, language,
institutional affiliation or field of study into proxies for risk. Restrictions
can also concentrate access to equipment, data, publication networks and
funding in already powerful centres.

The relevant geographical unit is the collaboration corridor: who funds the
work, owns the facility, stores the data, controls access, interprets risk and
retains capability when a project ends. Academic autonomy requires not only
freedom from foreign interference but also protection from domestic political
pressure, commercial capture and unjustified security claims. The cultural
question is whether researchers and communities can participate, contest a
classification and preserve legitimate knowledge relationships without being
treated as security objects.

## Counterexamples and cautions

- Targeted security controls can protect sensitive people, infrastructure,
  intellectual property and public investment; the proposition is not an
  argument for unrestricted disclosure.
- A restriction may prevent a serious harm even if its public benefit is not
  immediately visible. Absence of a documented incident is not proof that a
  control was unnecessary.
- Disclosure can support transparency, but excessive or unclear reporting can
  deter collaboration and shift risk-management costs to researchers without
  improving security.
- A “trusted partner” designation can enable productive collaboration while
  creating blind spots, dependency or exclusion of non-designated institutions.
- Research quality, academic freedom, national security, human rights and
  geopolitical resilience are related but non-identical outcomes; the ledger
  keeps them separate.

## Promotion decision

Promote as **Tier 1 bounded distinction**: official sources document rapid
research-security expansion, concrete threat categories, implementation tools
and explicit safeguards for openness, proportionality, non-discrimination and
academic freedom. Promote as **Tier 2 conversion question**:
research-security-to-safe-open-collaboration-and-academic-autonomy, requiring
named evidence from risk identification through decision, support, appeal,
collaboration continuity and public-value effect.

## Six-stage closure table

| Stage | What to observe | Current boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | strategy, list, training, disclosure rule, security office, funding screen or export control | Policy expansion is strongly documented |
| Mechanism | threat model, partner risk, data sensitivity, oversight, proportionality, support and appeal | Official principles and tools exist; implementation varies |
| Operation | named researcher/institution receives a risk decision, support, restriction or clearance | Institutional routes exist; comparable case-level records are limited |
| Material consequence | sensitive work protected, collaboration retained, project delayed, cancelled, burdened or redirected | Research-quality, public-value and exclusion effects are not systematically joined |
| Meaning | academic freedom, dignity, recognition, fairness, belonging and autonomy | Normative safeguards are explicit; lived legitimacy is under-measured |
| Power | who defines risk, controls access, appeals, funds alternatives and retains capability | State and institutional authority are visible; researcher and partner bargaining power remains open |

## Next test

Select three cases: a critical-technology funding review, an international
research partnership with a formal risk assessment, and a collaboration that was
restricted or delayed. Trace one researcher and one partner institution from
disclosure through classification, advice, decision, appeal, collaboration
continuity, security result and public-value effect. Record nationality and
language only where relevant and lawfully available; test whether proxies,
administrative burden, self-censorship, alternative access and remedy changed
the outcome.

## Source boundary

The official record is strong for threat categories, policy expansion, support
mechanisms and the stated balance between security, openness, proportionality,
non-discrimination and academic freedom. It is weaker for comparable same-case
evidence that research-security controls reduced harm while preserving equitable
collaboration and autonomy. This remains a Tier 2 research opportunity, not a
promoted effectiveness claim.
