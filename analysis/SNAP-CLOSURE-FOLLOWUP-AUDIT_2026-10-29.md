# SNAP closure follow-up audit

**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Purpose:** test whether Connecticut SNAP theft/replacement cases close the route from administrative correction to actual benefit receipt and household protection  
**Status:** implementation obligation observed; post-order credit and household recovery remain unverified

## Executive result

The official Connecticut record adds an important stage that was missing from
the first packet: a hearing order can specify both a replacement amount and a
deadline for DSS to provide proof of compliance to the hearing office.

That is stronger than a generic correction order, but it is still not the same
as public evidence that:

- the account was credited on time;
- the household could use the balance;
- food was purchased or another need was protected;
- the replacement was adequate; or
- later SNAP continuity and trust improved.

The current public search found the decisions and the procedural route, but no
public post-order proof-of-compliance or account-level outcome for the named
anonymized requests. This is a documented source boundary, not a claim that
DSS failed to comply.

## Revised bounded finding

The strongest defensible administrative-access finding is now:

> Administrative systems can specify deadlines, correction orders and proof-of-
> compliance requirements before public evidence shows that a household received
> and could use the remedy.

This identifies a precise form of administrative legibility: the system can be
legible to the hearing office while remaining opaque at the household endpoint.
The public record tells us what DSS was ordered to do, but not whether the
household's usable food capacity was restored or whether the restoration lasted.

## Cultural and institutional meaning

For the household, the relevant institutional experience is not only whether a
hearing was won. It includes how long the balance was unavailable, how much
work was required to document the theft, whether the agency's clock was
observable, whether the remedy arrived, and whether the household had to borrow,
skip food or use another support route in the interim.

For the institution, a proof-of-compliance requirement is a promising
accountability design. It moves the record beyond a formal order. But unless
the proof is accessible and connected to actual account use, it remains an
internal process milestone rather than verified household protection.

## Counterexamples and boundaries

- A missing public proof document does not establish noncompliance; it may be
  retained in a nonpublic case file.
- An account credit does not prove adequate food access if the credit was late,
  incomplete, inaccessible at retailers or offset by other hardship.
- A hearing order can correct an agency decision while leaving interim household
  costs unrecovered.
- The cases are anonymized and selected through a hearing record; they cannot
  estimate the prevalence of theft, denial, correction or recovery.
- SIPP and WBNS population estimates should remain contextual rather than being
  merged with the Connecticut cases.

## Closure request

The decisive follow-up fields are:

```text
case/request key | order date | proof-of-compliance date | credit date
| credited amount | usable balance | first subsequent transaction
| interruption days | replacement adequacy | food/bill/work/care consequence
| later SNAP continuity | repeat theft | trust/action/exit
```

If Connecticut DSS or OLCRAH cannot release those fields because the cases are
anonymized or the records are protected, the correct result is a formal
source-boundary finding. Do not substitute the hearing order, agency procedure
or program participation for receipt and recovery.

## Promotion decision

Promote the narrower Tier 1 finding:

**A formal implementation requirement is not verified household receipt or
recovery.**

Retain the Connecticut cases as high-value administrative-legibility examples,
not as evidence of population-level SNAP adequacy or food-security recovery.
