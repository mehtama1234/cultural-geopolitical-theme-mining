A short argument · US automated service, complaints, and authority

A faster answer is not yet a remedy

Automation can improve the first reply without giving the customer a way to fix the problem.

The argument

The dividing line is not bot versus person. It is whether the customer can understand the decision, keep a record, correct an error, and reach someone who can change the answer.

Follow one complaint

A customer reports a missing delivery. An automated tool helps a worker reply quickly. The package is still missing. The customer repeats the story and reaches a worker who cannot issue a refund. Response time improved; the loss did not.

automated prompt or routing → faster first response → repeat complaint or denial → human access and authority → resolution, repeat contact, loss, complaint, or exit

HBS customer-service experiment · CFPB complaint database

The deeper finding

Service quality has two clocks: the first reply and the time to a final remedy. Firms may improve the first while leaving the second unchanged. Customers pay in repeated explanation, missed work, lost money, and weak appeal.

What remains unproven

The HBS experiment measures first-contact response and sentiment in one setting. It does not follow the same customer through repeat contact, transfer, decision, remedy, and appeal.

Next test

Choose one complaint system. Record first contact, repeat contact, automation, transfer, explanation, worker authority, remedy, appeal, customer time, money lost, and exit.

Reading rule

A quick reply is not a solved problem. A human transfer is not the same as human authority. Ask who can change the answer.
