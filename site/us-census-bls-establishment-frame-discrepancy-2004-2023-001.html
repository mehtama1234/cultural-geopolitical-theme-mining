Finding 001: The business count can change the story about who has economic power

Status: bounded Census CES measurement-and-firm-structure finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

Census CES Working Paper 26-36 compares two major U.S. employer-establishment frames. In March 2023, BLS's published count was about 9.2 million private-sector establishments, compared with about 7.5 million in Census's count. Linked data show that much of the difference comes from two places: BLS captures many more employers in services for older adults and people with disabilities, and BLS often assigns more worksites to firms that Census treats as single-unit or less multi-unit.

different record and firm-linking rules
  -> different counts of workplaces and parent firms
  -> different picture of small business, concentration, and local capacity
  -> different policy calculations
  -> [open] actual competition, jobs, care quality, and household effect

What this adds to the atlas

This is a cultural and political point about economic visibility: before people argue over whether the U.S. economy is becoming more concentrated or more decentralized, they may be looking at different maps of the same economy. One map sees many small, scattered service employers; another groups more activity inside fewer, larger firms. The disagreement is not just technical because it changes policy models and which kinds of work—especially home-based care—are visible as businesses.

The broader pattern is not “the data are wrong.” It is that measurement rules help decide which institutions and workers appear powerful, numerous, or missing. That makes the frame itself part of the political economy.

Limits and next test

  • The paper is a working paper, not a Census Bureau publication endorsement.
  • Linked establishment-level comparisons cover 2004–2016; the 2023 figures are published aggregate comparisons.
  • BLS and Census use different administrative sources and firm-linking rules, so the paper does not identify one universally correct count.
  • Household employers of home health aides may be represented differently from ordinary service firms.
  • A different establishment-size distribution does not prove actual market concentration, worker power, care quality, wages, prices, or local economic health.
  • The model exercise shows different policy predictions, not realized policy effects.

The next decisive test is a protected crosswalk joining both frames to named firms, worksites, workers, care recipients, wages, vacancies, service use, local entry and exit, prices, and later firm or household outcomes.
