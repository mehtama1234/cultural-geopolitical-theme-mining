AI capability can grow faster than shared control

Status: provisional cross-source synthesis · Checked: 2026-09-13

The bounded finding

The current evidence points to a split between capability and control. Firms can report productivity or output gains; statistical agencies can observe industry cost and utilization changes; households can be exposed to a modeled wealth channel; public institutions can add data and AI tools while losing staff; and countries can gain infrastructure while remaining dependent on foreign complements. These are compatible observations, but they are not one causal estimate.

The strongest defensible statement is conditional:

AI is more likely to become broadly shared capacity when the people and institutions affected by it can access the complements, ownership, review, portability, and bargaining routes that determine where gains and risks go.

That is a research proposition, not a claim that the US has already achieved or failed to achieve shared AI control.

The distinctions are substantive. A worker's AI use is not a firm's adoption decision. A lower industry price contribution is not a lower household bill. A modeled wealth effect is not a realized wealth change. New regulatory data are not the same as better oversight. A data center or cloud platform located in a country is not necessarily controlled or replaceable by that country.

The evidence chain under test

task fit and access
  -> worker or firm use
  -> output, productivity, cost, and labor composition
  -> ownership, wages, prices, wealth, and household room
  -> review, recourse, bargaining, public analytical capacity, and trust
  -> domestic resilience, dependence, and state bargaining power

The sources directly reach different points in this chain. NBER and BIS give worker/task and firm evidence. BEA gives account and measurement evidence. IMF provides a modeled distributional mechanism. OFR gives an institutional capacity case. OECD adds an employer-side measure of algorithmic-management exposure and reported governance before the chain reaches worker control. World Bank supplies the country-complement and governance frame. None of the sources alone follows a person, firm, agency, place, or country through the whole chain.

What can be said now

1. Productivity is not the same as distributed benefit

BIS's firm result is consistent with adoption complementing production in the short run, while the larger gains among medium and large firms make scale and complementary investment part of the distributional question. BEA's work adds different account-level signals: worker-reported utilization is associated with stronger state-industry output paths, while the industry-only employer-use specification does not recover the same output pattern. This is useful evidence of measurement and heterogeneity, not a universal AI productivity estimate.

Even when an industry cost or price contribution is negative, the result does not identify whether the saving became a lower purchaser price, a higher margin, a wage change, a quality change, or a return to capital. The chain must be completed with firm pricing, labor outcomes, and household expenditure or bill data.

2. Wage and wealth are different distributional channels

The IMF model makes the ownership issue explicit. Its baseline scenario reports a 1.73 percentage-point fall in modeled wage inequality and a 7.18 percentage-point rise in modeled wealth inequality between 2014 and 2048. The figures are not forecasts of US history. They demonstrate why a favorable employment or wage result cannot settle who captures an AI-enabled return.

Taxes, employee ownership, pensions, bargaining, competition, public provision, and broad access to capital could change the realized path. Those mediators are not supplied by the model and must be observed separately.

3. Public capacity can be augmented and weakened at once

OFR reports AI use in data processing, security monitoring, support, procurement comparison, and ChatOFR while also reporting a workforce path from 188 at the start of FY2025 to 109 at year end, an 11% budget reduction, platform changes, and reduced investment in several organizational functions. The report does not say AI caused the staffing change, and it does not measure whether supervision became more accurate or less accountable.

This is a valuable institutional counterexample to the simple story that automation equals more capacity. The relevant denominator is not only data processed; it is also retained expertise, review time, correction ability, interagency visibility, and responsibility when an automated output is wrong.

4. Household room and national judgment are separate endpoints

The Federal Reserve's 2025 SHED makes the missing household stage visible, without pretending to identify its cause. Fifty-eight percent of adults said higher prices made their finances worse and 77% reported at least one response; 16% did not pay all bills in the prior month and 8% reported a family that sometimes or often did not have enough to eat. Yet 73% said they were doing okay or living comfortably, while only about one-quarter rated the national economy good or excellent. These measures can coexist because personal room and aggregate judgment are different objects. They are not evidence that AI, financial-monitoring capacity, or any other particular institutional change produced the household response. They establish the endpoint that a future exposure design must explain.

5. Employer governance is not yet worker control

The OECD employer study places a useful intermediate layer in the chain. US managers report widespread algorithmic-management use, including monitoring and evaluation, while also reporting worker-impact concerns and governance measures. This is stronger evidence than a generic claim that “AI is coming to work”: it identifies the organizational functions where control may be changing and shows that employers recognize accountability and protection as problems. But a reported policy, consultation process, or governance measure does not establish that a worker can inspect a score, correct a record, reach a responsible human, refuse an unsafe use, bargain over the system, or obtain an effective appeal.

That distinction sharpens the end-to-end research target. The missing bridge is not simply more adoption data. It is a linked worker/workplace panel that can test whether monitoring and evaluation are associated with changes in discretion, pace, health, pay, status, training, voice, and recourse, and whether formal governance changes those relationships. The OECD results do not show that control was lost; they show why tool counts and governance presence cannot be treated as a control outcome.

6. Capability and dependence can rise together

The World Bank's complement frame and the program's infrastructure records imply a testable geopolitical distinction: a country can acquire access to AI services while lacking domestic control over power, chips, cloud, data, skills, finance, or contracts. That may still create real productive capacity, but it can limit replaceability and bargaining room during a disruption or dispute.

This remains a mechanism, not an observed geopolitical effect. A state should be called dependent or leveraged only after evidence of supplier concentration, control rights, switching cost, failed or successful substitution, and an external actor's changed behavior.

Counterexamples and safeguards

  • Smaller firms can gain from low-cost tools, open models, or shared public infrastructure even when average gains are larger for bigger firms.
  • A workforce reduction can reflect a mission change or administrative cut, not automation; an AI tool can augment remaining staff.
  • A modeled wealth channel can be offset by taxes, pensions, employee ownership, competition, or public provision.
  • Foreign-owned infrastructure can deliver affordable and reliable services without producing immediate political dependence; dependence requires a credible constraint on choice or exit.
  • A lower industry price measure can coexist with higher household bills when product mix, fees, quality, geography, or margins change.

The next empirical program

The next pass should build compatible panels rather than pool unlike headline numbers:

  1. Match firm adoption, size, capital/software investment, prices, wages, hours, training, ownership, and worker representation.
  2. Link employer algorithmic-management measures to worker-level monitoring, discretion, health, pay, voice, correction, and appeal outcomes.
  3. Match industry or place exposure to purchaser prices, household spending, wealth, debt, and time use, preserving the relevant denominator for each outcome.
  4. Track public AI use with staffing, workload, error/correction events, review routes, procurement dependence, and service or recourse outcomes.
  5. Compare countries or places with similar AI exposure but different power, data, supplier, ownership, and institutional complements; record actual switching, refusal, disruption, or negotiated concessions.
  6. Add repeated measures of trust, attribution, worker voice, public action, and political response only after timing and exposure are defined.

The decisive outcomes are not “AI adoption” or “AI productivity” alone. They are who gets the time, income, wealth, discretion, safety, explanation, and ability to exit after the system changes.
