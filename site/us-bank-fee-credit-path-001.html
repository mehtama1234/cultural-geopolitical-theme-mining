When a bank fee becomes a wider credit problem

Removing a bank fee gives immediate relief, but it may not solve the shortage that caused the fee.

The argument

A household with cash may avoid the next cost. A household with almost no room may still need overdraft access, late-payment credit, payday credit, family help, or another loan.

Did the charge disappear, and did the household gain lasting room? Those are different tests.

What the source establishes

An NBER study of transaction data and large-bank policy changes from 2017–2022 found that removing NSF fees cut those charges across income groups. Relaxing overdraft rules reduced overdraft fees mainly for wealthier and more liquid households; only those households later showed lower late fees, interest, account fees, and payday-loan use.

The policy changes did not substantially reduce financial stress among more vulnerable households. That is a liquidity difference, not proof the policy harmed them.

NBER Working Paper 34993

Follow the shortage

low cash → NSF fee or overdraft rule → payment succeeds or fails → later fee, interest, or alternative credit → money left for housing, food, health, or work → recovery or another shortage

A successful payment is not the same as recovery. Removing a fee may stop one added cost while leaving the original bill unpaid.

Keep these apart

  • Fee removed and shortage removed.
  • Payment success and recovery.
  • Overdraft access and affordable credit.
  • Lower fees and lower stress.

Next test

Follow a household for 90 days after a low-balance event. Record the original bill, payment result, charges, credit used, account status, and need delayed or protected.

Reading rule

Keep the first charge, later credit, and household recovery separate.
