Finding 004: Efficiency does not settle the control question

Status: provisional
Last checked: 2026-09-11
Project: AI, work, and control

Finding

Cross-country evidence suggests that a workplace can gain efficiency from software and still leave basic control questions unanswered. Managers report problems with accountability and opaque decisions. Case studies of logistics and health care show that algorithmic management can streamline work while also raising concerns about surveillance and job quality.

The practical test is simple: when a system affects a person’s work, can that person know it is being used, understand the basis of the decision, correct a bad record, and reach a responsible human?

What we directly know

  • The OECD studied more than 6,000 firms in France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States. Its report says managers using algorithmic management tools report concerns including unclear accountability, difficulty following the logic of decisions, and inadequate protection of worker health. OECD Algorithmic Management
  • An ILO/JRC study examined logistics and health care in France, Italy, India, and South Africa. It reports efficiency and work-streamlining benefits alongside risks to job quality and concern about intrusive surveillance. ILO algorithmic management case studies
  • A randomized NBER study found that surveillance alone did not significantly change average performance, while unclear explanations for surveillance changes reduced output. NBER Working Paper 33348

Accountability checklist

For every AI or algorithmic management system, record:

  1. Notice: Are workers told what is being collected or automated?
  2. Purpose: Is the system’s purpose stated in ordinary language?
  3. Basis: Can the worker see the facts or measures used in a decision?
  4. Correction: Can a wrong record be corrected without retaliation?
  5. Override: Can a person override the system, and must they explain why?
  6. Appeal: Can the worker reach a responsible human?
  7. Responsibility: Is a named institution accountable when the system is wrong?
  8. Boundary: Are there limits on use outside work time or outside the original purpose?

These are research variables, not claims that every workplace must use one legal form.

Causal chain under test

software measures or assigns work -> decision becomes easier to scale
-> responsibility may become harder to locate -> worker uncertainty and stress can rise
-> trust, effort, job quality, and bargaining may change

The evidence supports concern about the middle link. It does not yet establish the size of the effects on workers.

Four-map reading

Map First signal Missing evidence
Material efficiency and work-streamlining claims pay, workload, health costs, and long-run output
Social uncertainty, stress, autonomy, and trust worker-level measures by group and job
Institution firms create governance measures and rules whether workers can use them in practice
Power decision rights may move into software ownership, appeals, bargaining, and enforcement

Strongest challenge

Employer concerns and case-study risks may overstate harm if good governance works. The project must test whether notice, explanation, correction, and appeal reduce harm rather than assume they do.

What would change our mind

  • Evidence that accountability gaps do not affect trust, effort, health, or job quality.
  • Evidence that efficiency gains consistently come with stronger worker choice and recourse.
  • Evidence that workers can correct decisions even when the system’s logic remains private.

Next tests

  1. Read the OECD tables and worker evidence cited in its report.
  2. Extract the ILO case methods, worker groups, and institutional settings.
  3. Find public workplace policies that specify notice, correction, override, and appeal.
  4. Compare a unionized workplace, a contractor workforce, and a platform workforce.
  5. Add legal and regulatory evidence only after documenting the actual workplace practice.
