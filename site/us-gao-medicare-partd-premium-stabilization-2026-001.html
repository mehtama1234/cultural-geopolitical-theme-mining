Finding 001: Premium stabilization can preserve enrollment while shifting risk and leaving the causal effect open

Status: bounded prescription-drug, household-cost, and public-finance finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

CMS used a voluntary 2025 Medicare Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration to limit premium increases in standalone drug plans. Nearly all plan sponsors participated. Without the demonstration, GAO's analysis estimated that if beneficiaries stayed in their 2024 plans, average monthly premiums would have nearly doubled, and 37% of beneficiaries would have faced increases above $40 per month.

The demonstration reduced premiums by up to $15 and capped each plan's increase at $35 in 2025. Medicare also protected plan sponsors and took on more financial risk; CMS estimated the demonstration would cost $9.8 billion across 2025 and 2026.

Among beneficiaries not eligible for the low-income subsidy, average standalone plan premiums rose from $42 in 2024 to $43 in 2025 under the demonstration. Enrollment in these plans rose 2%, while the share of all Part D enrollees in standalone plans stayed at 42%.

The route is:

benefit change and projected premium shock
  -> public subsidy and insurer risk protection
  -> beneficiary premium and plan enrollment
  -> medication access and adherence [not observed here]
  -> durable affordability and public cost [not established]

What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a policy-stabilization version of a recurring subtheme:

A public subsidy can prevent an immediate household price shock while moving risk onto the state and leaving the longer-term effect on use and affordability to be evaluated.

It also separates visible stability from causal certainty. Premiums and enrollment looked relatively stable, but GAO says those changes could reflect other Part D benefit changes as well as the demonstration.

Limits and counterevidence

  • The hypothetical premium increases assume beneficiaries stayed in their 2024 plans or were transferred under specified conditions; they are not observed bills for a no-demonstration group.
  • The analysis focuses on beneficiaries not eligible for the low-income subsidy who paid plan premiums; it is not the experience of every Part D beneficiary.
  • A 2% enrollment increase does not show medication access, adherence, plan quality, or household financial relief.
  • The $9.8 billion is an estimated demonstration cost, not a measured net cost after avoided medical spending or other effects.
  • ASPE has an evaluation framework and planned continuing evaluation; the absence of a completed causal evaluation is an open stage, not proof that the demonstration failed.

The next decisive artifact is a beneficiary-plan route joining projected and actual premium, subsidy, plan choice, medication access, out-of-pocket spending, switching, and later health or financial outcome.
