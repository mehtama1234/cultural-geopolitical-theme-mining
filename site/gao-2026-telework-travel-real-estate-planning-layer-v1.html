GAO 2026 telework, travel, real estate, and planning layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-107532 Reference window: 2019–2024 travel and telework data; MPO actions through March 2025; report published March 11, 2026 Status: place-level behavioral and planning finding; not a causal estimate of telework’s effect on any single city or household

Why this update matters

Telework is often described as a workplace arrangement. GAO's review shows it is also a city, consumer, real-estate, transit, vehicle-use, and public- investment phenomenon. When people work remotely or hybrid, the timing and location of trips change; transit ridership and fare revenue shift; demand for central business district space and housing can move; and metropolitan planning organizations must forecast infrastructure for a travel pattern that no longer resembles the pre-2019 commute.

This is a particularly useful broad trend because it links individual work choice to collective systems. The same flexibility that reduces one person's commute may weaken a transit system's revenue base, redistribute retail demand, alter downtown land use, increase travel at other times or places, and produce new political conflict over which communities receive investment.

Recorded findings

Surface Result Boundary
Telework level Average telework rates remained about twice 2019 levels across large, medium, and small communities over 50,000 people This is an average context measure, not a uniform experience across occupations, workers, or places
Geographic concentration Increases were highest on average in communities with 1 million or more people Large-community averages do not identify household, neighborhood, or worker-level effects
Transit MPOs and reviewed studies reported decreased transit ridership and fare revenue; among the 26 largest transit cities, only one had 2024 ridership above 2019 levels The result is not a claim that telework caused every ridership change or that all transit modes behaved alike
Vehicle travel MPOs reported changes in when and where people drove New travel patterns are not necessarily more or less total vehicle travel, congestion, or emissions without route- and time-specific measurement
Real estate MPOs and reviewed studies reported shifts in demand for commercial real estate and housing away from some central business districts A demand shift does not establish vacancy, price, conversion, household displacement, or downtown recovery
Local response Most of the 352 responding MPOs reported that their communities took actions related to transit, vehicle use, or housing Responding does not establish that the action matched the new demand or improved outcomes
Transit adaptation More than half of MPOs reported that transit providers improved or increased service to attract riders More service can raise cost without restoring ridership, revenue, reliability, or access
Planning gap About one-third of MPOs said they did not intend, or did not know how, to incorporate telework into planning This is self-reported planning capacity and uncertainty, not proof that plans are wrong
Guidance demand More than 90% of MPOs wanted more DOT guidance on incorporating telework into forecasting Demand for guidance does not establish which model or investment would be correct
Federal execution Federal law directed DOT to complete a travel-demand study by November 2023; GAO found DOT had no plan or timeline for completion, and its recommendation remained open The finding concerns federal planning and implementation, not a direct local service failure

The central mechanism

remote/hybrid work arrangement
  -> fewer or differently timed commute trips
  -> transit demand, fare revenue, driving, parking, retail, and real-estate shifts
  -> agency budgets, service design, land use, housing demand, and local politics
  -> new investments, conversions, incentives, or continued mismatch
  -> household mobility, employer location, and community distributional effects

Telework changes a network, not only a workplace. A commuter rail line built around weekday peaks may lose fare revenue while a bus route serving dispersed workers needs more off-peak coverage. A downtown office vacancy may coexist with stronger demand for housing or services outside the core. A worker saving time may spend it on nonwork travel, care, shopping, or additional work; a worker who cannot telework may face fewer services or a changed local labor market.

The planning problem is a clock problem. Household behavior can change faster than capital planning, forecasts, leases, zoning, and transit procurement. Planning models calibrated to 2019 may understate new trip geography, while short-term ridership recovery may not justify permanent service cuts or expansion without subgroup and time-of-day evidence. “Return to office” and “remote work” are not binary population states; hybrid frequency, occupation, income, place, employer policy, and household care needs matter.

What this changes in the broader trend map

This layer adds a collective-geography consequence to the work-control lane. It connects employer policy and worker autonomy to transit finance, downtown commercial property, housing demand, local government forecasting, and public investment. It also provides a counterweight to interpreting telework solely as individual flexibility or solely as urban decline.

It should be read with the airport transit adoption layer. Airport evidence shows route presence versus practical use for a particular place and trip system; this evidence shows how changing work geography can alter the demand that transit and real-estate systems were designed around. Neither alone identifies household welfare or a universal future of cities.

What remains unproven

GAO does not estimate telework’s causal effect on transit ridership, fare revenue, driving, emissions, housing prices, office vacancy, retail sales, local tax bases, or political response. It does not separate telework from pandemic disruption, service changes, migration, employer policy, fuel prices, or other simultaneous changes for every outcome.

The MPO survey had an 86% response rate but not every MPO answered every question, and reported actions are not a common treatment. The 26-city ridership comparison is a selected large-transit-city measure. Community averages conceal workers who cannot telework and households whose care, disability, income, occupation, or housing conditions make remote work unavailable.

Next test

Build a place-month-mode ledger linking:

  • worker telework frequency, occupation, employer policy, and household care;
  • origin/destination, trip purpose, day, time, mode, fare, parking, and accessibility;
  • transit boardings, service hours, reliability, fare revenue, and route change;
  • vehicle miles, congestion, emissions, retail activity, office occupancy, housing price/rent, vacancy, and local tax base; and
  • planning forecast, investment decision, intervention date, subgroup burden, and public response.

Compare similar metropolitan areas with different telework exposure and planning responses, preserving worker and non-teleworker differences. The strong result is not a headline about empty trains or full suburbs; it is a measured account of who gains time and flexibility, who loses service or faces new cost, and whether public investment catches up with the changed geography of daily life.
