Finding 001: A deletion system can make requests contestable while showing partial broker-level control

Status: bounded platform and administrative-contestability finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

California's Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) lets a verified resident send one request to more than 600 data brokers. The official workflow gives the consumer an eight-digit tracking ID and broker-level statuses: deleted, exempted, opted out, record not found, or pending. Data brokers must begin processing requests in August 2026, access them at least every 45 days, report their status, and keep screening for newly collected matching data.

That is a real improvement in visibility and contestability. A later CalPrivacy implementation update reports that 99.9% of registered consumers had a profile deleted by at least one broker, and that the typical user had deletion from more than 40 brokers. This is a meaningful later implementation result, but it is not an all-broker or person-level audit. The public record still does not provide the full consumer-broker status table, show how many records remained in downstream contractors or copies, establish re-collection, or show what happened to the consumer afterward. Several statuses also leave data in place: “not found” can reflect a failed match rather than no data, “opted out” means the broker may still hold data, and exemptions or first-party/public data can remain.

The route is:

verified consumer request
  -> broker matching
  -> per-broker status
  -> deletion, exemption, opt-out, or no match
  -> contractor/dependent action and recurring suppression
  -> residual data, reappearance, safety, trust, or durable exit [open]

What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a digital version of the program's recurring distinction:

Making a process visible can increase a person's ability to contest it while leaving the final material outcome unmeasured.

The system is not merely an offer on a webpage. It supplies a tracking ID, broker-specific states, a route to add identifiers, and recurring obligations. That gives the consumer a way to see and challenge administrative progress. It still does not establish that every copy, inference, contractor record, or future collection was controlled.

Limits and next test

  • The official pages describe system rules and status categories; they are not a public microdata file or aggregate completion report.
  • A status is not a full data inventory. “Record not found,” “opted out,” and “exempted” can leave uncertainty or data in place.
  • The pages do not show broker-by-broker completion rates, unmatched-request rates, contractor compliance, downstream deletion, data reappearance, or consumer harm.
  • The system is California-specific and newly operating; the 90-day reporting window means early statuses may be incomplete.
  • More than 600 reachable brokers, sign-up counts, and the agency's typical-user result describe implementation reach; they do not establish complete all-broker deletion or improved wellbeing.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected aggregate DROP ledger with requests, broker matches, statuses, timing, exemptions, contractor actions, reappearance checks, and later consumer-reported safety or trust. Until then, the safe claim is that DROP makes deletion more visible and contestable, not that it has achieved complete or durable data removal.
