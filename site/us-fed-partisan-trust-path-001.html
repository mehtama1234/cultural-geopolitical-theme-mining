Connected US finding · Fed trust and economic messages

An economic message lands differently when trust is partisan

People hear economic messages while comparing them with rent, food, wages, debt, and their view of the institution speaking.

The argument

If the Federal Reserve appears aligned with one party, the same message may receive more attention from that party’s supporters and more doubt from the other side.

A trusted message can change belief, but it cannot create cash or lower a bill.

Follow one message

A family sees its credit-card rate rise and hears that tighter policy will bring prices down. It may delay a purchase or reject the explanation. If rent and medicine are due, neither reaction changes the next payment. The message can still change blame.

price experience + political identity → perceived alignment → trust and receipt → inflation belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

The deeper finding

Communication is part of policy transmission because people must decide whether a signal deserves weight. Credibility is built against a household’s own bills, party identity, media path, and ability to respond.

Officials may explain a rate through national inflation. Households may judge it through a credit-card bill or mortgage reset. The explanation can be accurate while failing to fit the decision a family must make today.

What remains unknown

The studies do not show actual spending, saving, borrowing, credit balances, lasting communication effects, or whether partisan trust changes a vote.

Next test

Follow households from a real Fed communication for six months. Join exposure, source, party cues, belief, prices, income, spending, saving, borrowing, credit, turnout, and policy views. Record whether the household had room to act.

Reading rule

Keep alignment, trust, message receipt, belief, behavior, and political judgment separate.
