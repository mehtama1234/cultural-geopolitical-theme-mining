Connected US finding · Fed trust and household response

When the price message depends on who says it

An economic message is filtered through a household's bills, political identity, and view of the institution speaking.

The argument

A message can change an inflation expectation before it changes a budget. Trust may alter how people hear a signal, but it does not give a family cash to act.

Credibility is built against the household's own bills.

Follow one message through a household

A family sees its credit-card rate rise and hears that tighter policy will bring prices down. If it trusts the Fed, it may delay a purchase. If it sees the Fed as partisan, it may reject the explanation. If rent and medicine are due, neither reaction changes the next payment. The message can alter blame without changing the budget.

price experience and political identity → perceived institutional alignment → trust and message receipt → inflation belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

What the sources establish

EvidenceWhat it means
In a survey experiment with 5,205 US consumers, NBER finds people who saw the Fed as aligned with their party reported more trust and were more likely to receive or pay for Fed communications.Perceived alignment affects whether the message gets through.
Those respondents gave the messages more weight when updating inflation expectations.Trust can change how information is used.
Another NBER study finds trust in the Fed is often higher among people of the same party as the president.Institutional trust can move with political identity.
Trust and inflation beliefs do not always move together.One trust score cannot stand in for later outcomes.
The studies measure reported trust, communication, and beliefs, not actual spending, borrowing, or voting.Message credibility is not proven behavior.

Sources: NBER communication and NBER Fed trust.

The deeper finding

Public communication is part of economic policy because people decide whether a signal deserves weight. A trusted message can still be unusable when the family has no short-term choice.

What remains unproven

The studies do not show actual spending, saving, borrowing, credit balances, or lasting communication effects. They do not establish that a partisan trust gap changes a vote.

Next test

Follow households from a real Fed communication for six months. Join exposure, source, party cues, belief, prices, income, spending, saving, borrowing, credit, turnout, and policy views. Record whether the household had room to act.

Reading rule

Keep alignment, trust, message receipt, belief, behavior, and political judgment separate.
