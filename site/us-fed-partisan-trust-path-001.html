Connected US finding · institutions and trust

When the price message depends on who says it

An economic message does not enter an empty room. People hear it while comparing the message with rent, food, wages, and their view of the institution speaking.

The argument

If the Federal Reserve appears aligned with one party, the same message may receive more attention from that party’s supporters and more doubt from the other side. A person may distrust the speaker and still follow the rate, or trust the speaker and still have no cash to act.

Trust can filter a message without determining behavior.

What the sources establish

In a survey experiment with 5,205 US consumers, NBER finds that people who saw the Fed as aligned with their party reported more trust, were more likely to receive or pay for Fed communications, and gave those messages more weight when updating inflation expectations.

Another NBER study finds trust in the Fed is often higher among people of the same party as the president, while trust and inflation beliefs do not always move together. The studies measure reported beliefs, not actual spending or voting.

Follow the message

price experience and political identity → perceived alignment → trust and message receipt → inflation belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

The path can break at every arrow. A person may hear the message but not believe it, believe it but lack cash, or change a purchase without changing a vote.

Keep these apart

  • Perceived alignment and trust.
  • Trust and message receipt.
  • Belief and a real transaction.
  • Behavior and political judgment.

Next test

Follow households from a real Fed communication for six months. Join exposure, source, belief, prices, income, spending, saving, borrowing, credit, and political action. Compare the same message with and without party cues.

Reading rule

Keep alignment, trust, belief, behavior, and judgment separate.
