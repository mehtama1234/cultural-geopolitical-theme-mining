Connected US finding · trust, money and public life

When the price message depends on who says it

People can hear the same economic message differently when they think the speaker is on the other party's side.

In one sentence

Trust may change whether people use a message, but it is not proof they changed a budget.

Keep trust, belief, behavior, and political judgment separate.

What the sources show

Political identity changed trust and belief weight

In a survey experiment with 5,205 US consumers, NBER finds that people who see the Federal Reserve as aligned with their party report more trust, are more likely to receive or pay for Fed communications, and give those messages more weight when updating inflation expectations.

Another study finds partisan differences in trust, while trust and inflation beliefs do not always move together.

NBER 33071 · NBER 33684

The connection

price experience and political identity → perceived institutional alignment → trust and message receipt → inflation belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

People may distrust a message because their bills conflict with it, not only because of party identity.

The four outside views

Household: A family compares a message with rent, food, gas, and wages. Attention may change, but the next bill still sets the budget.

Company and sector: Banks, lenders, employers, retailers, and media filter economic messages. Their wording affects what reaches households.

Money and finance: Belief can change a rate, purchase, or saving decision, but reported belief is not a transaction. Prices, income, debt, and cash matter too.

Public power: Fed independence, appointments, communication, media choice, and accountability shape whether information is heard as guidance or party argument.

What would change the finding

  • Personal prices, income, debt, cash, and prior trust.
  • Message source, party cues, media, repetition, and actual receipt.
  • Inflation beliefs, spending, saving, borrowing, and credit decisions.
  • Trust before and after a policy change.
  • Voting, turnout, party, age, race, income, place, and education.

Next test

Follow households from a real Fed communication through six months. Join message exposure, beliefs, prices, income, spending, saving, borrowing, credit, and political action.

Reading rule

Trust is not proof that a household changed its budget. Keep perceived alignment and behavior separate.
