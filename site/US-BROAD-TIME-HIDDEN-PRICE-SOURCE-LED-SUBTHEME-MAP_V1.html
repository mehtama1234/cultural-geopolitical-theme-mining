Time as a hidden price: source-led subtheme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: 142 records carry time_hidden_price. Time appears as a separate constraint across work, care, travel, and administration—not merely an inconvenience added to a dollar cost.

What the source material supports

1. Formal leave does not ensure usable time

The ATUS leave record reports 94% of wage and salary workers had paid or unpaid leave access, but 8% needed leave in an average month and did not take it. Among unmet-need workers, 29% cited too much work, 14% job risk/denial, and 13% income loss. Access is not approval, care completion, or job retention.

2. Childcare-related work loss has a selected, non-comparable clock

The SIPP childcare-time-loss record finds 8.43 mean reported hours below poverty and 24.40 at 4x poverty among selected reference parents whose care arrangements prevented work/work-more. The upper estimate is imprecise and its reporting unit differs; it cannot rank burden or prove a resource effect.

2a. Child-care difficulty has distinct money, lost-time, and work-adaptation surfaces

The Census child-care/work-adaptation record uses 2024-reference-year CPS ASEC data for households with children ages 0–13. It reports 23.9% used paid care; among those with nonzero paid-care spending, the mean was $10,520 or 5.6% of household income. Separately, 3.7% reported a care-obtaining problem that prevented more work. Within that affected group, the converted mean was 65.8 work days lost, including 114.6 among households below the poverty line. Adaptation choices are not mutually exclusive: 15.6% of all child households selected paid leave, 8.1% unpaid leave, 6.7% reduced hours, 2.0% quitting, and 4.1% not looking for a job; paid-leave and non-search patterns differed sharply by poverty group.

Supported subtheme: care affordability, a care-access constraint, time lost from work, and the work response are related but separate household fields. Lower reported paid-care use or paid leave is not evidence of lower care need when non-search and other adaptations are more common.
Not established: a particular provider disruption, causal policy effect, job or wage loss, child outcome, later recovery, or why an individual household selected a given adaptation. The data are descriptive, time-loss responses use converted and top-coded units, and the adaptation categories overlap.

The SIPP utility/tenure childcare record adds a deliberately bounded cross-field screen. Among identified reference parents reporting utility-payment difficulty in the December 2024 SIPP slice, the estimated share saying fall child-care arrangements had prevented work or more work was 9.35% for owners/buyers (105 valid records; 95% CI 3.26–15.44) and 8.13% for renters (146; 95% CI 3.37–12.89). The corresponding estimates without reported utility difficulty were 2.84% for owners/buyers (1,677) and 5.01% for renters (673). These are weighted descriptive cells, not evidence that a utility bill or tenure caused a work outcome: the care question has an annual fall reference while the utility condition is attached to December, and the difficulty cells are small.

Additional boundary: material pressure, tenure, care arrangements, and work prevention can co-occur in a way that exposes a multi-domain time route, but their timing and mechanisms cannot be inferred from a single cross-section. The estimates do not establish household prevalence, a renter-owner effect, bill amount, care-time loss, job loss, or recovery.

3. Travel time shapes usable options without revealing unmet trips

The NHTS mobility record finds 35.23% zero-vehicle prevalence among low-income urban households versus 3.54% among higher-income counterparts; mean shopping trip time was 34.01 versus 20.71 minutes. A travel-day diary misses unmade, cancelled, or substituted trips.

4. Administrative burden can convert a nominal benefit into lost time and denied access

The SNAP procedural-denial record finds work requirements significantly increased procedural and overall denials in its administrative difference-in-differences design. It does not observe a claimant's notice, paperwork time, appeal, food security, or restored benefit.

5. Extending care hours can convert time availability into maternal work, with bounded coverage

The Head Start full-day record uses a 2016 federal initiative and treated-versus-untreated areas. It estimates a 19% increase in full-day enrollment and, among single mothers, increases of 2.5% in employment, 3.2% in full-time work, 2.9% in hours, and 7% in earnings. This is a targeted expansion, not an all-childcare result.

Supported subtheme: care hours are a labor-market input. Changing the duration of an existing care route can shift work, hours, and earnings—not just parents' reported preferences or a household's child-care spending. Not established: child-care quality, child outcome, maternal time satisfaction, schedule control, access equality, household recovery, or durable effects.

6. Food assistance is also a queue, transport, schedule, and treatment route

The charitable-food access record finds 16.1% of adults reported charitable-food receipt in 2025, while 8.0% reported unmet need. Among people with unmet need, 45.5% named closed hours, 42.8% not knowing where to go, 35.2% no transport, 27.7% a long wait after arrival, and 26.6% a long trip; these multiple-response reasons overlap. Among recipients, 25.8% reported difficulty obtaining food within reasonable travel time and 20.9% reported difficulty when available.

Supported subtheme: a food buffer is not simply delivered or not delivered: hours, awareness, travel, waiting, physical carrying, safety, and treatment determine whether a nominal local resource becomes usable food. Not established: nutritional adequacy, provider capacity, causal stigma or discrimination, later food security, SNAP substitution, trust, or action.

7. Cultural and civic participation consume differently scheduled time

The ATUS cultural/civic-time record reports that on an average day 30.4% of people age 15+ socialized, 9.4% participated in religious/spiritual activities, and 5.3% volunteered. Average socializing time was 0.44 hours on weekdays and 0.92 on weekends/holidays, whereas average volunteering time was 0.12 hours on both. Primary-activity diaries do not capture all simultaneous, digital, informal, or short interactions.

Supported subtheme: time is a practical precondition for social infrastructure and civic participation, but socializing, religious activity, volunteering, informal help, and political action have different calendars and should not be treated as one civic-capacity measure. Not established: belonging, organizational strength, cultural decline, political influence, or a causal work/care explanation for participation.

8. Movement purchases access while claiming both a household budget and a work-time clock

The transportation cost-and-time record keeps BLS spending and ACS commuting estimates separate. In 2024, average transportation spending was $13,318 per consumer unit (17.0% of expenditure), and vehicle-insurance spending rose 12.3% from 2023. The ACS reports a 27.2 minute mean one-way commute for workers not at home and 9.3% with a 60+-minute trip; only 3.7% used public transport and 13.3% worked from home. Neither source links cost and commute for the same household or measures non-work trips.

Supported subtheme: the practical price of place is both money and time, and movement can create access even as it consumes household room and workday capacity.
Not established: a particular household's burden, service reliability, care/food access, job choice, or later mobility.

9. Service-transition delay can create an unmeasured disruption clock and a visible price spike

The federal telecom transition record finds the federal transition to Enterprise Infrastructure Solutions ran more than three years past a revised deadline, with legacy service extended to May 2026. GSA did not know which agencies, if any, experienced disruption. For six selected agencies, combined legacy billing rose 206% from February to March 2025 ($13.4 million to $40.9 million), with agency increases from 138% to 365%. The comparison does not isolate pricing, use, or a completed-transition counterfactual.

Supported subtheme: infrastructure transition has its own continuity and cost clocks: a temporary extension can preserve service while leaving disruption visibility and price exposure unresolved.
Not established: outage rate, employee or public-service harm, mission failure, or the causal effect of the replacement contract.

10. Dated medical events and work/household pressure do not share one recovery clock

The dated MEPS medical-event record orders prescription, emergency-room, and inpatient events before later round fields. Emergency-event people had $155.52 mean self/family payment and 24.12% medical debt versus 15.10% in a complementary group; inpatient-event people had $733.03 mean payment and 60.13% later nonemployment versus 33.03%. In the selected three-round prescription screen, employment-status movement was higher while worsening health was not. These are same-person, month-ordered descriptive comparisons, not causal episodes.

Supported subtheme: care events, immediate payment, household debt, and work-status change can occupy different time horizons; a single post-event status cannot be called recovery or harm by itself.
Not established: event causation, clinical need, total bill, claim outcome, job loss, adaptation, remedy, or durable recovery.

10a. Improved self-rated health does not by itself close a household financial-adaptation episode

The SHED panel adaptation/health record follows recontacted adults from 2024 to 2025 and conditions on having reported an adaptation in 2024. Among 2024 borrowers, borrowing persisted into 2025 for 59.0% of respondents whose self-rated health worsened, 50.5% whose health was unchanged, and 56.4% whose health improved. Delayed-purchase persistence was 69.3%, 64.7%, and 66.3%, respectively; reduced-savings persistence was 62.7%, 60.3%, and 56.9%. The improved-health group has 492 paired respondents, and the source supplies no design-based standard errors.

Supported subtheme: a repeated household adaptation can outlast a reported improvement in health. Health direction, financial adjustment, and recovery are therefore separate clocks even within a same-respondent panel.

Not established: that health change caused persistence, a dated illness or bill, care intensity, debt balance, income path, whether an adaptation was harmful or helpful, later financial security, or a durable health recovery.

11. Eldercare-provider time profiles persist after some compositional differences are held constant

The ATUS eldercare-provider record compares people with an eldercare-roster record to nonproviders after standardizing both groups across supported age, sex, labor-force status, household-child, and broad education cells. In 2024, providers averaged 70.7 more eldercare minutes, 16.8 more household-work minutes, 14.8 more care-for-people minutes, 9.7 more travel minutes, and 19.7 fewer paid-work minutes per diary day; in 2025, the corresponding eldercare contrast was 62.4 minutes and the care-for-people and travel contrasts persisted. The paid-work and socializing contrasts in 2025 were imprecise, so neither year supports a fixed displacement estimate.

Supported subtheme: eldercare is associated with a distinctive one-day time profile that is not eliminated by these basic compositional controls, making time allocation—not just reported care status—a relevant exposure surface.
Not established: that care caused any difference, when care began, recipient need, health, job/schedule accommodation, lost earnings, household burden, social withdrawal, or recovery.

11a. The broad time budget was largely stable across the two annual diary samples

The ATUS annual time-use comparison keeps the population-level baseline separate from the provider comparison. For people age 15+ in the 2024 and 2025 annual samples, weighted mean daily paid work moved from 190.8 to 185.7 minutes, household work from 120.7 to 119.8, care for people from 32.8 to 31.5, travel from 65.0 to 62.3, and socializing or communicating from 273.6 to 276.1. The record reports separate replicate-weight standard errors for each annual estimate; it does not claim a tested annual difference or a causal explanation.

Supported subtheme: the all-adult annual allocation surface is comparatively stable across these two diary samples, so a change in a care, work, or access route needs its own population, mechanism, and timing evidence rather than an assumed economy-wide time reallocation.
Not established: that any apparent year-to-year movement is statistically reliable; which groups moved; gender or life-stage ordering; a respondent-level transition; or an effect of price, work rules, care demand, technology, or policy.

12. Income timing is partly absorbed through uneven family and network support

The income-volatility and support record reports that 30% of adults had income vary at least occasionally and 11% said that variation made bill payment difficult; among self-employed adults, the figures were 58% and 22%. Twenty-three percent received outside-household help with a listed expense; among adults 18–29, 47% received some such help, including 30% phone-bill and 23% housing help. The survey does not identify amount, timing, reciprocity, adequacy, or whether support prevented hardship.

Supported subtheme: income volatility creates a calendar problem that can be bridged by household networks, but network availability and bill-specific support are unequally distributed resources rather than a universal buffer.
Not established: a dated income shock, dependency or control within help, debt repayment, recovery, or later autonomy.
