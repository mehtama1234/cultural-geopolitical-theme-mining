Repair access is part of a product’s real price

The argument

The price of a product includes what happens when it breaks. A phone, car, appliance, or work tool can be cheap to buy and expensive to keep alive. If parts, tools, repair information, software access, or a usable warranty are unavailable, a failure can become a forced replacement or a long period without the product.

Safety and quality are real reasons to control some repairs. The customer still needs a fair, workable choice: an authorized shop, an independent shop, a part, a manual, a warranty claim, or a replacement with a clear price. Without that choice, the purchase price hides a future cost and shifts risk toward people with the least cash to replace the item.

The deeper finding is that repair access is financial slack. A repairable product gives a household time to absorb a shock. A product that can only be replaced turns a small failure into a second purchase, debt, missed work, or lost care.

This is a timing issue as much as a price issue. A $150 repair due today can be harder than a $400 replacement paid next month, while a two-week wait for a work tool can cost more than either option. The real product price is the money and time needed to restore its use before the next job, meal, trip, or care duty.

Follow one broken product

A delivery worker’s phone stops charging, or a family’s washer fails. The part is unavailable, software blocks a third-party fix, the approved shop is far away, or the warranty process takes weeks. The household borrows a device, misses work, pays for a replacement, or puts the repair on credit.

The barrier may be physical, contractual, informational, or simply the time needed to find a competent shop. The customer experiences all of them as one question: can I get the function back before the next obligation is due?

The customer also has to judge risk. A cheap repair that fails again, voids a warranty, or creates a safety problem is not the same as a durable repair. An authorized repair may be safer or easier to document but too far away or too expensive. An independent repair may be practical but hard to compare. “Repairable” must therefore include outcome, not just access to a part or manual.

What the sources actually establish

Evidence Meaning Limit
FTC identifies design, parts, information, software, and contract limits that can make repair harder. The barrier can be built into the product. The report does not measure every product’s household cost.
FTC says warranties may cover repair, replacement, or refund and must be available before purchase. Coverage is part of the product offer. A warranty’s existence does not show speed or ease of use.
FTC warns that requiring specified parts or service providers may violate warranty law. A warranty should not quietly remove lawful repair options. Legal protection does not guarantee a nearby repair path.
FTC says repair restrictions can steer people toward replacement before useful life ends. Failure can create a second purchase. It does not measure how often this happens by household.
FTC–Deere settlement advances repair access for farm equipment. Enforcement can change information and tools owners receive. A farm-equipment settlement does not establish results for phones or appliances.

Sources: FTC Nixing the Fix, FTC warranty warning, FTC warranties, and FTC–Deere settlement.

The deeper finding

Repair access determines who can keep a product useful. A household that can fix it may avoid a large payment and keep work, transport, communication, or care moving. A household that cannot may pay for replacement even when the original could have continued working.

The burden is uneven because repair choices depend on place and bargaining power. A rural customer may face shipping and a long wait. A renter may not control an appliance or building system. A small business may lose revenue while waiting for a specialized tool. A low-income household may choose a risky temporary fix because replacement credit is unavailable. The same design restriction can therefore produce very different losses.

Repair rights also shape local markets. Parts and manuals can support independent shops and keep money in a community. A locked repair channel can concentrate work with the manufacturer and make local service disappear. That is a possible market effect, not something the cited evidence proves for every product.

The strongest measure is not “repair available.” It is whether repair is affordable, timely, safe, and available where the customer lives. A nominal right that requires weeks without a work tool is a weak practical right.

What remains unknown

The FTC record identifies restrictions and legal protections but does not join a product failure to a repair quote, waiting time, replacement debt, work interruption, and later household stability. It does not show whether independent repair produces the same safety and repeat-failure results across products.

It also does not show whether firms respond by improving durability, raising prices, changing warranties, or shifting repair costs elsewhere. A lower repair price is not enough if failure becomes more common; a longer product life is not enough if the repair is unavailable when needed.

Next test

Track product failure, repair options, parts price, labor, warranty claim, waiting time, replacement, borrowing, work or care interruption, and repeat failure. Compare authorized and independent repair by product, place, income, rural access, and disability.

Follow products from purchase through the first failure and for two years afterward. Record failure type, diagnosis, quoted price, part and labor source, days without use, warranty decision, repair durability, replacement financing, and what the customer could not do while waiting. Compare identical products sold with different repair access, and report purchase cost, lifetime cost, useful days, waste, and household disruption separately. This would show whether a repair right creates real financial room or only a formal option.

Reading rule

Do not call a product affordable from its purchase price alone. Include the cost and availability of keeping it working.
