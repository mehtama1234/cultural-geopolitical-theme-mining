Finding 306: A completed transmission project can make infrastructure capacity visible before household savings are verified

Status: bounded infrastructure realization finding · Checked: 2026-09-20

The bounded finding

The New York Power Authority reports that Smart Path Connect completed and energized three new substations, modified six existing substations, and built 45 miles of transmission lines in the North Country. Linked with the earlier Smart Path project, the announcement describes nearly 200 uninterrupted miles of upgraded 345-kilovolt transmission and reports more than $438 million in annual savings for New Yorkers.

This is a stronger infrastructure realization stage than an award, forecast, or approved route: the source reports physical completion and energization. But the savings claim is still a system-level or program-level outcome. The public record does not show which households or rate classes paid less, experienced fewer outages, or received equal benefit.

NYPA's companion project page adds an important intermediate state: it calls the annual figure an estimate and says congestion savings are expected to be reflected in costs for its customers, including municipalities, businesses, and other public-power customers. The completion release goes one step further in its framing, describing expected monthly electric-bill savings for households and businesses. That is a customer-facing benefit claim, not a household observation: it still supplies no per-household amount, rate-class allocation, timing, or observed bill.

The NYISO tariff adds a further institutional stage. Schedule 18 establishes a separate Smart Path Connect Facilities Charge, bills responsible load-serving entities according to actual energy withdrawals, and applies specified transmission-rights revenues and payments as offsets. This makes cost allocation observable at the wholesale/load-serving-entity level. It still does not show the amount charged in a particular period, retail pass-through, household incidence, or lower bills.

The related cost-containment schedule adds a governance layer: it sets a $481.8 million cost cap and provides for no return on equity on 20% of the equity portion of eligible costs above that cap. The public template still leaves the actual project-cost inputs to company records, so this is a rule for limiting recovery—not evidence of the final project cost or customer savings.

The January 2026 annual-update filing makes the current boundary concrete: the project-specific revenue-requirement schedules exist, but current gross plant in service and project-return inputs are blank or point to company workpapers. An earlier NYPA 2024 Annual Update, however, contains a populated Smart Path Connect line for the year ending December 31, 2023: $116.6 million in project net plant and $20.46 million in total annual revenue requirement, including a $291,508 incentive-return amount. This advances the chain to a real historical project-accounting amount. It still cannot be treated as a retail charge, household payment, or household savings outcome, and it must not be assumed to be the later 2026 billing-period amount.

The New York Department of Public Service proceeding adds an important measurement caution. The applicant's analysis presents several different benefit concepts: about $99 million in single-year production-cost savings, about $450 million in annual Northern New York congestion-cost savings, about $214 million in statewide load-payment savings, and roughly 7.5 TWh of curtailment elimination. These are modeled estimates with different scopes; they cannot be added together or treated as observed household savings. They strengthen the project-level benefit map while leaving rate-class pass-through, customer bills, reliability, and realized distribution open.

load growth / congestion / reliability need
  -> transmission construction and substation modification
  -> energization and network connection
  -> populated historical project revenue requirement
  -> reported annual system savings and expected monthly bill savings [customer incidence open]
  -> household affordability, reliability, local acceptance, and durable benefit [open]

Why it matters for the larger atlas

The infrastructure lane can now distinguish three stages that are often collapsed:

  1. Need and planning: a national or regional system identifies congestion, load growth, or reliability pressure.
  2. Physical realization: a named project is built, energized, and connected.
  3. Lived distribution: customers actually receive lower bills, better reliability, or more usable service.

The Smart Path case reaches stage two and reports a stage-three system claim, but does not identify the household distribution. That makes it useful for the firm/state and household-consumption themes without pretending to be a household affordability result.

Evidence boundary

  • The source is a named public project announcement, not an independent household-rate or outage evaluation.
  • “More than $438 million in annual savings” is retained as reported; no counterfactual, ratepayer denominator, or distribution by customer class is supplied here.
  • The companion project page describes customer-cost pass-through as expected; it does not report realized bills, customer-class allocations, or a measured household result.
  • NYISO Schedule 18 establishes a formal wholesale cost-recovery mechanism, not a retail bill outcome; the charge, offset, pass-through, and household effect remain separate stages.
  • The cost-containment schedule establishes an overrun rule, but its public template does not populate the actual eligible-cost fields; cost governance is not realized cost incidence or household affordability.
  • The January 2026 annual-update filing exposes the relevant schedules but not populated current project-charge inputs; the earlier year-end 2023 filing does contain a historical project revenue-requirement amount, but that is not a measured retail or household incidence.
  • Energization proves a physical operating stage, not equal access, reliability during future stress, maintenance durability, or local acceptance.
  • The project may strengthen regional capacity without automatically producing geopolitical leverage or household security.

Next test

Join the project identifier to public utility rate filings, congestion or reliability data, customer class, geography, and later billing or outage outcomes. Preserve the distinction between project completion, system savings, rate changes, customer incidence, and durable affordability.
