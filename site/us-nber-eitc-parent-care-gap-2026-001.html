Finding 001: Work incentives can reduce intensive parent care without a measured replacement

Status: bounded U.S. care/work substitution finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

The revised NBER Working Paper 32583 uses Health and Retirement Study data and a simulated EITC-benefit approach to study care from EITC-eligible daughters to older parents. A $500 increase in EITC generosity is estimated to reduce care received by 13–24 hours per month, driven by a lower likelihood of intensive care of 40 or more hours per month.

The study then tests whether the lost care is replaced by financial transfers, other children, spouses, or paid providers. It reports no evidence of increased financial transfers and finds that care gaps remain for older parents rather than being filled by those replacement channels.

The safe interpretation is:

A policy can increase the value of market work while reducing an unpaid care input; the social consequence depends on whether the missing care is replaced, not only on whether the eligible worker gains income or hours.

Evidence chain

EITC generosity and work incentive
  -> daughter labor/care allocation
  -> reduced intensive parent care
  -> [reported null] financial, family, or paid-care replacement
  -> [open] care quality, health, safety, household cost, and later work

What it adds to the broader atlas

This adds a concrete hidden-price and substitution mechanism to the time/care lane. It moves beyond measuring how much unpaid care exists: it tests what happens when a policy changes the opportunity cost of providing it and checks several plausible replacement routes.

It also prevents a simple “work incentive succeeded” interpretation. Work, care, income, and health are different currencies. A policy may improve one while shifting an unpriced burden to an older parent or leaving a need unmet.

What the source does not show

  • The study uses simulated EITC variation, not a randomized policy experiment.
  • It does not establish care quality, safety, unmet-need severity, or health deterioration for the parents.
  • It does not show that every reduced-care episode is harmful; work, autonomy, formal services, or other unmeasured support may improve.
  • It does not provide a universal national estimate for all caregivers or all EITC recipients.
  • The estimate does not identify daughter earnings, hours, household income, job quality, or long-run family security as a complete chain.

Next decisive test

Follow a parent-daughter care episode through EITC eligibility, work hours and earnings, care hours and task type, paid or public services, unmet need, health, financial transfers, and later work or institutional-care use. Preserve families with no reduction, substitute care, and changed living arrangements as counterexamples.
