Matched evidence · US public aid and household spending

The same dollar can do different work depending on how it arrives.

Food or cash, once or every month: the form and timing of aid change what households spend first. That near-term response is not the same as lasting financial safety.

In one sentence

A label may protect food spending, while cash may cover rent, medicine, transport, or a repair; recurring aid may help planning.

Immediate room and lasting security are different outcomes.

What the matched evidence shows

Part of the pathWhat the record showsWhat it does not show
Food aidNBER 35698 finds food-store responses of 0.38 for recurring in-kind aid and 0.18 for one-time in-kind aid.Better food security or health for every family.
Cash aidThe same study finds food-store responses of 0.20 for recurring cash and 0.06 for one-time cash.That cash is worse; it may pay a different urgent bill.
Basic needsNBER 32784 finds monthly cash raised spending, especially on housing, food, and cars, and increased mobility.Which need mattered most for one household.
Balance sheetThe cash experiment found higher debt offset modest asset gains, leaving near-zero average net-worth change.That households gained no useful room or choice.
DesignThe evidence is consistent with labels, timing, and planning changing use.The best design for every need, place, or family.

The connection

public need → aid amount, form, and timing → what the household can buy or postpone → food, housing, transport, debt, care, or savings choice → short-run relief or lasting security

Policy does not only choose how much aid arrives. It sets what the money points toward and when the family can count on it.

Four views

Household

Ask what bill is due, what food is needed, and what a label prevents or permits.

Customer and worker

Spending can reach groceries, rent, cars, care, or local shops; no category is the full need.

Money and finance

Measure cash, debt, assets, missed bills, credit, and the cost of waiting.

Public power

Judge a design by food security, housing, health, choice, take-up, cost, and trust.

Next test

Compare cash and food aid with the same value and timing for two years. Track what the family bought, postponed, borrowed for, and whether its next shock was easier to handle.

Reading rule

Do not call a larger short-run purchase response a better policy. Ask what the family needed and what risk remained.
