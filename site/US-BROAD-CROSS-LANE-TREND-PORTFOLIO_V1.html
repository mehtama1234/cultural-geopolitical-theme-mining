Cross-lane trend portfolio v1

Checked: 2026-09-25
Purpose: synthesize recurring dynamics across the source-led maps without pooling their statistics or claiming that separate records follow the same person, place, firm, or institution.

1. Formal access is increasingly separated from usable access

Leave can be available while work, job risk, or income loss prevents use (ATUS leave). Workforce programs can be formally accessible while disability-specific routes and oversight remain incomplete (GAO disability workforce). Water funding can exist while applications, deadlines, and match timing leave communities unserved (GAO rural water).

Trend proposition: institutional capacity is shifting from whether a program exists to whether people can complete its route under time, health, language, money, and local-capacity constraints.
Open link: completed access → adequate, durable outcome.

The health-care trust and care-seeking study adds an institutional-meaning layer to usable access. In a nationally representative U.S. adult survey and structural model, entity-specific trust is associated with preventive and elective care-seeking and with less reported or anticipated care avoidance; trust in doctors has the largest role in the study's simulations, with differences by age and political affiliation. This makes trust a possible interface condition rather than a general attitude score. The source remains survey/model evidence: it does not identify a causal trust shock, appointment completion, affordability, treatment, health improvement, remedy, or political action. Lower care-seeking can also reflect cost, time, transport, provider availability, health status, or an appropriate choice.

The NHIS coverage-route record adds a reach-versus-gap layer to usable health access. Preliminary 2025 NHIS estimates place 28.0 million people, or 8.3% of the civilian noninstitutionalized population, uninsured at interview; exchange coverage rose from 4.3% in 2021 to 6.3% in 2025 among people under 65, while selected adult and Hispanic subgroup uninsured rates also shifted. This shows that a coverage route can expand and a subgroup gap can narrow while a large national uninsured exposure remains. The release does not establish continuity, affordability, network access, care use, medical debt, health outcomes, or which policy caused the change; early-release status and coverage-type definitions matter. The next test is a person/household route through eligibility, enrollment, premium and cost-sharing, network/contact, completed care, continuity, health, debt, trust, and switching or non-use.

The Medicare Advantage skilled-nursing authorization ledger adds a visible denial-to-appeal-to-reversal route to the usable-access question. In HHS OIG's review of 19 Medicare Advantage organizations, 12% of skilled- nursing-facility admission requests were denied, 18% of denials were appealed, and 95% of appealed denials were overturned in favor of the enrollee. The pattern identifies a strong initial-review and contestability boundary: formal reversal can restore authorization without showing when care began, whether a facility was available, or whether rehabilitation and functional recovery occurred. The rates are request-level findings from selected organizations, not a population-wide denial rate, and appeal behavior reflects provider practice, case complexity, knowledge, health, time, and resources. No beneficiary-level admission date, interim-care route, out-of-pocket cost, caregiver burden, functional outcome, trust, or plan switching is linked. The next test is one request through clinical need, notice and denial reason, appeal effort, reversal/receipt, admission timing, interim care, rehabilitation, caregiver time and cost, functional outcome, recurrence, trust, and plan change.

The Medicaid eligibility-error correction record adds a public-system feedback loop above the individual access route. Medicaid eligibility errors are reported across all 50 states and the District of Columbia, while GAO reviewed corrective-action plans in seven selected states for 2019–2024. The selected states used training, guidance, and eligibility- system updates, but CMS feedback did not consistently ensure that plans contained all required elements, and CMS did not consistently compare errors and corrective actions across states and years to learn which fixes worked or transferred. This makes administrative learning itself an access mechanism: correction can exist without reliable system-wide learning, and a documented oversight gap does not show that every state fix failed. The seven-state review is not a national corrective-action quality estimate; error rates and plan types do not show who lost or retained coverage, correction speed, continuity, health, time, cost, trust, or appeal. The next test is one state or beneficiary route through error, notice, correction, coverage restoration, continuity, health and household cost, complaint/appeal, and later institutional response, while comparing effective, incomplete, and non-correction cases.

The GAO toxic-exposure record-access record adds a personal-control stage to the same public-system route. The Department of Defense and Department of Veterans Affairs' Individual Longitudinal Exposure Record is used by claims staff, clinicians, and researchers, while service member and veteran access and correction functions are being rolled out. GAO reports a VA estimate that about 3.2 million of 6.8 million veterans screened since 2022 had at least one potential exposure, but that is a screened-agency estimate rather than population prevalence. The record shows administrative visibility, staff use, and a route toward self-access and correction; it does not show verified exposure, correction success, claim approval, treatment, health improvement, household effects, or trust. This is a useful cross-lane distinction: an institution can use a record before the affected person can inspect, contest, or correct it. The next decisive episode is one record through access, correction, verification, claim or care decision, treatment, health, and later appeal or institutional trust.

2. Household resilience is increasingly assembled through substitutions

People report reducing product use, savings, and major purchases, borrowing, or working more in response to price changes (SHED food pressure). Energy insecurity directly places food/medicine, temperature, and service on the same budget surface (energy consequences).

Trend proposition: material pressure increasingly appears as a sequence of tradeoffs rather than one visible deprivation measure.
Open link: which sacrifice occurred first and whether the household later recovered its buffer.

The BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey income-quintile record adds a current budget-composition context. Average annual spending was $78,535 per consumer unit in 2024, with housing at 33.4% of the average budget; income and expenditure changes also differed across income quintiles, and the lowest quintile's spending increase was concentrated in housing. These are consumer- unit averages and grouped income/expenditure comparisons, not a household hardship rate or a causal price effect. Spending can reflect income, quantity, quality, composition, debt, or price, and a large housing share does not prove an unaffordable payment. The value for the cross-lane map is to locate where room may be concentrated before following a dated bill, payment, substitution, protected or sacrificed need, recovery, trust, or action for the same unit.

The New York Fed debt/credit snapshot adds an aggregate credit-market layer to the household-room question. In 2026 Q2, the dashboard reports $18.8 trillion in household debt, 4.7% of outstanding debt in some delinquency stage, and quarter-to-quarter movement in non-housing, auto, and credit-card balances. These figures make credit exposure and delinquency visible alongside household-reported adaptation, but aggregate balances are not household trajectories, payment burden, rates, hardship, lender remedy, or welfare; balance growth can reflect demand, prices, credit supply, vehicle purchases, refinancing, or distress. The next test is a lawful household or credit-record episode through product, balance, rate, payment burden, delinquency, lender contact, correction/remedy, substitution, financial room, trust, and action, preserving aggregate versus household units.

The SIPP resource/job/work-limitation cross-lag adds a same-person monthly transition layer. Among 2024 SIPP adjacent-month pairs, resource-to-job-count movement differs by reported work-limiting status, and the reverse job-count-to-resource movement also varies across zero-, one-, and two-job cells. This makes resource and employment adjustment visible as direction-specific clocks rather than one “work improves security” measure. The Fay-BRR estimates are descriptive: work-limiting status is not a diagnosis, job count is not hours, pay, quality, schedule control, or voluntary mobility, and the design does not identify accommodation, care, employer response, household security, trust, political action, or recovery. The next test is one worker/household through limitation, resources, hours/pay, accommodation, benefits, care, employer response, housing, trust, and action, retaining the forward/reverse and person-month boundaries.

The macro price/income/labor/household-room comparison makes the multiple clocks explicit. July aggregate personal income and outlays, August consumer and producer prices, CPS/CES employment, partial-year JOLTS, and 2025 SHED hardship and price-response measures can all move in different directions and use different universes. Aggregate income and payroll growth do not establish household liquidity; CPI/PPI do not identify a household basket or incidence; nominal earnings and job counts do not establish real purchasing power, job quality, or bargaining; and SHED hardship is not a same-respondent causal link to any index. The cross-source proposition is therefore temporal and interpretive rather than a single macro trend: people and institutions encounter price, income, labor, and security signals on different clocks. The next test is one household or worker through dated exposure, adaptation, attribution, institutional response, and recovery, preserving BEA, BLS, JOLTS, and SHED units.

3. Administrative procedure is becoming an independent distribution mechanism

Work requirements increased procedural denials in the SNAP design (SNAP procedural denials); counsel changed immediate housing-court outcomes (tenant representation).

Trend proposition: paperwork, timing, representation, and review routes can determine exposure before a formal eligibility or merits decision.
Open link: case route → benefit/restoration → later material and civic effect.

The SNAP work-requirement procedural-denial record adds a policy-associated mechanism to this procedural layer. Using SNAP administrative data, age-based exemptions around the ABAWD boundary, and waiver periods, the NBER study reports that work requirements increased procedural and overall denials and interprets the pattern as participation deterrence through administrative burden. The public abstract does not disclose the full record-level denominator, selected jurisdictions, effect magnitudes, or every implementation detail. The design therefore supports a bounded policy-to-denial comparison, not a national denial rate or a complete claimant story. A procedural denial can reflect missing paperwork, failure to document an exemption, or another process route rather than final ineligibility. The missing middle remains notice, claimant effort, appeal, restored benefits, food security, health, work quality, trust, and political action.

The property-tax fairness experiment adds an information-to-contestation mechanism. In a U.S. property-tax field experiment, information about the average rate paid by other households increased perceived fairness, reduced the likelihood of filing an appeal, and was associated with willingness to contribute more when others paid more. This shows that administrative action can be shaped by comparative interpretation, not only by the amount owed or formal appeal access. The local tax setting and specific information treatment do not establish payment, compliance, public-service trust, durable legitimacy, political participation, household welfare, or whether lower appeal reflected acceptance rather than resignation or another contestation route.

The affirmative-action contracting study adds a firm-entry and public-market-access route. Census restricted business records report gradual downsizing after state contracting bans, especially for Black-owned businesses and larger minority- and women-owned firms; existing firms were not more likely to shut down, while new MWBEs were relatively less common. This separates survival from scale and entry: a firm can remain alive while the route into the market narrows. The study does not establish total contracting demand, replacement markets, worker welfare, public-service quality, community prosperity, legal merits, or whether the calibrated productivity/scrap-value result reflects a directly observed loss.

The SNAP data-governance conflict adds a public-benefit data-control route. DOJ reported lawsuits against four state SNAP agencies after a federal request for five years of applicant data, alongside 29 jurisdictions reported as providing data; a related court order temporarily restrained threatened funding action for most plaintiff states. USDA described a FedRAMP High National SNAP Information Database with need-to-know access, authentication, encryption, bounded routine uses, and more than 450 public comments, including roughly 132 privacy or improper-sharing concerns. This creates a cross-cutting accountability tension: data access may support verification while also raising surveillance, legal-authority, privacy, and chilling concerns. Agency claims, a temporary judicial ruling, system design, and public comments do not establish fraud prevalence, safe use, applicant notice, benefit loss, administrative performance, remedy, trust, or political action. The next test is a lawful de-identified episode through data purpose, notice/disclosure, verification, decision and payment timing, correction/appeal, privacy concern, interruption, and food/work/care outcomes, comparing sharing and contesting states without treating legal posture as a household effect.

4. Place is increasingly a bundle of opportunity, risk, and time costs

Mortgage lock-in constrains moves (mortgage lock-in); flood buyouts can reduce physical exposure but distribute destination gains unequally (flood buyouts); access to amenities, transport, establishments, and hospitals is geographically uneven.

Trend proposition: staying, leaving, and daily mobility are becoming central mechanisms connecting housing, work, care, risk, and belonging.
Open link: actual destination, service use, social ties, and long-run security.

The neighborhood/firm/intergenerational-mobility study adds a linked place-to-work mechanism. Census/LEHD evidence reports stronger between-neighborhood than within-neighborhood intergenerational earnings relationships, separate person-effect and establishment-premium channels, and a conditional neighborhood contribution to the Black–White earnings gap. It also reports a counterpattern in which higher Black-share neighborhoods have higher adult earnings conditional on neighborhood average earnings. These results make reachable firms, networks, and local institutions part of the place mechanism, but they are observational: they do not identify the effect of moving, fully explain racial earnings gaps, or establish belonging, health, political action, or household security.

The county health-capacity and vehicle-constraint cells add a place-level diagnostic rather than a national access rate. A 2023 Census county join separates low/high local health-establishment density from low/high zero-vehicle-household share, producing four distinct place cells with different HPSA, rurality, income, rent, commute, language, vacancy, wage, and building- permit contexts. This makes service availability and transport feasibility jointly visible while preserving that establishment density is not provider adequacy, zero-vehicle share is not failed access, and county medians are not resident outcomes. The next test is a named patient or household route through travel, appointment, payment, completed care, continuity, and later health or work, with neighboring supply, transit, telehealth, and public provision retained as alternatives.

The 2024 ACS PUMS mobility-subgroup layer adds household- and person-level inequality to the county diagnostic. Among households below $35,000 in past-year income, 22.25% had no vehicle versus 3.74% among households at $75,000 or more; among workers with positive commute minutes, 30-minute-plus commutes were 32.30% and 40.98% in those income groups, and 42.64% for Black workers versus 36.64% for White workers. These are descriptive cross-sectional PUMS contrasts: income is not current liquidity, commute duration excludes work-from-home records and is not total mobility burden, and race is not a mechanism. The layer does not establish transport affordability, reliability, care or health access, discrimination, missed activity, or political response. The next test is a household/person or place route through vehicle access, fares and reliability, care/work/medical travel, service use, stay/move, and local response, retaining transit, walking, rides, and job/housing sorting as alternatives.

The Treasury/FIO homeowners-insurance record adds a market and public-risk layer to the place pathway. Across 2018–2022 ZIP-code risk groups, Treasury reports that the highest expected-loss quintile had higher average premiums, higher nonrenewal rates, and higher paid-claim severity than the lowest-risk quintile; the underlying workbook covers roughly 80% of HO-3 and HO-5 direct-premium volume while retaining policy, ZIP-year, and risk-group limits. This makes climate exposure legible as a possible cost, availability, insurer-choice, and mobility pressure, but it does not establish household affordability, a received quote, actual coverage loss, claim adequacy, repair, mortgage access, relocation, or political response. The next test is a matched property or household route through hazard, premium/renewal decision, deductible and claim, repair financing, mortgage or public aid, and stay/move, with insurer mix, state rules, property condition, and residual-market routes retained as alternatives.

5. Digital choice is moving from a product question to a power and exit question

Search exposure can change quality beliefs and continued preference (search experiment); data deletion, cancellation, platform remedy, and AI exit records show that nominal choice often depends on information, switching effort, and recovery.

Trend proposition: platform power is increasingly mediated through attention, defaults, data, and the cost of leaving rather than only a posted price.
Open link: verified correction/deletion/exit and later wellbeing.

The NBER search-engine learning record provides a U.S. observed default-to-learning comparison beneath that proposition. In a randomized field experiment of about 2,300 U.S. desktop Google users, requiring active choice increased Bing's share by only 1.1 percentage points. A paid two-week Bing trial instead changed relative-quality beliefs, and 33% of trial users preferred to continue using Bing. The demand model's larger market-share and consumer-surplus estimates are counterfactuals, not universal observed gains. The result separates an interface prompt from experience-based updating, while leaving durable switching, accessibility, privacy, security, data use, consumer welfare, and political action open.

The Pew smartphone self-regulation record adds an attention-control mechanism to the platform-choice lane. In Pew's May 2026 American Trends Panel survey, 53% of adults said they spend too much time on their smartphone and 45% said they had tried to cut back during the prior year. Among those who tried, 25% described the effort as extremely or very successful, 52% as somewhat successful, and 23% as unsuccessful. The device also remains useful: 73% said it helps them stay informed and 51% said it helps them feel connected, while 34% said it hurts productivity and 41% said it hurts sleep. This is a practical-agency tension rather than a net-wellbeing score: the same device can protect information and social connection while creating attention or sleep costs. The record uses retrospective self-reports, not device logs, product-level exposure, clinical measures, or observed post-survey behavior; failed reduction may reflect work, care, safety, navigation, or information needs rather than simple lack of willpower. The next test is a repeated or logged user episode through notifications and obligation, cutback attempt, actual use and displacement, privacy or platform choice, switching/non-use, and later sleep, work, care, trust, or political action.

The AI assistant platform-choice record adds task-specific choice and privacy-knowledge stages. In a June 2026 survey of U.S. adult AI-assistant users, ChatGPT was the named primary platform for 58.2%, Gemini for 25.4%, Claude for 7.0%, and Copilot for 4.1%; about 43% reported intentionally routing different tasks to more than one platform. Claude's primary share was 33% for coding tasks versus 7% overall, showing how a smaller provider can occupy a valuable niche without displacing a dominant default. Trust also differed between aware nonusers and users of a platform, while more than 80% reported concern about conversation-data use, roughly 60% did not know whether their assistant trained on conversations, and only 18% had paid for a better-privacy plan. Knowledge of training policy was associated with more reported protective tools than a one-point increase in concern, but the study is cross-sectional and self-reported; the association is not a causal knowledge-to-action effect. Its willingness-to-pay estimates for avoiding human review, sponsored answers, or training use are hypothetical choice-experiment values, not purchases or provider changes. The next test is a user/task episode through platform selection, multihoming, disclosure and policy comprehension, data handling, actual protective action, retention or switching, affordability, and later trust or practical exit.

The California DROP data-broker deletion record adds an implementation-and-contestability stage to the privacy route. The California Privacy Protection Agency reports roughly 325,000 consumers signed up and submitted deletion requests by July 2026, later roughly 500,000 registrants, and coverage of more than 600 brokers—654 in the later status frame—through one request. DROP hashes entered information rather than storing or sharing raw data with brokers, and exposes broker-level statuses such as deleted, exempted, opted out of sale, not found, or pending. But the agency does not publish the distribution of those statuses, broker-match or deletion completion rates, exemptions, re-collection, or consumer-level trust and behavior. About 25% of brokers reported processing in the later observation; that is a broker-level implementation share, not a consumer completion rate. The 45-day processing cadence and up-to-90-day status delay also make time and visibility part of practical control. A centralized route can lower effort and increase agency even when records are not found or exempted, while an opt-out or deletion status does not independently prove removal of every record or durable privacy. The next test is one authorized consumer through request matching, broker status, verified deletion or exemption, re-collection, follow-up burden, trust, switching, and practical exit.

The social-media non-use and exit study adds a lived practical-exit mechanism as comparative evidence rather than a U.S. estimate. Interviews with 20 Korean adults in their twenties included active, deactivated, deleted, inactive, and partially deactivated Instagram accounts. Participants described non-use as nonlinear and reversible: spatial and temporal limits, app tactics, account actions, psychological discomfort, social pressure, exclusion, incomplete disconnection, and compensating use of other platforms all shaped whether leaving was practical. Participants generally did not frame personal non-use as collective or political action, even when criticizing monetization and attention capture. This helps separate intended exit from durable exit, data erasure, and collective power, while allowing that non-use can produce relief, offline connection, and control. Because the study is a small purposive Korean interview sample, it does not establish U.S. prevalence, platform-wide deletion friction, verified account reactivation or data erasure, wellbeing change, or political effects. The next U.S. test is a repeated user episode through intended non-use, interface and social pressure, alternative platform use, durable account status, data deletion, wellbeing, trust, and collective action.

The App Tracking Transparency/fraud study adds an observed consequence stage to this platform pattern. A natural- experiment analysis reports fewer consumer fraud complaints after mobile tracking and sharing restrictions, larger effects for personal-information misuse and higher-risk data-practice areas, and linked changes in dark-web discussion and sensitive-data prices. This is stronger than a privacy-policy announcement but remains a set of complaint, geographic-risk, and data-market surfaces rather than one consumer-loss panel. Complaint reduction may reflect less exposure, different reporting, channel substitution, or other concurrent changes; it does not establish reimbursement, account restoration, trust, or durable exit.

The Pew news-platform civic-engagement record adds a U.S. attention-to-civic-action typology without treating platform use as the cause of political behavior. Pew's late-2025 19-measure typology identifies 9% of adults as Mobilizers and 28% as Connectors; 87% of Mobilizers reported voting in 2024 and 65% contacting an official, compared with 41% and 3% among Outsiders. A separate March 2025 survey finds partisan news-source ecosystems across 30 named sources, while 21% of adults—37% under age 30—regularly receive news from social-media influencers; 65% of influencer-news users said that use helped them understand current events and 70% said the news differed from what they saw elsewhere. These are distinct surfaces: cluster membership, source familiarity/trust, influencer exposure, perceived understanding, and reported political action do not form one respondent panel or causal chain. The labels are descriptive types, not stable identities; use and trust may reflect prior identity, resources, institutions, or selective exposure, and perceived difference is not accuracy, persuasion, informed control, or polarization. The next test is a repeated U.S. user episode through platform/source exposure, content interpretation, trust, sharing or non-use, civic contact/vote, attribution, and later institutional response or exit.

The AI political-advice mechanism extends the same choice-and-power question into information authority. A formal NBER model shows how an adviser that values accuracy while also seeking perceived user welfare can have an incentive to accommodate what a user wants to believe; even sophisticated users may then receive less information about the underlying state, while users who underestimate the accommodation can mistake agreeable advice for evidence. This creates a distinct possible path from adviser design → perceived helpfulness → information quality and attribution → political judgment, polarization, or action. It is a theoretical mechanism, not an audit of deployed systems and not evidence of actual knowledge, radicalization, voting, trust, or platform behavior. The next test is to compare disclosed objectives, source visibility, competing-advice exposure, user priors and sophistication, belief updating, confidence, and later political behavior while preserving model, experiment, and observed-outcome boundaries.

The broader AI macro/infrastructure/institutional record shows why AI capacity should not be treated as one economic or geopolitical outcome. Its sources place short-run firm productivity and employment estimates beside descriptive state-industry output and employment associations, exploratory industry price and input-share results, modeled wage/wealth distribution channels, an OFR workforce-and-analytics restructuring, conditional data-center energy scenarios, World Bank complement requirements, and SHED household price adaptation and national economic judgment. Together they support a cross-source proposition that AI capability is complement-constrained and distributed unevenly across firms, workers, institutions, places, and states. They do not establish worker gains, household benefit, public-service improvement, provider switching, or geopolitical leverage. The next test is to follow one firm, worker, institution, or place through ownership and complements, access and task change, productivity or service output, price/wage incidence, bargaining and voice, local infrastructure burden, provider replaceability, and later trust or external response while preserving firm-study, state-industry, model, scenario, institutional-report, and household-survey units.

The FTC AI-companion inquiry adds an institutional-scrutiny stage to the platform pathway. In September 2025, the FTC issued Section 6(b) orders to seven companies seeking information about monetization, data use, safety testing, age limits, disclosures, and possible effects on children and teens. This is a governance response to a product form that may combine relational framing, data extraction, and consumer dependence; it is not evidence that users formed relationships, experienced harm, or lost practical control. The inquiry provides no user denominator, company responses, completed enforcement, outcome rate, or remedy. The next test is to follow a named product through terms, design and monetization, data practices, age/safety controls, user exposure and exit, complaints, company response, and any later enforcement or user outcome while preserving the inquiry-versus-finding boundary.

The GAO emerging-technology foresight review widens the governance timing question beyond currently deployed AI. GAO's expert-informed review of neural implants, general-purpose robots, and orbital- debris removal identifies possible privacy, autonomy, environmental, safety, and legal exposures alongside standards, human-confirmation, safeguards, research, and legal-analysis needs. It uses literature and 11 expert interviews for technologies roughly ten years on the horizon; it is not a forecast or realized impact estimate. The review does not establish adoption, prices, employment, privacy violations, accidents, public acceptance, market concentration, or geopolitical response. The next test is one named technology through prototype, deployment, affected user/worker/community, standards or human override, incident, remedy, public interpretation, and external response, preserving non-adoption and governance-before-harm cases.

6. Capacity can grow while local usability, affordability, or accountability lags

Hospitals centralized even as zero-hospital counties grew (hospital concentration); transmission selections need not become energized service (DOE transmission); large-load rules allocate projected costs without proving realised customer incidence (Virginia large load).

Trend proposition: infrastructure debates are increasingly about conversion from nominal capacity into accessible, affordable, resilient service.
Open link: asset/project → actual service → household/place consequence.

The state and local disaster-response capability record adds an intergovernmental capacity layer beneath FEMA assistance and survivor access. GAO's selected assessments of 10 states affected by recent disasters find wide variation in state and local capability, with rural or less-resourced jurisdictions facing challenges even inside otherwise well-resourced states. Officials describe continued dependence on federal grants, training, technical assistance, and response support, and emphasize clear guidance and transition time when federal roles change. This is an institutional comparison, not a national capability rate or a survivor panel: it does not measure response speed, notice, assistance receipt, repair, health, housing, local economic recovery, trust, or political action. Federal pooling may complement local capacity rather than signal local failure. The next episode should connect one jurisdiction's capability and federal support to a named disaster route, survivor receipt, repair or return, local recovery, and later public judgment.

The GAO duplication/fragmentation review adds a federal-coordination version of the same conversion problem. GAO's 2026 portfolio reports 610 remaining matters, 97 new matters, and $774.3 billion in reported cumulative savings or revenue increases from prior congressional and agency action, with more than $100 billion in potential additional gains. The portfolio also treats overlapping or fragmented responsibility as a possible efficiency, effectiveness, service-delivery, and oversight problem. Fiscal savings and recommendations are not household savings, agency productivity, service quality, transition cost, public trust, or evidence that consolidation improves access; some overlap can provide redundancy, specialization, or local entry points. The next test is one named program through responsibility, coordination and navigation burden, implementation, service quality, affected households/firms, cost, appeal, and later legitimacy, preserving both consolidation and redundancy counterexamples.

The Technology Modernization Fund record adds a realized-versus-projected public-capacity clock. GAO reports $1.03 billion invested across 68 federal projects, with 11 projects reporting $13.5 million in savings to date while 24 projects projected $1.06 billion in combined savings. The gap is analytically important: project-reported savings, future projections, implementation status, and public value are separate stages. Modernization may avert failure or improve service without producing financial savings, while a reported agency saving does not establish a user time gain, better access, cybersecurity improvement, worker benefit, budget availability, or household welfare. The review does not provide a common project-year implementation clock, distribution of savings, or user-level outcome, and agency accounting may not equal an independently verified budget reduction. The next test is one named project through award, migration and training, implementation, actual cash or avoided-cost effect, service reliability, user access, correction, reallocation, and later public response.

The GAO cybersecurity-harmonization record adds a firm-facing governance-cost layer. Seven selected critical-infrastructure industry representatives reported overlapping controls, inconsistent definitions, and incident-reporting deadlines or thresholds that can make rapid response technically burdensome; GAO also records 11 of 12 earlier coordination recommendations implemented and one partially addressed. This supports a mechanism in which regulatory complexity can shift scarce operational capacity into duplicated compliance and reporting, potentially affecting security practice or service continuity. It is not a national prevalence estimate or causal test: the record does not quantify compliance hours, dollars, breach reduction, reporting quality, consumer harm, or security improvement. The next test is one named operator through applicable rules, control mapping, incident, reporting time, response, service continuity, customer effect, and harmonization, while retaining sector-specific safeguards and non-incident cases.

The AI capability/dependence/state-control bridge adds a technology-specific version of this conversion problem. World Bank framework evidence identifies infrastructure, skills, institutions, data, and local-language fit as complements; Romania and Malaysia cases add project, financing, ownership, and completion milestones; OFR adds a U.S. public- institution workforce/data-capacity layer; U.S. data-center records add load, revenue, and utility-governance surfaces. These sources show how useful AI capacity and dependence can expand together. They do not establish provider replaceability, public-service improvement, household benefit, state bargaining leverage, or geopolitical consequence. The next test is one named capability through procurement/use, ownership, local complements, inspection, portability, replacement time/cost, outage or switching, local incidence, and external response.

The automation-and-meaning-of-work record adds a cultural and worker-control mechanism that is easy to miss when capacity is measured only through jobs or output. Its theoretical model shows that a credible machine alternative can reduce the contribution-based meaning workers attach to retained work even when the firm does not eliminate the job; full wage adjustment can offset part of the modeled meaning loss, while partial adjustment leaves more of it with workers. The mechanism therefore runs through perceived contribution, compensation, and the credibility of substitution—not employment status alone—and can feed back into the firm's incentive to automate. This is a model, not evidence that any observed workers lost meaning, pay, health, identity, motivation, or bargaining power, and it has no worker or firm denominator. Automation can also remove dangerous or tedious tasks, increase agency, improve quality, or create new sources of meaning. The next test is a named workplace or worker episode through task change, perceived contribution, discretion and voice, compensation, useful output, wellbeing, retention or mobility, and later identity or collective action.

The AI-worker retraining returns record adds an observed adaptation route beside the theoretical meaning mechanism. Using U.S. Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act occupational-training spells matched against job-search-only service users, the study reports about $1,000 in estimated quarterly earnings returns for high-AI-exposure trainees in 2012–2019 and about $3,000 in 2022–2024. The reported route is primarily a move into less AI-exposed occupations, with a smaller role for AI-complementary training. This makes retraining a possible agency and income bridge, but not a universal escape from automation: the public abstract does not disclose the full sample or matching specification, and the comparison remains a workforce- program population. The estimate does not establish training completion, access, affordability, job quality, hours, benefits, stability, control, meaning, or household security; participant motivation, health, time, transportation, employer support, labor-market selection, and available jobs may shape the result. The next test is one worker through exposure, training eligibility and completion, occupational transition, pay and hours, benefits, control and meaning, household adaptation, retention or exit, and later voice or political action.

The BLS worker-requested-changes and job-control record adds an observed request-to-grant stage to the worker-agency lane. In the July 2024 supplement, 5.9 million wage and salary workers reported requesting or making a change to help do their job better; schedule requests were fully granted for 70.1%, partially granted for 21.9%, and not granted for 7.5% of reported requests. Separate preliminary 2025 Occupational Requirements Survey estimates describe job-level conditions: pause control for 53.5% of workers, self-paced workload for 18.5%, and employer-driven schedule variability for 48.3%. The CPS request/grant and ORS job-level estimates are not the same-worker panel. A granted change may be inadequate, temporary, stigmatizing, or costly, and job-level control is not worker-level bargaining power. The missing route is request, decision, actual use, schedule/pay and care effect, persistence, voice, retention, or exit.

The data-center capacity/revenue/rate-governance bridge adds a sharper public-incidence layer. LBNL provides modeled national load; Prince William County reports local data-center tax revenue and capacity; state records specify approved, proposed, modeled, and undisclosed large-load terms; Virginia testimony records two reported large-load drop events. These stages show digital demand becoming a fiscal and utility-governance question, but modeled load, county revenue, tariff terms, and operational stress are not household bills, net public benefit, jobs, water/environmental incidence, provider replaceability, public trust, or geopolitical leverage. The next test must follow a named facility/customer through load, contract, cost recovery, service continuity, local incidence, and public response.

The Georgia large-load contract record advances this route from policy and forecast toward a visible regulatory implementation stage. The Georgia PSC docket identifies at least four executed large-load contract filings in January–August 2026 and a named review episode with a filing, review extension, and later executed-contract entry. This is stronger than a rule or announced demand because a contract submission is an actual oversight object, but the docket does not disclose commercial terms, minimum billing, final approval, construction, energization, payment, cost allocation, residential bills, reliability, or local acceptance. The next test is one named contract through approval, security/minimum-billing terms, build and load, payment, utility cost recovery, ratepayer incidence, reliability, and amendment or nonperformance.

The bulk-power security record adds a governance-timing layer to the capacity problem. Executive Order 14421 defines foreign equipment, software, remote access, sabotage, cyber exploitation, and supply disruption as bulk-power security surfaces and authorizes possible restriction, isolation, monitoring, replacement, or removal; DOE's subsequent RFI opens a stakeholder-input stage involving utilities, vendors, labor, insurers, governments, and consumer representatives. This makes electricity simultaneously an essential service and a strategic-security object, especially as data centers, advanced manufacturing, AI, and defense production increase reliable-load dependence. The order and RFI do not identify affected equipment, binding vendor determinations, replacement cost or schedule, outage or rate effects, stakeholder influence, household service, or geopolitical outcome. The next test is a named covered transaction through determination, replacement, cost, reliability, worker/community burden, public response, and later industrial or external behavior, with non-covered and transition-cost cases retained.

7. Dependence is reorganizing both local political voice and international bargaining

Local employer concentration can shape political communication and turnout (electoral monopsony); arms supplier concentration can increase maintenance and bargaining exposure without proving political obedience (SIPRI dependence).

Trend proposition: dependence is a shared structure across labor markets, platforms, infrastructure, and alliances, but its consequences remain context specific.
Open link: dependency → exercised leverage → changed behavior or autonomy.

The Pew global-role legitimacy record adds the missing reception-and-meaning stage without treating reputation as capability. The 2026 survey separates three audiences that should not be pooled: adults in 36 non-U.S. countries, the U.S. public, and U.S. partisan subgroups. Across the non-U.S. country medians, 35% said the United States contributes to world peace and 32% said it considers other countries' interests; among U.S. adults, the corresponding peace figure was 57%, while 83% said the United States interferes in other countries at least a fair amount and 65% said it should consider foreign interests even when compromise is required. Domestic partisan meaning was sharply divided: 82% of Republicans versus 35% of Democrats said the United States contributes to peace and stability, while 77% and 89%, respectively, described it as interfering. The same interference judgment can therefore coexist with opposed legitimacy interpretations. These are perception and normative-judgment surfaces, not observed diplomatic behavior, alliance coordination, material capability, leverage, or political action; the international results are equal-country medians rather than a population-weighted global estimate. The next test is an event-country-party- year ledger linking a dated foreign-policy or security event to country-specific reception, domestic attribution, diplomatic/alliance response, aid/trade or market behavior, institutional adjustment, and later action while preserving capability-versus-legitimacy and audience boundaries.

The domestic-capacity/autonomy realization record formalizes the multiple clocks inside that dependence question. Its deliberately non-pooled comparison places critical-mineral import reliance and substitution, CHIPS awards and milestones, data-center/grid capacity and load events, and JASSM/LRASM awards, tests, planned delivery, and missing acceptance fields side by side. It distinguishes commitment, capacity, operation, replaceability, and leverage: a $13.1 billion CHIPS disbursement, a $999 million production-support ceiling, a 621 MW local capacity addition, or a planned 2026–2030 delivery window does not establish usable output, customer receipt, supplier switching, interoperability, autonomy, or external bargaining power. The next test is one identifier-bearing mineral, project, facility, order, or customer through production, acceptance, operation, substitute qualification, affected workers or communities, disruption, and observed external response.

The international AI-infrastructure comparison adds a concrete external-dependence counterpoint. Romania's government-cloud cases separate a reported infrastructure-completion announcement from a separate application-migration target; Malaysia's Johor case places planned capacity, development-finance lending, and an ownership transition beside unobserved operation and public benefit. These cases show how digital capacity can expand while dependence on external operators, capital, power, customers, skills, or security arrangements may also persist. They are two documented project cases, not a population trend or leverage estimate: neither establishes uptime, service speed/cost, data control, portability, replacement, local incidence, state bargaining, or external response. The next test is one named facility or public application through commissioning, use, ownership/control, local complements, inspection, outage or switching, replacement time/cost, and public or counterpart response.

The Taiwan reciprocal-trade response adds an external-response counterpoint to the dependence pattern. An announced agreement can convert U.S. trade and technology pressure into partner tariff, investment, and purchase commitments, but accommodation is not yet exercised leverage. The reported $84.8 billion figure is a sum of planned categories, not realized trade; the source does not show contracts, delivery, production, local burden, supplier substitution, or changed strategic behavior. This keeps partner response, capacity alignment, and geopolitical leverage as separate stages.

The bilateral conflict/trade-risk record adds a measured geoeconomic pathway. A GDELT-based monthly bilateral indicator separates kinetic fighting, military posture, sanctions-context hostility, and routine diplomacy; a gravity-trade model reports that sanctions-context hostility has become a larger share of measured hostility, that directed hostility can compound trade losses through retaliation, and that roughly $334 billion of bilateral trade was at risk relative to a pre-escalation baseline in the paper's comparison, with about half attributed to the U.S.–China pair. This is a comparative model-based trade pathway, not observed lost revenue, coercive success, U.S.-household or firm incidence, substitution, alliance durability, legitimacy, or strategic result. Routine diplomacy's null aggregate trade result is not evidence that diplomacy lacks political or security value.

8. Social participation is a resource with unequal access, not a residual attitude

Neighborhood amenities correlate with contact and stated mutual aid (American Neighbor Survey); arts participation and support are socially patterned (NEA arts); civic styles do not map neatly onto government trust (Pew public life).

Trend proposition: belonging and civic capacity depend on places, time, money, health, and organizations—not only on individual disposition.
Open link: actual mutual aid/participation → institutional responsiveness or later durable belonging.

The BLS cultural-and-civic-time record adds an everyday time-use denominator to this social-infrastructure lane. On an average 2025 day, 30.4% of people age 15 and older socialized or communicated, 9.4% engaged in religious or spiritual activities, 5.3% volunteered, and 13.6% did an organizational, civic, or religious activity. Socializing and religious activity were higher on weekends and holidays than weekdays, while published volunteering time was 0.12 hours on both. These are primary-activity averages, not measures of relationship quality, informal help, digital contact, belonging, organizational capacity, or political action, and they do not follow the same person across years. The cross-lane implication is that civic participation has a time budget and calendar structure before it has a trust or legitimacy outcome.

The NEA arts participation and social-connectedness record adds a cultural-infrastructure access surface. Across four 2024 Household Pulse Survey cycles, 25.2% of adults reported attending a live performance or art exhibit in the prior month; attendance ranged from 16.2% among adults with annual income below $25,000 to 55.2% among those at $200,000 or more. Attendees also more often reported always or usually receiving needed social or emotional support than non-attendees. These are repeated cross-sectional associations, not a participation intervention or a measure of venue supply, transportation, time, affordability, relationship quality, belonging, health, or civic action. Arts attendance is therefore a socially patterned resource and support- associated surface, not evidence that arts access caused connection or institutional trust.

The Pew religious-institution community-bonds record adds a legitimacy-versus-use distinction to the social-infrastructure lane. In Pew's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, 80% of U.S. adults said religious organizations help bring people together and strengthen community bonds, and 78% said they play an important role in helping poor and needy people. Yet monthly in-person or online participation was 40% and personal congregation membership 37%; among the religiously unaffiliated, only 23% expressed a net positive view in the reported comparison. This means institutions can retain a publicly recognized community-service role even when belonging and participation are lower or unevenly shared. The measures are cross-sectional self-reports and do not show services actually delivered, recipient adequacy, institutional trust over time, political influence, or whether favorable judgments reflect personal use, identity, or general social evaluation. Valuing a community role does not imply accepting religious political influence, belonging to a congregation, or receiving aid. The next test is a congregation or community episode through need, invitation and access, contribution, service delivered, recipient adequacy, internal correction, burden-sharing, trust, belonging, and later civic action or exit.

The CDC community-connection record adds a measurement boundary between having support and being able to participate. CDC's displayed national indicators report that 58% of youth received the social and emotional support they needed in 2021–2023 and 82% of adults did so in 2021, while a separate 2024 adult indicator reports that 12% had difficulty participating in social activities because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition. These figures make support availability and participation capacity visibly distinct: reported support can coexist with health-related participation difficulty. They are not a common panel or a single-year age comparison—the CDC summary draws on different survey systems, does not reproduce all underlying denominators, and does not measure whether support was adequate, reliable, reciprocal, materially useful, or delivered by a formal institution. Participation difficulty also does not identify whether the barrier was access, energy, transportation, fear, cost, stigma, or lack of invitation. The next test is a repeated person or household route through support need, invitation/access, health and time constraint, actual participation, practical help, reciprocity, adequacy, recovery, and later belonging or civic action.

The American Neighbor Survey record specifies the local social-infrastructure mechanism without converting it into a community-resilience score. In the October 2025 probability-based survey, 40% of adults reported talking with neighbors at least a few times a week and 40% reported working with neighborhood residents during the prior year to fix a problem or improve the community; 72% reported at least a fair amount of trust that neighborhood residents do the right thing. The resource gradient is substantial: weekly conversation was 27% among adults reporting no listed neighborhood amenity within a 20-minute walk versus 43% among those reporting six or more, and neighbor trust was 64% among adults with a high-school degree or less versus 81% among college graduates. Emergency-childcare comfort also varied by parent education, from 39% among parents without a college degree to 56% among college-graduate parents. These measures place amenities, education, contact, trust, and stated mutual-aid readiness in one bounded survey surface, but they do not show that amenities caused interaction, that stated comfort became delivered care, or that neighborhood trust produced political action. The survey is cross-sectional and self-reported; selection, residence length, safety, mobility, income, and willingness to walk may shape both place exposure and reported connection. The next test is a place-linked or repeated episode through a dated request, actual help delivered, helper time or money, formal substitution, adequacy, recovery, later trust, belonging, and civic action.

The Pew Latter-day Saints communal-trust record adds an institution-centered counterexample to the neighborhood mechanism. In Pew's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study analysis, 72% of current Latter-day Saint respondents reported volunteering in the prior year, compared with 41% of U.S. adults overall, and 48% said most people can be trusted, compared with 34% of U.S. adults. The internal gradients prevent that group average from becoming a general communal-capacity claim: reported volunteering ranged from 91% among respondents with at least a bachelor's degree to 61% among those with less education, and from 80% in the Mountain region to 62% elsewhere; reported generalized trust was about 60% among college graduates versus about 40% among those without a college diploma. These are affiliation-group comparisons, not randomized effects of membership or observed service delivery. Volunteering does not identify hours, recipient, congregational versus outside work, burden, role access, or later political action, and generalized trust is not the same as institutional trust or delivered mutual aid. The next test is a congregation or community episode through invitation/access, time and care contribution, recipient or public benefit, internal correction, burden-sharing, belonging, and later civic action or exit, retaining former members and nonparticipants as countercases.

The material/care/time political-friction record adds a deliberately non-pooled route from practical constraints to political life. CPS reports schedule, illness/disability, transportation, and candidate/ issue dissatisfaction as distinct reasons among reported nonvoters; SIPP adds care-related work prevention and following-month hardship; ATUS measures eldercare providers' time allocation; ANES and CCES add worry, trust, vote, and action surfaces. Together they show why “nonparticipation” cannot be read as one cultural or material outcome. The record does not establish that care, cost, work, health, mobility, or institutional experience caused nonvoting, distrust, or candidate choice; same-person dated exposure, attribution, institutional response, remedy, and recovery remain open.

The material-to-trust/action bridge tests the next interpretive step without pretending that the sources form one panel. SIPP supplies work/resource transitions by work-limiting status; ATUS supplies standardized eldercare, work, and social-time contrasts; MEPS supplies longitudinal health and expenditure changes; ANES supplies financial-worry, trust, and reported-vote cross-tabs; and CCES supplies joint material-proxy, institutional-trust, and civic-action comparisons. Together they show that material, work, care, and health conditions can be placed alongside trust and action, but do not automatically become a common burden, attribution, distrust, or vote mechanism. The next test is a same-person or household route through a dated condition, practical consequence, explanation/attribution, institutional response, trust, and action or non-use; preserve each survey's denominator, time window, weighting, and noncausal boundary.

The Pew working-class identity record adds a meaning-and-attribution layer between material conditions and political action. In Pew's January 2026 American Trends Panel, 60% of adults said “working class” described them very or extremely well, up from 54% in 2024, but the self-classification does not map cleanly onto income or education: 67% of middle-income adults and 57% of lower-income adults used the label, as did 50% of upper-income adults; 66% without a college degree and 50% of college graduates did so. The label also varied by party (67% Republican or Republican-leaning versus 55% Democratic or Democratic-leaning), financial stress (65% medium, 61% high, and 50% low), and source-defined work type (77% blue-collar versus 61% other workers). These contrasts make identity a political and cultural interpretation of status, not a proxy for low income, objective class position, hardship, or ideology. The comparison is repeated cross-sectional, not a same-person identity transition; changing political language may raise self-identification without material deterioration, and occupation, respect, party, education, stress, and income are jointly structured. The next test is a same-worker or respondent episode through job quality, pay, stress, respect, identity, attribution, workplace voice, organizing, consumption, and political action, retaining people whose identity differs from their material or occupational position.

The Pew Latino American-dream mobility record adds a cultural-meaning counterexample to the assumption that perceived progress and perceived opportunity move together. In Pew's October 2025 National Survey of Latinos, 57% said their standard of living was better than their parents' at the same age and 53% expected their children to do better, yet respondents were split on attainability: 20% said they had achieved the American dream, 33% were on the way, 18% said it was out of reach, and 27% said it did not exist. The meaning is also multidimensional: about 60% mentioned financial security, economic opportunity, or homeownership, while 40% mentioned a good life for self, family, or community; freedom, hard work, and equal treatment were separate themes. Immigrant Latinos more often reported being better off than their parents (70% versus 49% among U.S.-born Latinos) and expecting their children to do better (63% versus 48%), but these are descriptive nativity contrasts, not migration effects. The survey measures reported judgment and reference-group comparison, not linked income/wealth mobility, housing access, legal security, voting, or political action. A person can experience family progress while judging the wider opportunity structure as unavailable. The next test is a family episode through housing, work, education, service or discrimination encounter, mobility, belonging, attribution, trust, and later consumer or civic action.

The guaranteed-income political-attitudes experiment provides a causal counterexample to a simple material-relief-to-mobilization story. In a randomized three-year field experiment with 1,000 low-income treatment participants receiving $1,000 per month and 2,000 controls receiving $50, the study reports no detectable average effect on political participation, engagement, broad preferences, government trust, polarization, or support for democracy, alongside small affect and work-attitude changes. The result narrows the claim that increased household room automatically produces political mobilization or institutional trust. It remains bounded by the enrolled population, intervention, political context, outcome precision, and take-up; it does not negate other material-to-political pathways or observe every household work, health, relationship, meaning, or action mechanism.

The historical winner-premium study adds a different route: a narrow presidential win in a historical U.S. county can be followed by party investment, local officeholding, outreach, organizing, and voter support, with larger reported effects in urban, Black, and union areas. This makes political power partly an organizational and resource accumulation process after an election, not only a one-day expression of preference. The 1940–1968 close-election design does not establish current effects, household material benefit, national policy, trust, or democratic legitimacy; later investment may also reflect broader realignment and local conditions.

9. Receipt and durable recovery are different outcome clocks

Lottery-assigned emergency rental aid increased rent payment and reduced eviction concern but had little average effect on housing stability or financial distress in its pandemic setting (rental aid). Conversely, one-time homelessness-prevention assistance was followed by employment and earnings gains in linked tax records, while its address-change evidence did not robustly establish housing stability (emergency assistance). The Medicaid hepatitis-C payment model increased treatment volume and improved diagnosis outcomes, while equal treatment reach and individual cure completion remained unobserved (Medicaid subscription).

Trend proposition: payment, receipt, immediate relief, clinical activity, and later durable security are independently measured outcomes. A program can produce a real intermediate gain without closing the whole lived-recovery route.
Open link: same person's offer → receipt → adequacy → durable housing, health, work, debt, and later judgment.

The HUD–LEHD assistance-employment trajectory adds a linked administrative transition beneath this proposition. Census CES links HUD assistance records from 2005–2022 to LEHD earnings records from 2000–2023 over long windows around entry and exit. Among about 2.086 million first-time entrants, the earnings slope changes from a pre-entry decline to a post-entry increase; among about 1.233 million final leavers, post-exit employment and earnings slopes are less favorable than among adjusted stayers. These are selected transition events and adjusted longitudinal comparisons, not a complete housing-recovery episode: assistance amount and timing, rent burden, housing quality, alternatives, exit reason, health, trust, and political action remain open. Entry/exit-to-labor trajectory is therefore a stronger linked stage, not proof that assistance caused earnings or that exit caused loss.

The HUD HEARS exit-type record adds a rare same-linked-unit housing-exit bridge. In linked records from the Seattle and King County housing authorities, 13.5% of 8,266 classified heads of household had positive exits, 54.9% neutral exits, and 31.6% negative exits. Among 16,301 linked individuals, homelessness or unstable housing within one year was recorded for 3.1% after positive exits, 14.5% after neutral exits, and 25.4% after negative exits; the study also links exit type to behavioral-health, emergency-department, and wage outcomes. This is stronger than a transition total because exit categories are connected to later outcomes, but it is a regional observational study with locally defined categories and probabilistic linkage. Pre-existing health, homelessness, income, market, and administrative conditions may shape both exit type and later outcome. It does not establish a national or causal exit effect, universal adequacy, trust, political action, or security beyond the one-year window.

10. Cost allocation can be hidden inside the transaction or rule rather than visible as a posted price

Payment-system evidence models merchant-fee costs passing through common prices and rewards across consumer payment types (payment rewards). The tariff study separates a direct import-price effect from delayed input-cost/domestic-markup effects (tariff pass-through). Rent guarantees can make lease access possible while leaving the renter liable to repay a landlord-facing claim (rent guarantee). Large-load governance assigns projected network costs before household bill incidence is demonstrated (Virginia large load).

Trend proposition: many consumer and public burdens are allocation mechanisms embedded in payment rails, eligibility products, supply chains, and rate rules; the person bearing the residual cost may not be the visible counterparty. Open link: rule/contract → actual bill, fee, repayment, or price → service and household consequences → contestation, switching, or acceptance.

11. Control increasingly depends on status visibility and a reversible route, not nominal consent

Marketplace brokers can remain formally registered while consent and plan-change controls fail (Marketplace broker consent). SNAP EBT security modernization adds cards, PINs, replacement monitoring, and fallbacks without showing timely food access after a theft or error (SNAP EBT modernization). Digital-health enforcement similarly ties sensitive data, recurring billing, and cancellation to a separate complaint-resolution endpoint (Hims & Hers).

Trend proposition: consumer and beneficiary control is increasingly realized through whether people can see a status, understand a change, reverse it, and obtain timely correction—not merely whether a transaction began with a formal consent or an account exists.
Open link: status/notice → challenge or reversal → corrected coverage, payment, data, or food access → durable trust and control.

The SNAP EBT theft-control ledger adds a concrete beneficiary-control route. GAO reports more than $320 million in approved replacement benefits for nearly 679,000 households during the statutory replacement period, alongside card locks, PIN changes, transaction monitoring, chip-card and mobile-payment pilots, processor controls, and other prevention measures. The route makes visible how a formal benefit can become a loss of food-purchasing control, recipient monitoring and reporting work, and a possible shift of fraud risk between households, states, federal agencies, processors, retailers, and recipients. Approved claims are not a theft prevalence denominator or proof of individual receipt; the retained record has no theft date, claim effort, approval timing, replacement delivery, interim food route, next-month balance, adequacy, trust, or exit. Security controls may reduce theft while adding steps, false blocks, accessibility problems, or online/cross-state constraints, and a household may bridge a loss through borrowing or charitable food without a recorded replacement. The next test is one household through theft, discovery, report, claim, replacement decision and receipt, security response, interim food access, next benefit, repeat exposure, trust, continued use, or non-use.

The consumer visibility/attention/remedy/exit synthesis shows why those endpoints must remain separate. CFPB complaint-response labels change across years and products; SHED reports that many adults who experienced non-credit-card fraud lost money, some did not recover it, and a substantial share spent ten or more hours dealing with it; Pew and FTC provide separate platform/AI-use, privacy-confidence, and institutional-inquiry surfaces. These records jointly map visibility, attention cost, response labeling, and exit questions, but they are not a complaint incidence rate, same-case recovery panel, trust measure, or causal regulatory effect. A high explanation share or high platform use cannot be promoted to adequate remedy, dependence, or continued consent.

The consumer-loss and recourse bridge sharpens that distinction by placing four non-pooled stages side by side: household-reported fraud exposure, conditional money and time loss, FTC administrative visibility, and CFPB complaint-response labels. The 2024 SHED layer estimates exposure and conditional loss/recovery effort; FTC totals show reported administrative scale; CFPB records show explanation, narrative, and monetary-relief labels whose composition and coding can change. This supports a cross-source proposition that consumer protection can be visible and routable without yet being experienced as adequate recovery or trust repair. It does not establish a common fraud rate, firm liability, account-level remedy, restored security, switching, or exit. The next test is a dated same-consumer episode linking originating contact, loss, recovery effort, complaint, remedy receipt and adequacy, repeat exposure, and later behavior.

The Amazon Prime refund-distribution record adds a named, large-scale remedy while making the receipt gap unusually visible. The FTC-filed September 2026 status record reports about $845.2 million issued across automatic and claims-process phases but about $235.5 million accepted; these are phase-level dollar totals, not a customer denominator, individual receipt rate, average payment, or adequacy measure. The underlying settlement sets eligibility rules and a maximum refund, while later administration expands automatic eligibility and preserves a reissue route. This is evidence of legal remedy design and reported distribution/acceptance stages, not proof that every eligible customer received money, recovered time, restored household room, or changed continued use, switching, trust, or renewal behavior. The difference between issued and accepted can reflect timing, unredeemed or voided payments, cohort composition, or payment-channel friction; it should not be interpreted as one uniform recovery rate. The next test is an authorized account-level or administrator follow-up through eligibility, notice, claim, payment receipt, amount, delay, reissue, continued use or exit, and later material or trust outcome.

The newer FTC Amazon Prime redress-route record adds a later program-design and issuance stage. The September 2026 FTC update reports $845 million in aggregate redress issued by that point, a maximum of $200 per eligible consumer under the revised order, and automatic payment channels through mailed check, PayPal, or Venmo without a new claim form for future payments. The aggregate amount is not a consumer denominator, receipt rate, accepted amount, account correction, or restored household security; future payment stages were still scheduled. Automatic distribution can reduce claim-form burden while leaving notice, reachability, payment acceptance, subscription correction, continued use, switching, trust, and recovery open. This is a distinct remedy-design comparison with the earlier issued-versus- accepted record, not a replacement for account-level follow-up.

12. Operating capability is increasingly observable before its social or strategic effect

988 increased local crisis-line answer capacity without a detectable short-run population mortality change (988 capacity). NATO ISR recorded flight hours, intelligence products, and decision-support use without an attributed deterrent effect (NATO ISR operations). VA EHR deployment plans persist alongside unresolved user-readiness and operational-validation issues (VA EHR modernization).

Trend proposition: operational metrics—connections, answers, deployments, hours, products, or users—are becoming more legible than the downstream outcomes they are meant to enable. They are meaningful realization evidence, not substitutes for a care, public-service, or strategic endpoint.
Open link: operating output → decision/service quality → individual or partner outcome → durability and legitimacy.

The USPS service-performance record adds a public-network example in which financial-sustainability measures can change practical time and geographic access. GAO reports that USPS added one to two expected delivery days for some First-Class Mail products after shifting transport from air to ground, eliminated end-of-day or afternoon collection at more than 24,000 post offices located over 50 miles from a Regional Processing and Delivery Center, and has missed most lowered service-performance targets since fiscal year 2021. Oversight entities and stakeholders reported slower service and disproportionate rural effects. This is stronger than a planned cost-saving announcement because standards, operational changes, and target performance are visible, but it is not a household or business delivery-time panel, a quantified cost-savings estimate, a complaint rate, or a causal rural burden estimate. Product, route, customer reliance, digital substitution, and local processing differ; longer standards may reduce costs or improve financial sustainability, and not every customer experienced slower service. The next test is a named route and customer or business use through standard, collection, scan delivery, missed deadline, substitution, cost, complaint/correction, continued use, and exit.

The FEMA workforce and mission-readiness record adds a federal public-capacity route from staffing to assistance access. GAO reports more than 4,300 FEMA separations in fiscal year 2025, reported loss of institutional knowledge, heavy deployment during Hurricanes Helene and Milton, only 4% of the incident-management workforce available after that deployment, and anticipated 2026 surge support below 2025 levels. It also reports long waits and unanswered calls for Texas flood survivors after reduced surge-support and call-center staffing. These observations support a capacity-to-access mechanism, but not a representative contact-failure rate, survivor-level causal wait time, denial, housing or income effect, or trust/political response. The next test is a named disaster or applicant route through staffing and demand, contact, notice, application, assistance receipt, repair or return, and later security, while retaining augmentation, call volume, and systems explanations as alternatives.

The defense-resource/procurement/capability bridge extends the same realization clock across state power. SIPRI and IMF provide spending and conditional fiscal-tradeoff context; USAspending provides a bounded award/subaward structure; U.S. and Polish records add contract, production-capacity, financing, co-production, and acquisition stages. These are useful resource-to-capability observations, but unlike units and source methods must remain separate. Spending, a contract ceiling, a budget line, reported facility activity, or a co-production announcement is not accepted output, operational readiness, replaceability, public legitimacy, deterrence, or geopolitical leverage. The next valid unit is an identifier-bearing order or lot followed through obligation, production, delivery, acceptance, use, maintenance, substitute qualification, affected workers/communities, and observed external response.

The SIPRI military-expenditure record provides the monetary and alliance-scale input to that realization clock. SIPRI estimates $2.887 trillion in global 2025 military expenditure, with U.S. spending estimated at $954 billion and down 7.5% in real terms, Chinese spending at $336 billion and up 7.4%, European spending up 14%, and NATO accounting for about 55% of global spending. These estimates make resource allocation, alliance burden, and possible social-spending trade-offs visible, but monetary spending is not procurement delivery, readiness, interoperability, deterrence, household incidence, or geopolitical leverage; country estimates, exchange rates, accounting, and aid treatment matter. The next test is one budget or procurement line through appropriation, contract, delivery, acceptance, readiness/use, supplier/work effects, public attribution, alliance response, and household or social-program incidence, preserving the 2025 data vintage.

13. Informal support is functioning as a timed buffer, but it is not evenly available or automatically substitutable

Young adults reported much greater outside-household expense help than older adults, while income volatility and bill difficulty remain uneven (SHED income volatility and support). When EITC generosity reduced intensive care from adult daughters, the study did not observe replacement through financial transfers, other family care, or paid care (EITC parent care). Food, social, and care routes also depend on scheduling, transport, and availability rather than an abstract support label.

Trend proposition: households increasingly bridge shocks through timed family, network, and unpaid-care arrangements, but that buffer has its own availability, burden, and replacement limits.
Open link: need → support request/receipt → time and financial transfer → care/food/work continuity → reciprocity, burden, and later security.

The SIPP childcare-work-hardship bridge adds a same-person transition surface to the care/time route. Among stable SNAP-status person pairs, the small subgroup reporting that childcare prevented work or additional work had higher following-month hardship point estimates than the corresponding no-prevention subgroup: within stable no-SNAP pairs, rent/mortgage hardship was 7.4% versus 4.9% and utility hardship 16.9% versus 7.8%; within stable SNAP pairs, the estimates were 43.7% versus 12.0% for rent/mortgage hardship and 32.4% versus 19.4% for utility hardship. The no-SNAP prevention cell contains 71 valid pairs and the SNAP prevention cell 23, with wide intervals, so the contrast is a bridge and prioritization signal, not a stable population rate or causal care effect. The annual fall work-prevention measure is attached to a December hardship observation; it does not identify the exact care event, quality or price of care, income change, benefit effect, substitution, or later recovery. Rent/mortgage and utility hardship also have separate valid universes. The next test is a dated household-care episode through care need and alternative, work prevention, income/benefit route, bill or housing hardship, substitution, support receipt, recovery, and later trust or political action.

The wildfire-smoke caregiving record adds an external-shock route into that care buffer. The NBER study uses plausibly exogenous wildfire-smoke variation to connect smoke exposure with school closures and student absenteeism, creating intermittent caregiving demand even where formal childcare exists. The reported pattern is a contemporaneous labor-market cost for mothers and a decline in maternal employment when caregiving shocks accumulate; employment losses were offset among mothers outside male-dominated industries, consistent with workplace tolerance moderating the cost of intermittent care demand. This is a useful bridge from environmental exposure to care time and work continuity, but not a complete household episode. The retained public abstract does not provide all sample restrictions, coefficients, household-income or hours effects, schedule control, childcare substitution, health, housing, or later recovery. Industry gender composition is a proxy for workplace tolerance, not a direct measure of employer policy or bargaining power, and formal childcare availability is not equivalent to replacement care during a school disruption. The next test is a mother-child-place episode through smoke exposure, closure/absence, care arrangement, work schedule and pay, employer accommodation, health, housing, and later employment or care continuity.

The family-care/public-capacity synthesis adds a wider institutional frame. GAO's kinship-family and assisted-living reviews, SIPP childcare/work screens, SHED adult-care reports, and ATUS diary contrasts show distributed care across relatives, paid providers, households, workers, and Medicaid programs. The surfaces differ in unit and clock: a population frame, state coverage/spending, conditional person records, survey reports, and diary-day time allocation. Together they support a mechanism in which public capacity and family capacity can coexist while private time, money, housing, and work costs remain uneven. They do not establish a common care-burden rate, verified support receipt, quality, health/school outcome, trust, political action, or same-family recovery.

The ATUS eldercare-roster record adds a relationship-and-place constraint to that distributed-care picture. Among ATUS provider respondents with at least one listed recipient, roughly 79% in both 2024 and 2025 listed a non-household recipient, while roughly 21%–23% listed a household recipient; provider-level roster counts and recipient rows are kept separate. This makes care beyond co-residence visible, but annual samples are not a panel and do not measure distance, hours, burden, relationship quality, shared caregiving, paid substitution, schedule control, recipient outcome, recovery, trust, or political action. The next test is a family or care episode through recipient location and need, travel, hours, alternatives, paid/unpaid replacement, work and social time, support receipt, and later continuity.

The ATUS eldercare-provider-time record adds the diary-day currency to that relationship map. In the unstandardized 2024 and 2025 annual samples, eldercare-provider respondents averaged about 68.7 and 66.8 minutes of eldercare on the diary day, respectively, while nonprovider respondents are zero by construction. Providers also averaged more primary household work (142.6 versus 116.5 minutes in 2024; 136.6 versus 116.8 in 2025), more travel (71.8 versus 63.7; 67.3 versus 61.4), and more primary care-for-people time (39.2 versus 31.6; 39.6 versus 30.0), but less primary paid work (179.8 versus 192.8; 172.9 versus 188.0) and less socializing/communicating (264.1 versus 275.4; 265.0 versus 278.1). Secondary childcare is also lower among providers (55.5 versus 80.7; 60.0 versus 75.3). These are separate activity currencies: they should not be summed into a burden score or read as evidence that eldercare caused a work or social-time loss. Provider status is defined by a linked roster record, the annual samples are not longitudinal, and the contrasts remain sensitive to age, employment, health, household composition, recipient needs, and diary-day composition. The linked standardized records therefore matter: basic age × sex × labor-force-status adjustment gives an eldercare contrast of about 64.2/64.0 minutes, while adding household-child status and broad education gives about 70.7/62.4, demonstrating composition sensitivity rather than a single stable effect. The next test is a dated care episode that follows which time currency is displaced or protected, who absorbs the work, whether schedule control or paid substitution changes, and whether continuity, health, recovery, trust, or political action follows.

The care/time/material/mobility bridge adds the place-and-movement surface: SIPP links child-care work prevention to following-month hardship in small conditional cells; SHED shows caregiver financial adaptations; ATUS shows care-time, paid-work, travel, and socializing contrasts; NHTS supplies urban/rural vehicle, work-trip, and shopping-trip context. These records support a recurring pattern in which care constraints are paid through several currencies—work opportunity, time, savings, household room, and mobility—rather than one burden channel. They are not a common care-burden index or causal care effect, and do not establish unmet care access, recovery, trust, political action, or a same-unit trigger-to-outcome path.

The SHED financial-path/health/care panel adds an explicit same-respondent transition test. In the paired 2024–2025 panel, financial worsening, stability, and improvement each contained mostly unchanged health paths, and adult-care entry occurred within all three financial paths. The result is analytically useful as a counterexample to a synchronized household-state story: financial improvement does not automatically produce health improvement, financial stability does not eliminate health worsening, and care entry cannot be assigned to financial deterioration from these descriptive tables. It does not measure care hours or intensity, verify a financial shock, identify the trigger, establish causality, or observe trust, political action, institutional remedy, or later security. The next test is a dated respondent or household episode linking the financial event and care responsibility to hours, health, work, support receipt, substitution, and later recovery.

14. Aggregate averages increasingly hide opposed adaptation paths

Among low-liquid-wealth consumers, 49% spent below and 51% above a modeled frictionless benchmark (low-liquidity consumption wedges). Oregon Auto-IRA exposure was associated with more retirement and liquid-account ownership alongside more short-term card debt (Auto-IRA balance sheet). Preserving a distressed homeowner's house raised housing wealth without raising consumption (mortgage modification).

Trend proposition: identical headline directions—more saving, more spending, more assets, or more assistance—can reflect opposing household positions and constraints. Distributional mechanisms need to be treated as their own evidence layer rather than inferred from an average effect.
Open link: starting liquidity/asset position → adaptation and debt route → short- and long-run consumption/security → later mobility or recovery.

The SIPP assistance/housing-pressure context screen adds a month-ordered counterexample to a pooled assistance interpretation. In the streamed 2025 SIPP file, the monthly assistance field supports a descriptive next-month comparison: reported rent or mortgage difficulty was higher among month-t assistance-exposed pairs than unexposed pairs in the pooled screen, but the direction reversed among renters below twice the poverty line and differed again in higher resource bands. The assistance field is a combined clothing/housing-assistance measure, and the screen does not identify benefit amount, adequacy, application effort, landlord response, arrears, eviction, homelessness, or causality. Its value is methodological and distributional: assistance can coexist with pressure, and aggregate contrasts can conceal protective and adverse paths under different resource and tenure conditions.

15. Unequal distribution becomes political or geopolitical meaning through attribution

The cross-source evidence repeatedly separates an output from the way an affected person, community, institution, or partner interprets that output. Housing assistance, energy support, care, civic infrastructure, platform remedies, and public-safety response can be formally offered while receipt, adequacy, delay, burden, visibility, and alternatives differ. CIRCLE, CDC, ATUS, and SIPP records make support, participation, and next-period material conditions distinct; they do not identify a single household's attribution or later political judgment. The cultural source-led map similarly shows that hardship, fairness, belonging, trust, and action do not form one ladder.

The state and geopolitical records have the same structure at a different scale. Allied agreements, staffing, exercises, ISR output, local security coordination, and an air-policing response can be visible operational stages; they are not automatically partner consent, public legitimacy, deterrence, or changed counterpart behavior. Official claims of unity or protection are observed interpretations by the reporting institution, not evidence that all audiences shared them.

Trend proposition: distribution becomes meaning through the interaction of incidence, visibility, attribution, feasible alternatives, and response. The same capacity can be experienced as protection, extraction, abandonment, dependence, or solidarity by different actors. This is a comparative mechanism, not a pooled causal estimate.

Open link: dated condition or operation → affected unit and burden → available explanation and alternatives → attribution and action/non-use → institutional or counterpart response → later trust, belonging, recovery, acceptance, resistance, or adaptation. Preserve whether the source measures reported interpretation, official framing, observed action, or an independently verified outcome.

Use boundary

This portfolio is a synthesis of source-specific propositions. It is not a forecast, a common-rate estimate, or a claim that each pathway is rising at the same pace nationally. Each linked record supplies its own method, denominator, and counterinterpretation.
