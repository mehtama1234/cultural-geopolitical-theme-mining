US cross-source trend synthesis v1

Checked: 2026-09-15
Status: living cross-source synthesis; no single causal chain claimed
Scope: the current machine-readable trend/case registry and its linked analysis layers

Read this synthesis first

This atlas is currently strongest at showing how pressures and capabilities are distributed, how people and institutions adapt, and where the next link in the chain is still unmeasured. It is not yet a single model that explains political or geopolitical behavior from household conditions. The useful reader question is therefore: which stage is directly observed, which stage is compared across sources, and which stage remains an open test?

The rest of this page moves in four passes: the joint proposition, the evidence by arrow, the segments that are genuinely end-to-end, and the next research cycle. Each section keeps a direct observation beside its limitation and next test so that a compelling theme does not silently become a causal claim.

This is the current end-to-end reading of the atlas. It is deliberately a bridge across different units, not a merge of respondents, households, complaints, workers, places, or countries. The machine-readable trend registry is the source index; each record's boundary controls how far its evidence can travel.

The automatic-saving multi-currency bridge adds a policy-to-household sequence across NBER/CRI Auto-IRA studies, Federal Reserve SHED liquidity and credit, and BEA aggregate income and spending. It does not pool the estimates: the policy studies use different treatment designs and balance-sheet definitions, while the Federal Reserve and BEA layers are context rather than treatment outcomes. The surviving claim is that automatic saving can move current liquidity, future assets, and retirement timing on different clocks; the net-security and meaning/action arrows remain open.

The SIPP utility-credit-savings finding now adds a sharper household-room bridge inside the material and institutional route layers. In the same person-record/month diagnostic, reported utility payment difficulty co-occurs with more carried credit balances and less savings account ownership, while higher-resource respondents within the difficulty group are more likely to carry balances—an access counterexample, not evidence that they face less hardship. The separate following-month utility-to-work finding places utility difficulty or energy-assistance status beside next-month earnings and hours. These are adjacent descriptive stages, not a bill-to-credit-to-work causal estimate.

The directional SIPP work/resource finding sharpens the work/time bridge: earnings changes can rise or fall, while hours are unchanged for most adjacent-month pairs. Work-limitation and resource position condition the direction distribution, but no bill, health event, employer decision, care substitution, or later meaning/action is observed.

The new ATUS work-location finding adds a published 2025 work-location surface to the time architecture. Home and workplace indicators overlap, and their sex and education gradients are visible, but location does not identify schedule control, total workload, care conflict, or worker voice. The federal-shutdown coverage warning in the 2025 release is retained as a measurement caveat.

The new SIPP tenure/resource finding adds a replicate-weighted intersection to the household-room surface. Renters report more housing and utility-payment difficulty than owners within each displayed resource band, including at the highest band, while the food-hardship measure follows a different pattern and has its own universe. This sharpens the alternative-space question—resources do not erase tenure-linked exposure—but does not identify rent, local price, repair, insurance, debt, or a dated bill as the mechanism.

The new household-room layer puts that SIPP comparison beside the Federal Reserve's emergency-liquidity and linked-credit measures. It separates resource position, tenure, hypothetical emergency capacity, and realized card-balance exposure rather than treating them as one security index. The combined reading is that household room is about available alternatives when a dated cost arrives; the same-person event, time/care substitution, protected outcome, and later recovery are still open.

The new multi-clock material/time/care synthesis connects that open middle to the longitudinal evidence already acquired. SIPP shows monthly resource and work transitions for the same people; MEPS follows health spending, coverage, employment, wages, and perceived-health change in a panel; SHED and ATUS add emergency routes, care, and time allocation. The combined evidence supports distinct material, time, health, and work clocks, not a pooled household stress score. A complete dated trigger → alternative → protected/sacrificed outcome → recovery → meaning/action chain remains the program's acquisition target.

The new MEPS expenditure-conditioned finding deepens the health-cost/work bridge without turning spending into a burden score. Baseline 2022 expenditure bands have different perceived-health and employment-continuity profiles, but the differences remain confounded by need, age, insurance, retirement, and service mix. The correct next question is not whether high spending “caused” work loss; it is which treatment, coverage, out-of-pocket, unpaid-care, and baseline-employment paths explain the distinct outcomes.

The new public-system route-to-judgment synthesis extends the safety-net chain through institutional meaning without pooling incompatible respondents. USDA/FNA measures administrative timeliness, WBNS measures recipient route barriers, SIPP measures following material hardship, and Gallup measures institution-specific confidence. Together they support a layered route/buffer/judgment architecture; they do not show that one notice caused one hardship or changed one person's trust or political action.

The new CFPB route-vintage audit adds a measurement-integrity result to the consumer-power lane. In live 2024/2025 product samples, timely does not substitute for elapsed routing time, and the returned has_narrative field is unavailable in the re-query. The public API therefore supplies a route/schema diagnostic, not a remedy or consumer-welfare trend; omitted fields remain missing rather than zero.

The new USAspending supplier-concentration finding adds a quantitative replaceability check to the geopolitical/state-capacity lane. One JASSM/LRASM subaward extract has 51 reported recipient names, but the top five receive 49.50% of returned amount and the top ten 74.60%. The result keeps component diversity, financial concentration, production, replaceability, and external leverage as separate stages.

The entity-parent-facility reconciliation adds an official identity layer to that supplier test. DLA CAGE records make the apparent BAE route legible from the Merrimack recipient to BAE Systems, Inc. and BAE Systems plc, and provide a parallel General Dynamics-OTS to General Dynamics Corp route. This improves the jurisdiction and facility denominator without resolving the central capability question: parentage is not facility-level assignment, production, acceptance, delivery, replaceability, or external leverage. The next evidence must join the exact identifiers to procurement and performance records.

The follow-up recipient-identifier ambiguity audit tests that missing join directly. A name search returns 17 BAE recipient rows across 10 UEIs and three General Dynamics-OTS rows, while the retained JASSM/LRASM subaward response exposes no UEI or CAGE. One General Dynamics UEI overlaps the selected DLA facility set, but that intersection does not assign the $80.55 million subaward to a plant. The evidence therefore strengthens the measurement boundary: legal identity, facility identity, award action, and production location remain separate until an identifier-bearing performance record appears.

The richer subaward-to-UEI and location finding then closes an important measurement step. The official award-search route returns UEI, recipient-location, and primary-performance-place fields for all 74 rows, including exact identifiers for the selected BAE and General Dynamics-OTS actions. The locations are Nashua and Niceville, not automatically the previously selected DLA CAGE facilities. This turns the next question into a real CAGE-to-site and production/acceptance/delivery join rather than a name search, while preserving the operational and geopolitical arrows as open.

The facility-capability context finding adds a meaningful middle layer. Official BAE quality material places military electronics design/production/servicing scope at the Nashua address, and official General Dynamics-OTS material names Niceville Operations and advanced-warhead capability. The sources make those locations plausible industrial sites without proving that the selected subaward work occurred there. Production, acceptance, delivery, alternatives, and external response remain separate gates.

The realization-stage milestone finding adds dated movement beyond procurement intent: a DoD JASSM/LRASM production-support IDIQ, Lockheed Martin capacity investment/ramp-up evidence, and a completed first phase of LRASM F-35C flight-science testing. This strengthens the production-support and integration stages while preserving the missing accepted quantity, delivery, fielding, maintenance, replaceability, and external-response links.

The Poland JASSM-ER delivery non-observation keeps the foreign customer stage honest. The May 2024 agreement's 2026–2030 window and the later 2028–2030 schedule reference conflict without a verified amendment, and the reviewed official source set still lacks a delivery, acceptance, fielding, training, or inventory event for the order. The absence is bounded to public-source review; it is not evidence that no delivery has occurred.

The historical Polish audit-control finding adds an institutional-observability layer: NIK found that a JASSM-ER production-cost settlement relied on milestone/process material without a numeric value tied to the settlement. This is evidence about documentation and control of realization, not a missile quantity or delivery estimate. The separate 32-unit comparator is retained as a general Polish defence-contract example and is not attributed to JASSM.

The PSID 2023 field-audit finding closes a different acquisition gap. Official documentation confirms a large panel family file with work, unpaid labor, care, perceived time pressure, health, income, wealth, insurance, and utility fields. The fields make a longitudinal material/time/care test feasible, but they do not yet supply a dated event, attribution, recovery, trust, or political-action result.

The FMS timing-asynchrony finding shows why the realization arrow needs multiple clocks. NIK records four F-35 deliveries in December 2024 and January 2025 while the related settlement forms arrived in 2025, and states that FMS delivery schedules are estimates. The comparator improves the measurement design but does not establish a JASSM-ER delivery or operational capability.

The historical GSS finding adds a long-run meaning countercheck: financial dissatisfaction is associated with lower trust and fairness expectations in each selected anchor year, but the gap and overall trust level change across decades. This supports a relational meaning route without turning financial position into a stable causal explanation of social trust or political action.

The SHED annual fraud comparison adds a second annual household sample to the consumer-power row. The domestic capacity and state-leverage bridge now integrates SIPRI resources, procurement transactions and modifications, realization milestones, ownership context, and international infrastructure cases. The resulting cross-source chain is staged rather than collapsed: resource allocation and commitment can precede operation, and operation can coexist with external control or weak replaceability. Leverage remains open until another actor changes behavior.

The state-capacity synthesis now makes that distinction concrete in the data-center lane. LBNL's 176 TWh 2023 estimate and 649 TWh 2030 reference scenario describe national modeled demand; Prince William County's $465.9M reported TY2025 tax revenue describes local fiscal capacity; Virginia filings describe modeled residential-incidence illustrations and reported large-load drop events. These are not one benefit/cost estimate. Together they show the political economy of infrastructure: growth can create public revenue while leaving customer allocation, interruption risk, inspection, portability, and community incidence unresolved. The missing state-capacity test is not another load forecast; it is observed cost allocation, local experience, control or switching rights, and an external actor changing behavior.

The new Poland reciprocity and co-production finding adds an official response-stage layer: Poland's 2026 announcements describe potential buyer/seller access in a US-linked UAS/C-UAS arrangement and planned regional Barracuda B500M co-production with the US, Estonia, and Latvia. This is evidence of institutional and industrial response around a procurement relationship, not evidence of output, delivery, public reception, replaceability, or bargaining leverage. The next test is to observe the named transaction, plant, workforce, component-control, delivery, or switching event.

The July 6 Bydgoszcz implementation account adds a more specific stage inside that response: assembly followed by production at PGZ/WZL-2, reported PGZ exclusivity for a Polish version, and technology/know-how/competence transfer language. Those are agreement terms, not commissioned output or independently verified control rights. An August 19 BBN follow-up with Anduril adds a dated senior-level governance checkpoint focused on the Barracuda-500M program and Polish implementation competencies. It still does not establish a production milestone, output, delivery, or amended contract. The Ministry of State Assets' detailed production page adds reported targets of thousands of systems and staged Polish/European supplier localization. These make scale and local-content denominators testable, but remain plans rather than completed units or observed supplier shares. The September 9 Polish-US FMF agreement adds a broader financing checkpoint: Poland reports $4 billion in Foreign Military Financing and acceleration of existing contracts, including Apache helicopters. It is not Barracuda-specific and therefore belongs to the resource/implementation stage, not the production, delivery, or leverage stage. The September realization watchpoint records the current public-source non-observation: no first assembly, commissioning, completed quantity, acceptance, delivery, or measured local-content share was located in the checked primary-source set.

The new public-system buffer-and-credit bridge adds a financial-coping stage to the public-system row. SIPP shows that SNAP transitions can sit beside declining resources, stable job counts, and following hardship; Federal Reserve SHED shows that liquid capacity, revolving balances, linked balance growth, student-loan difficulty, and retirement actions are distinct coping layers. This is a cross-source mechanism map, not evidence that a public-system transition caused credit use or that financial pressure produced political action.

The follow-on route-and-buffer synthesis adds the WBNS lived-route and charitable-food layers. It places notice, recertification, interruption, cash/credit capacity, supplemental food, and unmet need in one staged public/private safety-net map without pooling state, respondent, person-month, and conditional-credit denominators. The result is stronger evidence for incomplete protection and route substitution, not a same-household causal chain or a political-trust finding.

The care-cost/time/work cross-source finding extends the care/health row by putting MEPS, SIPP, and SHED beside one another without merging their samples. The layers jointly support a bounded social- reproduction pattern: health and care costs can be transferred among system spending, out-of-pocket payment, unpaid time, work capacity, borrowing, and saving. The same-person dated event through household recovery, institutional response, trust, or politics remains an open arrow.

The capacity-to-leverage finding now adds the IMF April 2026 WEO defense-boom estimates. The macro layer shows that fiscal and social-spending trade-offs can appear between resource allocation and usable capacity, but its cross-country estimates and US aggregate projections do not establish a US household burden or realized geopolitical leverage. The defense resource-to-capability finding now aligns that macro stage with SIPRI spending estimates, a bounded USAspending JASSM/LRASM supplier extract, and the Poland-US procurement and co-production sequence. It makes contract, supplier, implementation, and realization stages more explicit while preserving the missing tests for production, delivery, domestic incidence, replaceability, and external response.

The strongest current composite proposition

The long-form reader version is Unequal optionality is the recurring structure behind pressure, adaptation, and power. It carries this proposition through the household, institutional, work, cultural, place, infrastructure, and geopolitical scales and records the counterexamples and tests required before it can become a causal claim.

Across the current evidence, a defensible provisional proposition is:

US social and political change is often organized by unequal optionality: households, workers, customers, public agencies, and states respond to pressure through different combinations of money, time, care, representation, institutional route, and ability to switch or refuse. The same visible outcome—continued consumption, benefit receipt, employment, care, voting, or infrastructure growth—can therefore coexist with unresolved burden or dependence.

This is a synthesis of recurring measurement patterns, not a causal estimate. The current registry contains 220 machine-readable records, 839 observations, and 103 source packets across 14 program themes. It does not claim that the 197 records describe the same people or that one mechanism explains all domains. The counterexamples are part of the proposition: financial improvement can coexist with worse health; SNAP exit can coexist with hardship; continued receipt can coexist with utility burden; assistance participation can coexist with very low food security; adults and children can experience different food-security states; care can occur across work and financial paths; a reported response is not a verified remedy; and infrastructure scale is not state leverage.

The AI/work and digital-habits lanes now deepen the synthesis in two ways. The AI capability conditions finding places NBER worker/task adoption beside BEA, BIS, IMF, World Bank, and OFR evidence. It shows why exposure, measured output, inequality models, public capacity, and infrastructure dependence must be kept as separate stages. The new AI capability/shared-control synthesis extends that comparison into an explicit control test: productivity, price, wealth, public-capacity, and country-capability signals do not identify who owns the gain, reviews the decision, can exit the system, or bears the risk. The source units and evidence statuses remain separate. The platform agency finding similarly separates attention, trust, civic action, complaint visibility, response, remedy, switching, and exit. Both strengthen the central optionality proposition while leaving the same-unit causal joins open.

The digital-life synthesis and digital consumer recourse synthesis now make the handoff explicit: platform reach, chatbot purpose, news attention, reported loss, recovery effort, complaint visibility, explanation, relief, and exit are different control surfaces. A socially central platform can still be replaceable for one user and essential for another; a visible complaint can improve institutional legibility without restoring money, access, or trust. The next test remains a lawful case-linked or diary-based episode design with contact, loss, effort, verified correction, switching, and later meaning/action in the same unit.

The AI synthetic-contact experiment adds a rare causal middle step to this otherwise mostly descriptive political surface. Across five preregistered studies of 3,960 US partisans, the tested outgroup-bot interaction increased immediate warmth and increased the choice of a real outgroup conversation in Study 4 (67% versus 61% in control). The one-week follow-up in Study 5, however, found a much smaller and statistically non-significant warmth difference. This supports a carefully bounded inference: a designed AI contact can lower the immediate barrier to cross-partisan contact, but the current evidence does not show durable attitude change, turnout, voting, or a population-level platform effect. It also leaves governance questions open: whether users know they are interacting with a bot, whether the bot represents the outgroup accurately, who controls the framing, and whether repeated contact produces durable behavior or merely short-lived experimental compliance.

The latest cross-source rotation adds four bounded tests to that proposition. The AI work-control finding places task-level adoption beside sector mobility, union representation, and pay without treating any one of those as worker control. The consumer recourse finding places household fraud loss and recovery effort beside CFPB complaint visibility and routing, while preserving the denominator gap between a household survey and an administrative complaint system. The immigration and local-demand finding uses county growth, foreign-born population, service-capacity screens, and a Chicagoland attitude survey to separate place pressure from political meaning. The local capacity and political-context findings add a same-county capacity/mobility comparison: trust, civic action, and reported voting do not form one gradient across the four cells, and the party-conditioned layer shows identity organizing the meaning of the same place context. This is a context join, not respondent exposure or causal political response; service use, wait, price, attribution, belonging, and institutional action remain open.

The new comparative worker-control finding sharpens the AI/work arrow. JRC worker survey evidence associates algorithmic management with autonomy, break, stress, and intensity differences in Europe; ILO cases show that collective voice sometimes changes consent, pay, data limits, human oversight, or workflow discretion, while other challenges remain partial or unresolved. Read alongside the US NBER/OECD/BLS layer, this supports a conversion hypothesis rather than a US causal claim: information and voice become control only when they alter a rule or produce enforceable remedy. The housing and insurance finding tests California ZIP-level income and wildfire-risk combinations and shows that the apparent low-income/high-risk gradient is sensitive to the income cutoff. Together these are not one causal chain; they are a current stress test showing that unequal room, institutional routing, and interpretation can be aligned only when units, denominators, and counterexamples remain visible.

The latest continuation pass extends the same discipline across several scales. The SNAP transition-reason finding separates job-loss and wage-reduction reports from other entry and exit reasons instead of treating every transition as the same hardship event. The SIPP race/tenure comparison and SIPP disability/resource comparison show why subgroup intersections need explicit universes and replicate-weight checks. The fraud recovery finding adds age, income, and payment-route conditioning while keeping complaint visibility separate from household loss. The party-conditioned worry/vote finding keeps political meaning distinct from material exposure. The payment-rewards finding keeps modeled incidence distinct from observed household welfare. Finally, the infrastructure-control finding keeps project completion and ownership change distinct from sovereignty or geopolitical leverage. These additions expand the atlas; they do not close its same-unit causal arrows.

The newest continuation pass adds four further bounded results. The FTC reporting-to-recovery finding places 2025 administrative reported-loss scale beside Federal Reserve household recovery burden and CFPB response routes; it does not create a same-case remedy or trust measure. The card-offer surface finding shows why a larger public credit-card offer surface and lower product-level median APR do not prove cheaper realized credit or practical access. The regional price-and-firm-capacity finding keeps Second District household price exposure, gasoline adjustment, and small- firm employment expectations as distinct place indicators. The 2026 JOLTS finding extends the labor context through July while preserving the establishment-versus-worker boundary and preliminary status of the latest month.

The 2026 CES earnings finding adds January–August establishment pay context for four sectors. It shows why partial-year pay movement and labor mobility must remain separate surfaces: CES averages do not identify worker control, and JOLTS rates do not identify individual outside options or household security.

The August 2026 Employment Situation record adds a current CPS/CES layer: steady unemployment and participation alongside positive payroll growth, nominal private-sector pay and hours, long-term unemployment, and uneven industry changes. The detailed finding shows why household labor-force status, establishment payroll jobs, JOLTS turnover, and sector earnings are conditioning surfaces rather than a common worker panel or an AI/work-control estimate.

The follow-up labor-to-household-room finding connects the BLS labor surface to SIPP same-person resource/job cross-lags, Federal Reserve liquidity and repayment measures, and New York Fed aggregate credit exposure. The combined evidence supports household room as a likely conditioning variable for practical worker exit and voice, but the sources do not share a person or workplace key and therefore do not establish that arrow.

The new firm AI expectations finding adds the employer-side clock from NBER W34836: broad current use, limited retrospective own-firm effects, and larger forward expectations for productivity, output, and employment. Its employer/employee expectation gap is useful for the institutional meaning layer, but it is not realized displacement, worker control, household security, or political consequence. The worker/workplace event ledger remains the required bridge from firm expectation to implementation, appropriation, voice, and household response.

The SIPP utility-to-work following-month finding adds a same-person timing surface: utility-payment difficulty or energy- assistance status at month t sits beside earnings and hours change at month t+1. Earnings movement is common across all groups, while the utility- difficulty hours estimate is imprecise and the assistance comparison is selected. This narrows the material-to-work arrow without turning a monthly utility screen into a dated bill shock, work-loss effect, or recovery result.

The BEA July 2026 income-and-outlays record adds a current aggregate benchmark for income, disposable income, consumption, services-versus-goods movement, outlays, and saving. The household-pressure finding uses it to anchor the macro environment without imputing equal household room, real purchasing power, consumer choice, or political meaning.

The BEA Q2 2026 growth/profits record adds a separate macro-firm surface for real GDP, private domestic demand, prices, and corporate profits. The work-control finding uses it to distinguish output and profit movement from worker authority, household benefit, and firm-level AI effects.

The IMF 2026 US Article IV finding adds country-surveillance context: recent aggregate resilience and productivity coexist with fiscal debt, current-account, tariff, labor, and financial-stability risks. IMF projections are conditional macro scenarios, not household or worker outcomes; the next bridge must connect any adjustment event to distributional, institutional, and political-response evidence.

The Federal Reserve financial-stability and monetary-policy finding adds the institutional-financial layer: resilient banks and moderate aggregate debt vulnerability coexist with selected household delinquency, tight credit for some borrowers, elevated inflation, high valuations, and funding-risk exposure. The Fed's system aggregates do not identify household incidence or political meaning; the next bridge must join a dated rate, energy, tariff, credit, or funding event to borrower type, firm size, repayment/spending response, and later institutional or political action.

The public financial-capacity and household-room bridge now places BEA aggregate output/profits/income, Federal Reserve financial and household surfaces, OFR public monitoring capacity, IMF fiscal scenarios, and New York Fed aggregate credit in one deliberately non-pooled architecture. It sharpens the end-to-end gap: macro resilience, public data visibility, aggregate debt/delinquency, and household liquidity can move differently; the missing test is still a dated event joined to compatible exposure, attribution, action, and recovery or remedy. The follow-up OFR institutional-finance finding turns that architecture into a longitudinal institutional sequence: 2022 repo and data-visibility pilots, 2023 elevated-risk monitoring, 2024 persistent data gaps and differentiated borrower risk, and 2025 expanded repo visibility and AI workflow reporting alongside lower staffing and budget. The sequence supports public-capacity analysis, not a claim that visibility became protection. The next empirical pass is to align those milestones with the IMF/Findex provider- demand layers and CFPB/SHED household-response layers, retaining market, institution, firm, account, household, and respondent units separately.

The financial-access route synthesis now supplies that provider/demand comparison in reader-facing form. Global Findex shows broad US adult account and device ownership and selected financial behaviors; IMF FAS shows provider-side counts, branch density, and balance ratios; SHED shows household loss, recovery time, buffers, and adaptation; and CFPB/FTC/OFR show institutional visibility, routing, and public-capacity surfaces. These layers establish reach and infrastructure as different stages, not a single inclusion score. They still do not identify favorable terms, failed-account episodes, verified correction, switching, or later trust for the same person.

The new county capacity/outcome diagnostic adds CDC PLACES modeled social-need outcomes to the existing CBP/HRSA/NHTS place architecture. Low visible health-establishment capacity aligns with higher modeled transportation, food, housing, utility, mental-distress, and uninsured medians, but routine-checkup medians are nearly unchanged. The result advances the place outcome surface while preserving the ecological and cross-vintage boundary: it does not identify patient access, provider quality, travel, price, causal effect, trust, or political response. The follow-up PLACES vintage comparison uses 2,885 common county FIPS across the 2024 and 2025 releases. Routine-care, insurance, food, transportation, housing, utility, and mental-distress medians do not move as one local-health trend, and the social-needs measure universe is not identical across releases. This strengthens the program's temporal quality control while preserving CDC's modeled small-area and non-intervention boundary. The HPSA component diagnostic then decomposes the shortage label itself. Geographic, population, facility, tract, low-income, and migrant/seasonal categories produce different overlapping county medians, which blocks a single “HPSA county” interpretation. This adds classification specificity but still does not observe component-level reach, patient use, quality, price, household time, trust, or political response. The follow-up HPSA operational-field audit finds that FTE, shortage, and estimated underserved-population fields are not uniformly populated by designation type; facility/site rows in particular have very sparse FTE and no estimated underserved-population values in the current extract. This is an institutional measurement boundary, not evidence of zero capacity or a resident-level care result.

The July 2026 Census HTOPS/HPS finding adds a current same-round household surface: expense and price perception, energy trade-offs, food and assistance routes, anxiety/loneliness, and trust in federal statistics and Congress. It strengthens alignment of material and institutional endpoints without turning a cross-sectional co-occurrence into a price-to-trust mechanism or political-action result.

The follow-up Census cross-wave finding uses the official successive-difference replicate weights across corrected March, May, and July 2026 releases. It shows broad expense difficulty nearly flat from May to July while food, energy, and trust measures move differently; the 2026 files are repeated snapshots after a documented design break, not a same-person recovery panel.

The follow-up July expense-to-confidence cross-tab tests one missing comparison within that same release. High confidence in federal statistical agencies was 43.9% among respondents reporting no usual- expense difficulty and 28.8% among those reporting at least a little difficulty; high confidence in Congress was 20.8% versus 13.8%. The replicate-weighted contrast is useful evidence that material pressure and institution-specific confidence can be related in the same population snapshot, but it still cannot identify timing, blame, prior identity, political action, or recovery. The larger statistical-agency gap also argues against collapsing all institutional confidence into a single trust score.

The April–June 2025 linked HTOPS finding adds a same-respondent transition surface for 6,564 linked IDs. Persistence, entry, and exit can now be observed for selected material, work, and institutional measures, but the retained sample lacks a documented attrition-adjusted longitudinal weight and does not identify a causal trigger. The linked cross-lagged audit shows baseline expense difficulty alongside materially higher later food, energy, and job-loss shares, while later Congress confidence is nearly similar across baseline-expense groups. This is an associative counterexample to a single material-pressure-to-trust pathway.

The World Bank Enterprise Surveys access recheck also strengthens the acquisition map: the US AI follow-up is confirmed as an available survey, but firm microdata remain behind the authenticated portal. That is an access result, not a firm adoption estimate; the next AI firm test still requires the file, questionnaire, weights, and variance documentation.

The latest rotation adds two bounded bridges. The household adaptation and recovery finding places SHED financial-condition persistence beside health, unpaid-care, food-pressure, and fraud-recovery measures. It strengthens the recurring optionalities proposition: current stabilization can consume savings, time, health, care capacity, or attention, and broad financial improvement is not the same as restored future room. The consumer visibility and exit finding then places CFPB complaint labels beside SHED fraud burden, Pew platform/AI use and privacy concern, and the FTC information-gathering inquiry. It keeps visibility, explanation, verified remedy, attention cost, dependence, trust, and exit as separate stages; neither complaint routing nor regulatory inquiry closes the consumer-power chain.

The recurring-source watch also refreshes the AI/work mobility context through January–July 2026. The partial-year JOLTS mean remains cool (4.3714% openings and 1.9571% quits, with July preliminary), but it is an establishment-rate context rather than an annual worker trend or an AI effect.

The current macro-to-household finding adds the latest BEA July income/outlays, August CPI and PPI, August CPS/CES, JOLTS partial-year, and SHED hardship vintages. It reinforces a central denominator rule: aggregate income and payroll growth can coexist with energy/upstream price pressure and income-graded household food or bill hardship. The release set identifies adjacent currencies, not a same-household price-to-politics or recovery chain.

The safety-net transition/work-room finding adds a same-person SIPP sequence around SNAP entry. Following resource-ratio, earnings, hours, job-count, and hardship surfaces do not move as one outcome: resource position often falls while job count and hours remain stable in their selected universes. This keeps benefit receipt, work continuity, household room, administrative adequacy, and recovery separate. The next test is a same-episode notice-to-remedy record with amount, gap days, alternatives, care/work response, and later recovery or action.

The US firm-capacity baseline adds the public World Bank Enterprise Survey profile as a distinct firm-level context layer: size strata differ in reported employment growth, formal training, outage exposure, bank-financed investment, and manager-selected obstacles. This improves the AI/work bridge by making implementation capacity and infrastructure exposure visible before the gated AI follow-up is used. It does not estimate AI adoption, productivity causality, worker control, or household incidence; the next test remains the documented firm file with its AI questions, weights, missingness, and variance guidance.

The firm-to-worker comparison then places that size-conditioned baseline beside NBER task adoption, BLS representation, and JOLTS mobility. The comparison makes four gates explicit: firm complements, actual worker use, institutional voice, and practical exit are not interchangeable. It strengthens the worker-control boundary without turning size, adoption, union coverage, or quits into a composite index.

The latest household-room pass adds a FRED timing screen. From January 2024 to July 2026, the University of Michigan consumer-sentiment index fell 23.8 points while the CPI index rose 7.5% and unemployment remained close to 4%. This does not identify an inflation effect: the series have different constructions and do not share a household denominator. It does establish a useful divergence that the household-level work must explain: a comparatively strong aggregate labor signal can coexist with weak public economic confidence.

The FRED finding therefore strengthens the distinction between national conditions and practical household room. SHED, SIPP, MEPS, BEA, BLS, and Federal Reserve credit records are needed to test which households faced shelter, food, energy, insurance, debt, care, or work constraints, and what they did next. The FRED series alone cannot supply that exposure, adaptation, political attribution, or recovery step.

The Gallup institutional-confidence finding adds a parallel meaning layer: average confidence in long-tracked institutions was low, but confidence differed sharply by institution, party, and racial group. The Chatham House data-trust memo extends the route to public measurement itself, treating confidence in economic statistics as a possible condition of domestic coordination and international credibility. These are not proof of a material-to-trust or domestic-to- geopolitical causal chain. They make the missing links more precise: measure the statistic a person encountered, their own material exposure, who they believed, whether an institution acted, and whether confidence changed after a verifiable correction or remedy.

The UNDP human-development finding adds a comparative capability frame to the AI and household-room lanes. The United States has a high average HDI and a strong international rank, but that average does not show who can exercise a choice, access training, refuse an automated decision, or absorb a work or health shock. UNDP therefore helps keep the program’s question focused on human capability rather than technology or income alone. It remains a country-level context measure; subgroup incidence, distribution, agency, and worker control still require US household, worker, firm, and institutional evidence.

The follow-up UNDP AI-and-human-development survey finding adds the missing perception layer for selected countries. In the US tabulation, weekly-or-more AI use (32.6%), confidence in controlling AI interactions (33.9%), confidence in government AI use (29.1%), and confidence that AI is designed in people’s interests (37.5%) are separate measures. Selected-country comparisons show why they should not be collapsed: more frequent use can coexist with lower personal control, and high public confidence does not verify service quality or actual agency. The survey is cross-sectional and internationally comparative; the next test remains a US subgroup and same-episode join to work, household, public-service, remedy, and political-action outcomes. The same survey’s domain layer adds another boundary: reported use was highest for entertainment, while likely future use was highest for entertainment and education, with work, health, and finance carrying different stakes. The next analysis must therefore follow the system into its domain of use rather than treating AI adoption as one cultural or economic event. Its work-expectation layer adds a further split: expected productivity, new job creation, and current job change or replacement can coexist, and expected productivity is much higher among people already likely to use AI for work tasks. This is an expectation bridge, not realized productivity, displacement, or bargaining power.

The newest bounded findings add four important controls to the cross-source story. The SHED household-room finding shows that income timing, outside help, assistance receipt, budget margin, and price adaptation are related but distinct household surfaces. They should not be summarized as one measure of financial stress or treated as proof of a single coping sequence. The RECS energy-room finding adds energy spending, energy burden, assistance, disconnection, and inability to repair or replace equipment as separate routes. Its burden result is a transparent spending-to-income proxy, not a complete welfare measure, and the public-use survey year limits claims about the present.

The BFS/BDS firm-and-place finding adds a firm-stage boundary: applications, establishments, openings, and net job creation are different events. A place can show entrepreneurial entry without a corresponding positive net-job result; sector composition is part of the explanation. This keeps local business formation from being used as a shortcut for local prosperity, worker bargaining power, or community recovery. Finally, the World Bank poverty, climate, and data-coverage finding adds comparative context rather than a US outcome: global poverty remains highly concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and fragile or conflict-affected settings; climate exposure and missing household surveys constrain what can be known. Those facts matter for the geopolitical context and for interpreting international capability, but they do not establish a US household, conflict, or geopolitical-behavior effect.

What is genuinely end-to-end today

The program currently has partial end-to-end coverage in bounded segments:

  1. Same-respondent persistence: SHED 2024→2025 financial-condition, adaptation, health, and selected care transitions.
  2. Time and care scale: ATUS published prevalence, care-day intensity, employment, age, and household/nonhousehold distinctions.
  3. Time-ordered public-system condition: SIPP SNAP transitions followed by rent/mortgage and utility hardship with Fay–BRR uncertainty, alongside USDA food-security severity and assistance participation. The buffer-and-credit bridge adds adjacent Federal Reserve coping measures, but does not claim a same-person route-to-credit or route-to-politics result.
  4. Pressure-to-adaptation comparison: BLS food-at-home prices, SHED income-conditioned food insufficiency and price responses, and USDA food security, with different reference periods and units kept explicit.
  5. Institutional visibility: CFPB published complaint route and endpoint trends, with product and time conditioning. The 2025 product comparison and household fraud layer now make the split between response labels, residual loss, recovery effort, and exit capacity explicit; a verified same-case remedy remains open.
  6. Political participation surface: CPS voting/registration and distinct nonvoter barriers, beside separate trust and judgment layers.
  7. Infrastructure/state-capacity boundary: national load scenarios and two international project milestones, with ownership and replaceability still open. IMF WEO adds a conditional macro-fiscal stage for defense mobilization; financing and social-spending incidence remain unlinked to US households or observed leverage.
  8. AI and platform agency boundary: task-level adoption, industry and firm outcomes, modeled distribution, public analytical capacity, attention, complaint visibility, and institutional scrutiny are now linked as bounded stages; worker/household incidence, remedy, exit, and geopolitical leverage remain unjoined.

The program does not yet have one valid record that carries a dated material or institutional trigger through adaptation, time/care, wellbeing, meaning, political action, institutional response, and later recovery. That is the central acquisition target—not a reason to erase the partial evidence or pretend that cross-source alignment is a respondent-level join.

Next rotating program cycle

The next cycle should advance all three scales together:

  • Micro/same-unit: run the PSID structural gate when authenticated files arrive; pursue SHED/ATUS/SIPP event-compatible comparisons where valid.
  • Institutional/episode: implement the same-case public-system and consumer recourse event ledger with notice, effort, remedy, and follow-up.
  • Place/state: retrieve utility, ownership, local-benefit, portability, and observed-response records for data-center and public-cloud cases.

Every new record should enter the trend-observation registry, carry its own denominator and boundary, and add either a new time period, subgroup/place conditioning, uncertainty, reversal, or same-unit link before a provisional trend is strengthened.
