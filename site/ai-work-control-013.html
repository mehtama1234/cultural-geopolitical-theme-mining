Finding 013: AI sovereignty is more than owning a model

Status: provisional
Last checked: 2026-09-11
Project: AI, work, and control

Finding

A country does not become capable merely by buying access to an advanced AI model. A more useful measure of sovereignty is whether people and institutions can adapt the system to local needs, inspect its decisions, switch providers, protect local data, develop skills, and enforce rules. Owning a frontier model may help, but it is only one layer of capability and is not a realistic path for most countries in the near term.

What we directly know

  • The World Bank reports that advanced models, chips, and data centers are concentrated in a small number of countries and firms, creating dependency risks. It distinguishes adopting existing tools, adapting them to local contexts, and advancing frontier systems. World Development Report 2026
  • The World Bank says imported systems may not fit local languages, institutions, norms, or development needs, and that countries need reliable electricity, internet access, skills, data, capital, and strong institutions to gain from AI. World Development Report 2026
  • The report recommends that national systems work with multiple platforms and providers and describes low-cost “small AI” delivered through voice calls, text messages, basic phones, and other limited-connectivity channels as a possible route to wider access. World Development Report 2026
  • The IEA shows that AI capability also rests on physical systems such as electricity generation, grids, data centers, and critical-mineral supply chains. IEA Energy and AI
  • The World Bank's AI-readiness work describes four practical foundations: connectivity and reliable power, compute and secure cloud, context and quality data, and competency and skills. World Bank: Building Data Infrastructure for AI Readiness

These sources support a capability framework. They do not establish that a particular country is sovereign or dependent.

Causal chain under test

provider or model access
  -> local adaptation, data, skills, infrastructure, interoperability
  -> usable system in a real institution
  -> worker, household, public-service, or firm outcome
  -> local learning and bargaining power
  -> ability to switch, regulate, or refuse external providers

The final two arrows are the most important and least measured.

A practical sovereignty test

Capability Test question What failure looks like
Adaptation Can the system work in local language, law, norms, and work practice? Imported advice is technically available but socially or legally wrong.
Interoperability Can institutions use more than one provider or move their data? A pilot becomes a lock-in.
Inspection Can workers, agencies, and regulators see enough to test errors and bias? The system is treated as a sealed authority.
Local learning Do local workers and firms gain skills, data, and reusable tools? Use grows while knowledge and value leave the country.
Physical capacity Can reliable power, connectivity, compute, and maintenance be secured? Model access fails during outages, congestion, or price shocks.
Public capacity Can governments procure, evaluate, regulate, and remedy harm? Adoption proceeds faster than oversight.
Exit Can a user switch without losing records, service, or income? Formal choice exists but practical dependence remains.

Cultural and social meaning

The political story of national AI competition can hide a more ordinary question: can a teacher, nurse, farmer, public worker, or small business use a system that understands the setting and can be corrected? Local adaptation is not cosmetic. It affects whether people see the system as useful, alien, unfair, or imposed.

This is an inference from the source set, not a measured public-opinion result.

Geopolitical meaning

The relevant power relation may be a chain of replaceability:

model provider -> cloud and chip provider -> data and energy system
-> public or private buyer -> worker and citizen

The less replaceable each link is, the more a technical relationship may become a bargaining relationship. But concentration alone does not prove coercion. The project must measure contracts, switching cost, local alternatives, public procurement, and actual interruptions or disputes.

Strongest challenge

Interoperability and local adaptation may cost more than a single imported system and may reduce short-run performance. Provider concentration can also create economies of scale and lower prices. A country may rationally accept dependence for a benefit. Sovereignty must therefore be measured by outcomes and options, not by a slogan about self-sufficiency.

What would change our mind

  • Countries with no local adaptation, switching ability, or inspection capacity achieve equal durable outcomes.
  • Frontier-model ownership predicts public and worker outcomes better than local complements and exit options.
  • Provider concentration does not change costs, terms, service, or policy choices when systems become critical.
  • Imported systems consistently build local skills, data assets, and institutional capacity without deliberate local action.

Next tests

  1. Build country profiles using the seven capability fields without collapsing them into one score.
  2. Compare the same public-service or workplace function across single-provider and multi-provider settings.
  3. Find cases where local language, data, or procurement changed system performance.
  4. Track data portability, switching costs, service interruptions, and contract renewal terms.
  5. Add a country that uses open or locally adapted systems as counterevidence to concentration.
  6. Link capability measures to worker, household, public-service, and firm outcomes.
