When spending hides the cash constraint

A spending number can stay steady while the household underneath it becomes less safe. Lower spending may be a choice, a limit, or both.

The argument

One person spends less because cash is tight. Another spends more because waiting would mean missing care, losing a job, or paying more later. The transaction total cannot tell us which life sits behind it.

Check cash, credit, need, and later cost before judging spending.

What the source establishes

Using transaction records linked to survey expectations for middle-income US consumers with little liquid wealth, NBER finds large and varied gaps from a no-friction benchmark. The median absolute gap is 40% of the benchmark: 49% spend below it and 51% above it.

Borrowing limits alone cannot explain both directions. The benchmark is a model measure, not a bill or welfare score.

NBER Working Paper 34891

Follow the purchase

income and liquid cash → ability to wait, borrow, or change a plan → observed spending → food, care, housing, or debt choice → later security

Lower spending may protect rent while sacrificing care. Higher spending may prevent a larger loss. Without the need and cash around the purchase, the number cannot tell us which.

Keep these apart

  • Observed spending and welfare.
  • Preference and cash constraint.
  • Income and liquid wealth.
  • Immediate savings and later cost.

Next test

Follow consumers for one year and join transactions to item, need, price, cash, credit, health, work, housing, food, debt, and later security.

Reading rule

Do not label lower spending a preference until cash, credit, need, and later cost are visible.
