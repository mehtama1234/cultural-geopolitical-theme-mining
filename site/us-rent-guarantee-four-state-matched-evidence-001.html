When four states make rent guarantee a different deal

A national website can make rent guarantee look uniform while state law changes what may be sold, disclosed, claimed, and recovered.

The argument

“No deposit” does not mean “no exposure after default.” The first question is what obligation replaced the deposit and whether the renter can understand it before signing.

Legal permission, product availability, price, claim, and household result are separate facts.

Follow the money after a missed payment

California exposes bond terms, New York regulatory history, Texas a fee-in-place-of-deposit rule, and Illinois a licensing gate. Each record answers a different question. None alone proves a renter's long-term result.

state rule and filed form → provider offer and renter fee → landlord accepts lower upfront cash → default → provider pays landlord → provider seeks recovery → debt, dispute, renewal, credit, or housing result

The deeper finding

Combining four states into “available in the state” hides the part that matters after a claim. A lower move-in cost may help someone get housed while a repayment demand later makes the next move harder.

What remains unproven

The states do not yet show four renter outcomes. We lack a common record of fee, covered amount, claim, dispute, repayment, renewal, credit, move-out, and later housing.

Next test

Collect provider, carrier, filed form, fee, covered amount, disclosures, claim trigger, notice, repayment, dispute, collection, renewal, credit, and later housing in all four states. Compare a guarantee with a deposit, guarantor, direct aid, and public insurance.

Reading rule

Do not call a state covered because a provider has a national website. Find the actual state rule, form, quote, and renter duty after payment.
