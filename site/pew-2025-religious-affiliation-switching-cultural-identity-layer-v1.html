Pew 2025 religious affiliation, switching, and cultural identity layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: Pew Research Center, 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study Reference window: nationally representative U.S. adult survey conducted July 17, 2023–March 4, 2024; comparisons with 2007 and 2014 studies Status: religion, meaning, identity, and social-change finding; not a forecast of congregational decline, political realignment, or personal belief causation

Why this update matters

Religious change is often summarized as a rise of the religiously unaffiliated or a decline of Christianity. Pew's Religious Landscape Study supports that long-run description but adds a more useful mechanism: affiliation, switching, spirituality, practice, cultural/familial connection, and political identity are related but not identical.

This is a missing cultural layer for the atlas. Institutions such as families, congregations, schools, media, charities, and political coalitions may draw on religious identities even when formal affiliation changes. A person can leave a religious label while retaining cultural attachment, family practice, moral language, or community ties. Conversely, a formal affiliation does not imply frequent practice or one political position.

Recorded findings

Surface Result Boundary
Survey frame Pew’s 2023–24 RLS included 36,908 U.S. adults in a nationally representative English- and Spanish-language survey It is cross-sectional; retrospective childhood identity is subject to recall and does not identify causal timing
Adult affiliation 62% identified as Christian: 40% Protestant, 19% Catholic, and 3% other Christian; 29% were religiously unaffiliated Affiliation is a self-description, not attendance, belief intensity, cultural connection, or political identity
Long-run change Christian share declined from 78% in 2007 to 62% in 2023–24; unaffiliated rose from 16% to 29% The newer surveys suggest the short-term change has slowed or leveled off; long-run and short-run clocks differ
Religious switching 35% of adults said their current religious identity differs from the one in which they were raised “Switching” includes movement among Christian traditions and between religion/no religion; it is not necessarily conversion or loss of all practice
Direction of flow 20% of adults were unaffiliated after being raised in a religion, while only about 3% identified with a non-Christian religion after being raised Christian or with no religion; Christianity loses more through switching than it gains Net flows are aggregate retrospective reports, not individual event histories or reasons for switching
Switching ratio There were roughly six former Christians for every convert to Christianity; Catholic losses were especially lopsided Ratios summarize flows under Pew’s categories and do not predict future retention or congregational membership
Generational structure Among people born in the 1980s, unaffiliated identification grew from 26% in 2007 to 37% in 2023–24; older adults were more likely to have been raised and remain Christian Cohort comparisons mix age, period, and survey-design effects; they do not prove generational causation
Short-term stability Across roughly the last half-dozen Pew surveys since 2020, Christian share was 60%–64% and unaffiliated 28%–31% A plateau is provisional and does not reverse the longer decline from 2007
Spirituality 53% described themselves as both spiritual and religious; 21% spiritual but not religious; 5% religious but not spiritual; 21% neither Spiritual/religious self-description is not a measure of doctrine, practice, community, or moral behavior
Practice 40% attended religious services at least monthly in person or online/TV; 16% participated both in person and online, 17% only in person, and 8% only online/TV The 2014 survey used a different attendance question, limiting direct comparison
Cultural connection Pew separately asked whether respondents identify with Catholic, Jewish, Buddhist, Muslim, or Hindu traditions for cultural, ethnic, or family reasons Cultural identification is not formal religious affiliation or active participation
Religious diversity Adults identifying with religions other than Christianity rose from 4.7% in 2007 to 7.1% in 2023–24; immigrants were more likely than U.S.-born adults to identify with a non-Christian religion, 14% versus 6% Small-group estimates and immigrant comparisons require subgroup uncertainty and place context
Political alignment Religious profiles are closely aligned with partisan identities in aggregate, but Black Americans are a notable exception: at all levels of religiousness, most identify as Democrats or lean Democratic Association between religion and party is not a causal political pathway or a unified religious bloc

The central mechanism

family upbringing, migration, community, institution, or personal experience
  -> affiliation, switching, cultural attachment, spirituality, and practice
  -> belonging, moral language, social support, institution choice, and political meaning
  -> coalition, service, family, charitable, or civic behavior
  -> reinforcement, disaffiliation, intermarriage, generational replacement, or re-entry

Religious change is a pathway system rather than a one-way secularization switch. A person can move from Christian affiliation to “nothing in particular” while retaining a cultural identity, family rituals, or spiritual belief. A religious institution can lose formal members while remaining influential in local care, education, charity, or political mobilization. A growing unaffiliated population can contain atheists, agnostics, spiritual people, and people with low institutional attachment who do not share one worldview.

Generational replacement is one mechanism Pew identifies, but the data do not resolve all period, cohort, family, migration, and institutional effects. The “stickiness” of religious and nonreligious upbringing matters: religious identity is not simply transmitted from parent to child at a fixed rate. Mixed marriages and household practice can also create intermediate forms of belonging, especially for children.

The political mechanism is equally nonbinary. Religiousness, affiliation, race, age, immigrant generation, geography, and party intersect. A highly religious Black American and a highly religious White American may share practice intensity but differ politically; a culturally Jewish, Catholic, Muslim, Hindu, or Buddhist person may carry identity meaning without using it as a current religious label. The atlas should therefore measure religion as identity, practice, community, and political interpretation separately.

What this changes in the broader trend map

This layer adds cultural meaning, family transmission, institutional belonging, and political identity to the societal trend map. It connects demographic change and migration to community institutions, social support, public norms, charity, education, and coalition formation without treating religion as a single political cause.

It complements the electorate-composition and public-life-engagement layers. The electorate layer shows who is structurally eligible to vote; the engagement layer shows how people participate in public life; this layer shows one set of identity and community pathways that may shape meaning and participation. None should be collapsed into a religious or political prediction.

What remains unproven

Pew does not establish why individuals switched, whether a change in affiliation changed political views, whether religious institutions lost members or merely changed participation modes, or whether cultural attachment preserves charitable or civic behavior. It does not identify causal effects on family formation, fertility, education, trust, voting, social cohesion, or consumer behavior.

The RLS is cross-sectional and fielded over a long period. The 2007, 2014, and 2023–24 studies had different modes and designs. Retrospective childhood identity, current labels, spirituality, attendance, and cultural connection are different measurements. Aggregate switching flows do not track the same person through institutions, family events, or political action.

Next test

Build a cohort-household-community ledger linking:

  • childhood affiliation, family practice, education, migration, marriage, and current affiliation or non-affiliation;
  • spirituality, belief, attendance mode, congregational/community connection, cultural identity, and social support;
  • race/ethnicity, age, generation, geography, income, education, disability, and political identity;
  • charitable, civic, family, school, service, media, and voting behavior; and
  • institutional entry/exit, identity change, trust, belonging, and later political or community action.

Use repeated or panel-compatible measures where possible, and compare formal disaffiliation with continued cultural/community practice. The strong result is not a larger “nones” percentage or a claim that religion is disappearing; it is a traceable account of how identity, practice, family transmission, and institutions change—and what social or political functions move with them.
