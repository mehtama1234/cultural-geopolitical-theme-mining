US transport reading · 12 September 2026

Transport is not one problem.

A ride can be a convenience, a work requirement, a care duty, or a last chance to earn money. The research looks different depending on which one it is.

A short paper scan for the household calendar project. Read the Markdown record.

The useful question is not “does transport improve access?”

Ask: access for whom, to what, at what price, with what time cost, and with what alternative when the service fails?

One word hides three different stories

Household

Can I get there?

Vehicle access, fare, wait, work schedule, care duty, safety, and what happens after a missed trip.

City

Who carries the side cost?

Traffic, delay, fuel use, road danger, pollution, and public space can change even when one rider gains an option.

Work and firms

What fallback is available?

A platform can open a way to earn after job loss while also changing older jobs, costs, and bargaining power.

The deeper connection

Repeated need can become a source of control.

A platform may learn that a person travels at the same time every day. That pattern may mean work, school, or care—not a freely chosen purchase. Better timing data can improve the service. It can also tell the firm who has fewer ways to leave.

The household calendar should therefore record the real alternative, not only the selected ride: another route, another provider, a family helper, a later shift, or no workable option.

Convenience can move a burden rather than remove it.

A rider may save time while a city absorbs more delay or danger. A worker may gain quick income while paying for fuel, repairs, and unstable hours. The benefit and the cost can land on different people.

What the data already shows

Our weighted 2022 NHTS comparison found that 9.73% of urban households had no vehicle, compared with 2.82% of rural households. Among people with a valid answer, 20.05% of urban respondents reported rideshare use in the previous 30 days, compared with 5.69% of rural respondents.

This is not proof of rideshare dependence. NHTS gives us vehicle access, travel purpose, mode, and some time measures. It does not give us the exact fare, whether the trip was the only workable choice, whether work was missed, or what happened to the budget afterward. See the full comparison.

The next test

For one important trip, record five things:

1 · Need

Why was it needed?

Work, care, food, health, school, or something optional.

2 · Alternatives

What could actually work?

Include time, price, safety, reliability, and notice.

3 · Control

Who could fix it?

Household, employer, provider, agency, or another person.

4 · Transfer

Who carried the cost?

Cash, debt, unpaid help, missed work, or public space.

5 · Recovery

What happened next?

Was the lost room restored, or did the burden move to the next month?

The working finding

Transport dependence is not one condition. It can mean a fixed work or care duty, a lack of vehicle access, a platform that knows a repeated need, or a worker using the platform as a fallback. Those forms create different kinds of risk.

The next step is a dated household record, not a larger pile of broad averages.
