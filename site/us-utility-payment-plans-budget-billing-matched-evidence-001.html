A short argument · US energy, payment plans, and cash

A smoother bill can move the problem rather than end it.

Budget billing changes when a household pays, not necessarily how much it owes.

The argument

A predictable payment can be real relief. But if actual use is higher than the estimate, the account may carry a balance into a later true-up.

Smoothing changes timing first. It may not change the burden.

Follow the account, not just the payment

A family enrolls after a hard winter. The monthly number falls and the family stops cutting other expenses. Months later, use rises again and the utility adjusts for the gap between estimated and actual cost. The household now faces both the monthly payment and a balance it may not have seen coming.

budget plan → smoother payment → actual use differs → true-up balance → stable service or another cash crisis

Another household may benefit greatly. The difference is the fit between the plan, the home, the weather, and the household's pay calendar.

The argument has limits

The sources establish the mechanics. They do not show how many customers reach a true-up, how large balances are, or whether budget billing lowers shutoffs. A plan can be good cash-flow design without being good debt design.

What this changes

Every plan should show the monthly payment, actual use, running balance, and likely adjustment together. It should also explain what happens if the customer leaves, misses a payment, or cannot pay the true-up.

Next test

Track enrollment, use, payments, balance, true-up, notices, arrears, and service status at 30, 90, and 180 days.

Reading rule

Do not call a payment plan affordable because the monthly number is smaller. Follow the balance, the true-up, and the next bill.
