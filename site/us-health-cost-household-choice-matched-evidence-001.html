A medical cost can change care, work, and debt in different ways

The argument

“Medical debt” sounds like one problem. It is several problems that can arrive in a different order.

The need for care comes first. Then comes the price, the insurance decision, the payment, and the question of what the household can no longer afford. A person may delay care before any bill arrives. A hospital visit may create a bill and make work harder at the same time. An unpaid balance may later become a collection record. Removing that record can help a credit decision without treating the illness or replacing the lost income.

These are not small distinctions. A policy can improve one layer and leave the others untouched. A family can have less medical debt because it received coverage, because it avoided care, or because a record was removed. The number alone does not tell us which happened.

The timing of the bill can be as important as its size. A deductible may be manageable over a year but impossible when treatment begins. A prescription may be affordable once and unaffordable every month. A bill can arrive after the worker has already lost hours, so the household faces lower income and higher cost together. A person may delay care not because the total cost is known, but because the first payment is not possible today.

Follow the shock through the household

Imagine a worker with a serious health problem and a high deductible. The first cost may be the appointment or prescription. The second may be lost hours or a missed shift. The third may be borrowing, a late rent payment, or a bill sent to collections.

The household is now making several decisions at once: seek care, keep working, pay the provider, protect housing, or preserve cash. Employer health coverage may make the worker stay in a job that no longer fits. Medicaid may reduce collections and out-of-pocket cost. Debt relief may clean up the credit record. None of those outcomes automatically tells us whether the person became healthier or had better care.

The household may also provide the missing care itself. A partner takes time off, an adult child drives to appointments, or a relative pays a bill. The medical record may show no formal service and the credit file may show no new debt, while family income and rest have fallen.

Coverage has a usability test. A plan can exist while the doctor is out of network, prior approval is delayed, the deductible is too high, or the clinic is too far from work. A person may be insured and still postpone care or use an emergency room because the ordinary path is hard to reach. Enrollment is an input; completed, timely, affordable care is the household result.

The work decision can run in both directions. A worker may stay in a job to keep coverage, leave because treatment makes the job impossible, reduce hours, or use unpaid leave. A family member may change jobs to provide care. The same diagnosis can produce different earnings and debt depending on schedule flexibility and who else can help.

What the sources actually establish

Step Evidence Meaning
Care choice The Federal Reserve reports that 26% of adults skipped some care because of cost in 2025. Price can change care before debt is visible.
Health shock NBER research links hospital admissions with unpaid bills, earnings, credit, and borrowing. Illness can create both a money and work shock.
Coverage NBER Medicaid research finds lower medical collections and lower out-of-pocket costs. Coverage can protect the financial side of care.
Debt record NBER experiments find medical-debt relief can improve some financial measures without improving average health or care use. Credit relief is not the same as treatment.
Work NBER research links employer coverage with job decisions after health shocks. Insurance can affect the freedom to leave a job.

Sources: Federal Reserve household survey, NBER hospital-admission study, NBER Medicaid and financial health study, NBER medical-debt relief experiments, NBER medical-debt reporting study, and NBER employer coverage and work study.

There is a fourth layer: the ability to avoid the bill in the first place. A person may not receive care, buy a medicine, or return for a follow-up. That can make the balance look better while increasing later risk. A low medical-spending household is not necessarily a healthy or financially secure household.

The political question is who carries uncertainty. The patient may not know the price before care, the employer may tie coverage to staying, the insurer may decide what is covered, and the provider may bill several organizations. When the household must absorb the uncertainty, a medical event becomes a test of cash, work, family support, and trust at the same time. Public policy has to decide whether to lower the price, make the price knowable, replace lost income, or protect the family from the record afterward.

The deeper finding is that health cost is a household systems problem. Care, coverage, work, debt, transport, and family time are linked. A successful intervention should state which link it repairs and whether the remaining links make that repair usable.

The important split

There are at least three things people call medical debt: the bill, the payment problem, and the record that follows the person. They need different measures.

Coverage may reduce the bill and make care easier to use. A payment plan may keep the account out of collections while consuming money needed for rent. Record deletion may improve access to credit while leaving the original illness and price unchanged. Even “more care” is not automatically a bad result; it may mean someone finally received needed treatment.

The honest question is therefore: which layer changed, for whom, and at what cost?

What remains unproven

The studies support separate links in the path, not one complete story for the same household. They do not yet show whether a person who delayed care later became sicker, whether coverage protected work across a job change, or whether debt relief improved long-run household security.

They also do not show how treatment quality, provider distance, network restrictions, prior authorization, caregiving, or unpaid leave changed the result. A claim paid by insurance can coexist with delayed care, lost work, or family debt.

The next test is a linked record of care need, price, coverage, payment, work, debt, and later health. It should also count rent, food, saving, family help, and caregiving.

Follow patients and their households from the first symptom or care need for two years. Record quoted and final price, deductible, network, approval delay, travel, treatment timing, prescriptions, paid and unpaid caregiving, hours worked, leave, income, rent, food, transport, debt, collections, and later health. Compare lower upfront cost, portable coverage, price navigation, income replacement, and debt relief by the first point of failure. Measure timely usable care, health, work, household cash, and the next medical shock separately.

Reading rule

Do not say “medical debt relief worked” until you name whether it changed the bill, credit record, care use, health, work, or household security.
