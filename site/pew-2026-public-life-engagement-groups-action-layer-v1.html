Pew 2026 public-life engagement groups and action layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17
Source: Pew Research Center Pew-Knight Initiative
Field dates: Cross-Sectional Engagement Survey, July 9–December 5, 2025; American Trends Panel, September 8–14, 2025
Status: current published action-structure layer; no causal participation claim

Why this update matters

The atlas has measured voting, contact, volunteering, news exposure, trust, and political barriers in separate routes. This Pew study is useful because it classifies 19 forms of participation together: politics, civic and community involvement, religious attendance, and news attention. It produces four participation styles rather than one ladder:

  • Mobilizers: active across politics, civics, news, and religion.
  • Connectors: broadly involved, but less politically active.
  • Spectators: follow news but participate less directly elsewhere.
  • Outsiders: less involved across most measured activities.

The groups are analytical clusters, not social identities or causal treatments.

Recorded findings

Surface Mobilizers Connectors Spectators Outsiders
Extremely/very interested in politics 58% 43% 28% 9%
Trust federal government always/most of the time 22% 20% 19% 17%
Some/a lot of trust in national news 62% 64% 57% 45%
Some/a lot of trust in local news 77% 78% 72% 58%
Some/a lot of trust in social media 51% 32% 48% 28%
Say news organizations favor one side 77% 82% 75% 72%

The pattern is more interesting than a simple engaged/trusting continuum. Mobilizers show the highest political interest, but federal-government trust is low across all groups. Connectors report the highest national and local news trust in this table but are not the most politically interested group. Outsiders have lower news trust, especially for national and social-media information, but their low engagement does not automatically reveal why they are outside or whether they are politically indifferent.

What this adds to the broad goal

This layer helps close one part of the route from institutional meaning to action by distinguishing participation currencies:

  1. attention to news;
  2. political interest;
  3. direct political activity;
  4. community and religious participation;
  5. trust in information sources;
  6. trust in government; and
  7. normative judgments about fairness.

They are correlated in some combinations but not interchangeable. The study also shows why low institutional trust should not be equated with withdrawal: the most politically active group still reports low federal-government trust, while the least engaged group reports lower news trust but not a directly observed political motive.

Open arrows and next test

The next stronger design would follow a respondent through a dated information or institutional event and observe source exposure, interpretation, trust, political contact or organizing, voting, switching/non-use, and later response. The current cluster design is a strong descriptive map of action styles but does not establish temporal ordering or a cause of membership.

The study also warns against overgeneralizing the political activity measures: many questions refer to the prior 12 months, which for most respondents included the 2024 presidential election. Engagement may look different at other points in the political cycle.

Boundaries

Cluster assignments depend on the 19 selected measures, survey design, and field period. The American Trends Panel is used to examine attitudes after assignment to the cross-sectional cluster solution; this is not a joined longitudinal respondent history. The record does not measure objective news fairness, persuasion, causal trust formation, turnout effects, or the material conditions that make participation possible.
