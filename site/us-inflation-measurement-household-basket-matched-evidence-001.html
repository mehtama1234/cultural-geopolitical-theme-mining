When the inflation average is not the household basket

An official inflation number can be accurate and still fail to describe a particular family's month. A renter and a homeowner do not buy the same economy.

The argument

A national average is useful for scale. It is not a household account. The family may buy less, borrow, change quality, or delay care—choices the average cannot show.

Combining national averages does not create a record for one person.

Follow one family through a year

A low-income renter sees rent and food rise while wages change by fewer hours each week. A higher-income homeowner spends more on other goods and sees a different average. The first family switches brands and delays a repair; the second postpones a purchase. Both sit inside one national inflation story.

national price index + average wage + average spending → broad pressure signal → household basket still unknown → substitution, debt, saving, or reduced quality → possible change in trust and political judgment

The deeper finding

The index tells institutions how the average price moved; the basket tells a family which choice disappeared. A policy can be judged by the average while its burden falls on a narrow group.

What remains unproven

The records do not show the same household's product purchases, rent, debt, hours, benefits, savings, and economic view together. They cannot tell whether a spending change was willing substitution, quality loss, or unmet need.

Next test

Use Consumer Expenditure Survey microdata to build sample baskets by income and tenure. Compare each with CPI categories, wages, rent, and savings. Label every result as an average, subgroup estimate, or same-household measure.

Reading rule

Do not turn three national averages into one family story.
