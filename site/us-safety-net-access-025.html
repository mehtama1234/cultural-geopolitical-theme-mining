Finding 025: People can experience violence without it becoming a police record

Status: bounded public-safety and institutional-visibility finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The finding in simple words

BJS's 2024 National Crime Victimization Survey estimated 23.3 violent victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older. The rate reported to police was 11.2 per 1,000. Put another way, about 48% of violent victimizations were reported to police, compared with 30% of property victimizations.

The practical route is:

experienced harm
  -> report, nonreport, or third-party notice
  -> police-visible record or private experience
  -> response, remedy, safety, trust, and later action [open]

What this adds to the broader atlas

This adds a public-safety version of a recurring atlas pattern: a system can count what reaches it without counting everything people experience. The official record is therefore not the same as the full social condition. A person's decision not to report may reflect fear, privacy, doubt that police could help, or a belief that the incident was personal or too minor. Those are different meanings, and the survey does not turn them into one trust score.

It also separates visibility from remedy. A reported incident is visible to police, but the report does not show whether police responded well, whether the victim received services, whether safety improved, or whether the person later trusted or withdrew from public institutions.

Limits and counterexamples

  • The estimates come from the 2024 legacy NCVS instrument during a split-sample transition to a redesigned instrument; BJS warns that precision is lower in the transition year.
  • The survey measures nonfatal victimization and reporting status, not every crime, all police records, or the quality of a response.
  • Nonreporting is not proof of distrust, and reporting is not proof of safety, justice, or confidence.
  • The source is not a same-person follow-up from victimization to response, recovery, trust, or political action.

Next decisive test

Use a privacy-protected repeated-person or case-linked record that preserves victimization, reporting choice, reason, police contact, response, victim services, safety, remedy, and later trust or action. Keep survey estimates and police-record counts as complementary evidence rather than forcing them into a single rate.
