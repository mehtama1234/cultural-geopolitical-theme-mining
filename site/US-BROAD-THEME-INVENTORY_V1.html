US broad theme inventory v1

Purpose: preserve the actual end-to-end objective after a session crash or a change of working topic.

The big end-to-end objective

Build a living, evidence-grounded trend atlas that discovers and tests recurring cultural, societal, political, consumer, institutional, financial, firm, infrastructure, and geopolitical themes in the United States.

Use many source families to determine what is changing, who is exposed or protected, how people and organizations adapt, what meanings and conflicts recur, how power and risk move between actors, and which patterns survive comparison across populations, places, sectors, and time. The objective is to turn scattered data into a defensible map of themes and trends—not to reduce the program to one dataset or one household narrative.

For every important pattern, preserve the source, unit, time period, exposed and comparison groups, counterevidence, uncertainty, and exact link that remains unmeasured. Household records are one instrument for testing selected links; they do not replace the broader population- and system-level map.

The source universe can include surveys, administrative data, public records, research papers, company filings, product and platform evidence, media and cultural measures, election and opinion data, and international or supply-chain records when they explain a US exposure. The theme coverage matrix records the current evidence anchor and missing link for every theme.

The recurring themes to extract

  1. Household room and consumption: prices, fees, subscriptions, credit, debt, savings, food, health care, housing, insurance, utilities, repairs, and what people delay, substitute, borrow for, or give up.
  2. Time as a hidden price: commuting, caregiving, waiting, paperwork, customer-service loops, multiple jobs, unpaid work, and who gets time back from convenience or automation.
  3. Consumer power and recourse: whether people can understand a deal, reach a human, appeal, correct a record, obtain a remedy, switch providers, leave a platform, or refuse data collection.
  4. Platforms, data, and attention: ranking, reviews, identity checks, personalization, privacy, surveillance, automation, digital access, and how platform rules shape choice and dependence.
  5. Work, control, and bargaining: wages, schedules, monitoring, AI, productivity, benefits, job lock, career movement, worker voice, multiple jobs, employer dependence, and who receives the gain or carries the risk.
  6. Care, health, and social reproduction: medical access and bills, delayed care, disability, mental health, caregiving, paid and unpaid support, family time, transport, and the work required to keep people functioning.
  7. Housing, place, and mobility: rent, mortgages, ownership, repairs, insurance, energy, water, transport, climate and disaster exposure, neighborhood change, displacement, staying, and moving.
  8. Unequal exposure and status: race, class, age, gender, disability, family structure, migration, geography, ownership, and institutional access as differences in risk, treatment, voice, and recovery.
  9. Trust, identity, and cultural meaning: whether lived material conditions become dignity or humiliation, belonging or exclusion, institutional trust or distrust, blame, identity, community practice, or changing norms of fairness.
  10. Public systems and policy feedback: benefits, eligibility, cliffs, enforcement, courts, regulation, public investment, take-up, administrative burden, and how public action changes behavior and later demand.
  11. Political judgment and collective action: personal versus national economic perceptions, parties, elections, turnout, protest, organizing, lobbying, media narratives, public demands, and whether private pressure becomes political action.
  12. Firm, sector, and market power: consolidation, pricing, risk transfer, labor strategy, investment, profitability, bankruptcy, ownership, competition, and the gap between company success and customer, worker, or community security.
  13. Infrastructure, technology, and dependency: energy, water, logistics, communications, AI capacity, supply chains, critical inputs, foreign dependence, resilience, and who controls the systems on which US life and firms depend.
  14. Geopolitical and state consequences: how domestic affordability, industrial capacity, technology, energy, finance, migration, public legitimacy, and corporate exposure affect US leverage, alliances, and strategic room to act.

These are themes to investigate, not claims that every pressure is rising together or affects the same people.

The end-to-end chain

condition, price, rule, shock, technology, or institutional decision
  -> money, time, access, control, status, health, or security changes
  -> consumers, households, workers, firms, communities, or voters adapt,
     delay, borrow, switch, stay, organize, or go without
  -> firm, employer, lender, platform, agency, court, media, party, or
     foreign actor responds
  -> costs, risks, data, assets, and decision power move between actors
  -> cultural meaning, trust, identity, public demand, political action,
     company/sector exposure, or geopolitical leverage changes
  -> finding with direct evidence, counterevidence, limits, and next test

Every link must be labeled as observed, reported, estimated, inferred, compared, or open. A household action is not automatically a cultural explanation; sentiment is not automatically political action; and a company or national outcome is not automatically household security.

What we are pursuing now

The arrow-by-arrow source assignment is maintained in the US broad evidence matrix. It is the operational companion to this theme inventory.

The active program starts with five cross-domain bridges, each requiring population-, market-, firm-, place-, institution-, and political evidence where available. These are priority routes through the 14-theme program, not a replacement for the full societal scope:

  1. Price/payment -> household room -> substitution and public judgment.
  2. Service/platform rule -> recourse and customer effort -> trust and exit.
  3. Work rule/tool -> control, security, and bargaining -> household and local power.
  4. Housing/energy/insurance -> health, mobility, and place -> staying, moving, or recovery.
  5. Public aid/rule -> exposure and take-up -> material result, interpretation, and political response.

The next work is to attach valid data to these bridges, preserve source and method limits, apply the trend-extraction protocol, look for the reversals in the counterexample register, and publish only the strongest supported findings in the reader. The first tests may use SIPP, NHTS, ATUS, CE, SHED, MEPS, RECS, Treasury/FIO, BLS, BEA, CFPB, company records, and opinion/election sources, but no single dataset is expected to answer the whole chain.

Definition of an end-to-end result

An end-to-end result is not a long narrative or a single household story. It is a documented path in which the project can show, with the appropriate source for each step:

  • what changed;
  • who was exposed and who was not;
  • what choice, behavior, or condition changed next;
  • who controlled the next decision or remedy;
  • where money, time, risk, data, or power moved;
  • what social, cultural, consumer, political, company, sector, or geopolitical meaning is supported;
  • what counterevidence weakens the interpretation; and
  • what joined record or new comparison would test the remaining link.

When the records cannot connect two steps, the honest output is an open question, not a completed causal story.

Recovery rule

If work resumes after a crash, read this file first, then the execution queue, then the relevant bridge record. Do not restart by narrowing the program to the latest dataset or to one household.
