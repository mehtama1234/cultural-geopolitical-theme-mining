When credit makes a shock smaller now and larger later

Short answer

Credit can protect a household from an immediate break: a needed purchase can happen, and a bill can be spread across paychecks. But the payment system can turn a cash shortage into a new deadline. The people most likely to need the bridge may also be more likely to miss a later payment.

The careful finding is not that credit is bad. It is that “access” has two moments: getting the purchase today and carrying the payment tomorrow. A fair reading has to measure both.

What the sources show

1. Many households do not have a cash answer to a small surprise

In the Federal Reserve's 2024 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking, 63% of adults said they could cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent. Fifteen percent said they would put it on a credit card and pay it off over time. Thirteen percent said they would not be able to pay it right away.

This is a response to a hypothetical expense, not a record of what each person later did. It does show that credit is part of the household buffer for a large minority of adults.

Federal Reserve household well-being report

2. Pay-over-time products are used because the first payment is easier

The same Fed survey found that 15% of adults used buy now, pay later in the prior year. Among users, the top reasons were spreading payments and convenience. Fifty-eight percent said it was the only way they could afford the purchase; among users with family income under $50,000, that share was 72%.

This is a useful distinction. A product can be chosen for convenience by one person and because there is no cash alternative by another. The checkout screen does not reveal that difference.

Federal Reserve banking and credit report

3. The later payment is not evenly shared

Among BNPL users in the Fed survey, 24% said they had paid late. The figure was 40% for users with family income below $25,000, 26% for users with income from $25,000 to $49,999, and 13% for users with income of $100,000 or more. Fifty-seven percent of people who paid late said they were charged extra.

This is the strongest distributional clue in the pass: the people using the product to make a purchase possible are not all facing the same chance of a late payment.

Fed BNPL tables and findings

4. Product records show a smaller but different kind of risk

The CFPB's review of six large BNPL providers covers 2019 through 2023. It reports a 4.1% loan-level late-fee rate and a 1.83% loan charge-off rate in 2023. These measures describe loans in provider records, not the share of people who ever reported being late.

That difference is a necessary counterpoint. The survey and provider records are not competing answers to the same question. One describes people; the other describes loans and firms. Most provider-recorded loans were not charged off, and a late survey response does not tell us that a household suffered lasting harm.

CFPB BNPL market report

5. Credit pressure is visible beyond BNPL

The New York Fed reported that US credit-card balances reached $1.28 trillion in the fourth quarter of 2025. It reported 4.8% of outstanding household debt in some stage of delinquency, with mortgage deterioration concentrated in lower-income areas and places where home prices were falling.

This does not show that BNPL caused credit-card or mortgage trouble. It places the small payment decision in a wider setting: households can carry several kinds of debt, and the room to absorb another payment is shaped by place and income.

New York Fed household debt and credit, Q4 2025

The connection

bill or income shock
  -> cash buffer is not enough
  -> credit or pay-over-time purchase
  -> another deadline, fee, balance, or credit-record entry
  -> less room for the next housing, energy, food, or care choice

The first two links are supported by the Fed survey. The product-level late-fee and charge-off measures support the existence of a later payment risk. The final link is still a test, not an established path for the same households.

What changes the meaning of “access”

  • A person with savings uses credit for convenience and pays it off.
  • A person with no cash uses credit to keep the heat, food, medicine or a needed item available.
  • A person can qualify for the product but cannot carry several payment dates at once.
  • A late payment can add a fee, freeze future use, reach a collector, or affect another decision; the exact path depends on the product and record.

These are distinct situations. A single approval rate cannot describe them.

The payment schedule can become a second budget. Four small installments may look easier than one large bill, but they can overlap with rent, utilities, a loan reset, or another purchase. A household that makes the first payment successfully may still be building a cluster of deadlines. The risk is not only the size of each installment; it is the number and timing of obligations that must be remembered and met.

Credit can also protect a household in a way that is hard to see later. Buying tires may preserve a job. Paying for a prescription may prevent a health break. Replacing a refrigerator may protect food. If the purchase is recorded only as debt, the data misses the need it kept intact. If the account is current, it also misses how much future room was consumed to keep the present stable.

The lender's view is different from the household's. A product can be profitable because customers pay interest, fees, or late charges, while the customer experiences it as the only available bridge. That does not establish exploitation in every case. It does mean that a product should be judged by the result after repayment, not only by approval, checkout completion, or provider loss rates.

What is fact, inference, and unknown

Fact: The Fed reports cash-buffer, BNPL-use and late-payment differences. CFPB reports provider-level late-fee and charge-off measures. The New York Fed reports aggregate balances and delinquency patterns.

Inference: Credit may be acting as a household timing tool: it keeps the present purchase intact while moving pressure into the next pay period. The burden may be heavier where income leaves less room for error.

Unknown: We do not know which purchase was made, which bill was delayed, whether the late fee was paid, or whether the household later reduced food, energy, care, housing or work spending.

We also do not know how many plans a household held at once, whether a late payment caused a cascade, or whether the product replaced a worse option. A late payment may be a short inconvenience or a trigger for account closure, collections, or a new high-cost loan. Those paths need to be observed separately before assigning the same meaning to every late mark.

What would change the finding

  • Transaction or panel data following one household from the shock to the borrowing decision and repayment.
  • The same comparison for credit cards, BNPL, overdrafts, payday loans and family help.
  • Product terms matched to income, number of open payment plans, late fees and account freezes.
  • Evidence on whether borrowing prevented a shutoff, missed rent, missed care or job loss.
  • A comparison showing that lower-income users are not more likely to miss payments after controlling for income volatility, number of plans and size of the purchase.

Next test

Use the Fed's BNPL subgroup tables with the CFPB's provider measures and one household panel that records payment timing. Separate three outcomes: the purchase remained possible, the payment was late, and another need was reduced. Do not combine them into a single measure of financial harm.

Run a monthly payment diary linked to account records. For each borrowing event, record the need, the cash available, every open payment date, fees, late status, account freeze, and the next bill that changed. Compare households using credit for a one-time bridge with households carrying several plans. Follow work, housing, food, energy, care, savings, and stress for a year so the test shows whether credit bought recovery or only postponed the shortage.

Reading rule

The survey, provider records and credit panel measure different units. The connection is a working interpretation. Credit can help and still create a later constraint; both parts belong in the finding.
