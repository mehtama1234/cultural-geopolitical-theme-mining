When credit makes a shock smaller now and larger later

Credit can protect a household from an immediate break. But the payment system can turn a cash shortage into a new deadline, and the people most likely to need the bridge may also be more likely to miss a later payment.

In one sentence

“Access” has two moments: getting the purchase today and carrying the payment tomorrow.

Credit can help and still create a later constraint; both parts belong in the finding.

What the sources show

1. Many households lack a cash answer

In the Fed's 2024 household survey, 63% said they could cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. Fifteen percent would use a credit card and pay over time; 13% could not pay right away.

Federal Reserve household well-being report

2. Pay-over-time products make the first payment easier

Fifteen percent used BNPL. Fifty-eight percent said it was the only way they could afford the purchase; among users under $50,000 income, that was 72%.

Federal Reserve banking and credit report

3. The later payment is not evenly shared

Twenty-four percent of BNPL users paid late. The rate was 40% for users under $25,000 income and 13% for users at $100,000 or more. Fifty-seven percent of late users said they were charged extra.

Fed BNPL tables

4. Product records show a different risk rate

CFPB's review of six large providers reports a 4.1% loan-level late-fee rate and a 1.83% loan charge-off rate in 2023. These are loan measures, not the share of people reporting a late payment.

CFPB BNPL market report

The connection

bill or income shock → cash buffer is not enough → credit or pay-over-time purchase → another deadline, fee, balance, or credit-record entry → less room for the next choice

The first two links are supported by the Fed survey. The final link remains a test, not an established path for the same households.

What is still unknown

  • Which purchase was made?
  • Which bill was delayed?
  • Was the late fee paid?
  • Did borrowing prevent a shutoff, missed rent, missed care or job loss?
  • Did the household later reduce food, energy, care, housing or work spending?

Next test

Join the Fed's subgroup tables to provider measures and a household panel that records payment timing. Keep three outcomes separate: the purchase remained possible, the payment was late, and another need was reduced.

Reading rule

The survey, provider records and credit panel measure different units. The connection is a working interpretation.
