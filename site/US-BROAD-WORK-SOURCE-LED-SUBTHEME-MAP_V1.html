Work, control, and bargaining: source-led subtheme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: 248 current registry records carry the work_control_bargaining program-theme label. This map separates labor-market entry, job risk, workplace control, firm technology, household credit, care, and collective representation instead of treating employment as one outcome.

What the source material supports

The evidence does not support a single story in which new technology, employment, or flexibility is uniformly good or bad. It supports a more specific pattern:

A job's practical value depends on its entry route, risk, credit access, control over time and tasks, ability to accommodate care, and ability to contest management—not only on whether it exists.

1. Technology can alter the first career rung before long-run effects are known

The AI-exposed graduates record uses 6.67 million PSEO graduate records and linked employment data. After ChatGPT's release, graduates in the most AI-exposed major decile had about a five-percentage-point lower probability of observed employment immediately after graduation and about 13% lower earnings two quarters later than the least-exposed comparison. About half of the earnings difference was attributed to movement toward lower-paying retail and accommodation/food-service industries. Effects attenuated but remained lower around two years out.

Exposure is assigned from pre-period occupation-task measures, not actual AI use. The selected institutions cover about 29% of bachelor's degrees and the study does not establish national displacement, long-run scarring, household security, or discrimination.

1a. A minimum-wage start does not define one later career, and later-career policy effects can be null

The minimum-wage-worker career record merges 1992–2016 SIPP panels with longitudinal employer-household records. It reports that younger workers starting at the minimum wage had strong subsequent earnings growth and became indistinguishable from peers who began modestly above the minimum; the authors attribute most aggregate growth to these younger trajectories. Older minimum-wage workers instead showed a modest earnings dip around the minimum-wage observation. In its policy analysis, state minimum-wage increases did not significantly change the later careers of workers who were on the minimum wage when the increase occurred.

Supported subtheme: initial wage level, age, later earnings trajectory, and the longer-run career effect of a state wage increase are distinct work surfaces. Neither a low-wage start nor a later-career policy null establishes one common life-course or policy result.
Not established: job quality, stable hours, benefits, household security, the mechanism behind young-worker growth, short-run employment or firm effects, prices, bargaining power, or the absence of unequal policy effects. This is a working paper whose public abstract omits the full sample, comparison, timing, and estimate ledger.

1b. AI-exposed entry markets can lose hires before later recovery restores the employment base

The AI-exposed early-career hiring record uses matched employer-employee administrative data and Quarterly Workforce Indicators in industry-state exposure cells. For workers ages 22–24, adjusted employment declined 12% in the most AI-exposed quintile over the ten quarters after ChatGPT's introduction, while less-exposed industries remained stable; the paper identifies lower hiring as the primary mechanism. Hiring largely recovered by early 2025, but from a smaller employment base, and earnings growth slowed slightly relative to less-exposed industries.

Supported subtheme: technology-exposure classification → entry hiring → early-career employment base and relative earnings path. A recovery in hiring does not erase a preceding cohort's smaller entry base, but neither it nor the exposure classification proves permanent displacement. Not established: firm-level AI use, individual automation, causal effects of ChatGPT rather than demand, interest rates, remote work, education, or sector shifts; lifetime scarring, occupation movement, household security, or the distribution by race, gender, education, family resources, or place. This is a CES working paper, not a Census Bureau endorsement.

1c. Earnings assimilation and remaining in a country are separate migrant work outcomes

The immigrant earnings/return-migration record uses longitudinal administrative earnings histories for U.S. immigrant arrival cohorts from 1981–2010. Roughly one-fifth to one-third of cohorts returned within ten years; return migrants had slower earnings growth than permanent migrants. Among immigrants who remained, cohorts arriving since the mid-1990s reached near-convergence with native-born earnings within ten years in the paper's measure. The NBER summary reports different parity timing by gender: about eight years for men and 19 for women.

Supported subtheme: arrival → earnings path and return-or-remain selection are distinct longitudinal outcomes. A stayer's earnings convergence cannot be used as an all-immigrant outcome when return migration changes the observed composition. Not established: why anyone returned or remained; causal assimilation, wealth, job quality, legal security, benefits, household wellbeing, belonging, or political action. Return can reflect family, health, legal status, planned temporary migration, discrimination, or opportunity elsewhere rather than a failed U.S. labor-market outcome.

2. Job security has an economic value because workers cannot fully insure risk

The job-security and mobility record finds that employed U.S. workers demanded a 1.63% pay raise for each additional percentage point of annual unemployment risk in a new job. Its calibrated model estimates that complete markets would raise mobility 12% and productivity 0.19%, while unemployment insurance can encourage acceptance of riskier higher-productivity offers and also increase job loss and unemployment.

The first result is a stated wage-risk tradeoff and the latter are model results. Neither demonstrates actual job switching, household security, or a universal unemployment-insurance effect.

2a. A usable competing platform can reduce a focal platform's labor-market power without proving better work

The gig-platform outside-options record combines randomized pay experiments among Uber drivers with variation in access to a competing platform. It reports that access to one competitor nearly doubled drivers' labor-supply elasticity to Uber and reduced the study's implied firm markdown from about 68% to about 50%. The source also reports women were about twice as elastic to the market as men while no less elastic to their employer. These are labor-supply and model-implied market-power measures, not logged welfare outcomes for any particular driver.

Supported subtheme: an outside option can make platform-work dependence more contestable even without a union, a formal appeal, or a change in the focal platform's stated rules. Market responsiveness and firm-specific dependence are distinct from one another.
Not established: net earnings after vehicle and waiting costs, hours stability, benefit/safety effects, successful sustained switching, household security, or whether nominal competitor access was equally usable across places and drivers. The public abstract does not disclose the full analytic denominator or study-area frame.

3. Firm automation is a technology choice before it is a worker outcome

The minimum-wage and robot-adoption record finds that a 10% higher minimum wage was associated with about 8% more industrial-robot adoption relative to the mean in the paper's state-border design. The public source does not report the worker outcomes needed to decide whether adoption meant displacement, safer work, higher productivity, wages, hours, or greater worker control.

Supported subtheme: policy and firm technology decisions can move before the distribution of their worker consequences is visible.
Not established: who gained or lost work, pay, safety, or bargaining power.

3a. AI access can change the timing of retained work before it changes the work's task structure

The generative-AI work-pattern record is a six-month randomized field experiment across 7,137 knowledge workers at 66 participating firms. About 80% of treated workers used the integrated tool. The current abstract reports about two fewer weekly email hours among treated users and reduced after-hours work; the revised public abstract reports 3.6 fewer email hours for frequent users and a 1.3-hour assignment-based estimate. Those are different estimands and are not pooled. It reports moderately faster document completion, no significant meeting-time change, and no detected shift in task quantity or composition from individual-level provision.

Supported subtheme: a technology intervention can alter a bounded time-of-work surface before it produces detectable task redesign. Access, take-up, email time, after-hours work, meeting time, and task composition are different worker outcomes.

Not established: lower total workload, who retained the saved time, care or rest use, performance expectations, pay, job security, autonomy, firm productivity, health, long-run job redesign, or generalization beyond the participating knowledge-worker firms.

3b. AI coding activity can scale far faster than shipped and used output

The AI coding production-stage record uses a matched event study covering more than 500,000 GitHub developers and four software marketplaces. It reports cumulative commit increases of 30% for autocomplete tools, 180% for interactive agents, and 240% for autonomous agents. For autonomous agents, projects rose 80% and actual releases 30%, while total usage across the four marketplaces did not increase despite more new apps.

Supported subtheme: AI-assisted task activity, project creation, software release, and user adoption are separate production stages. An increase in code or projects can attenuate before it becomes a shipped and used product, so it cannot by itself establish productivity, revenue, or a worker gain.
Not established: code quality, security, user value, firm profitability, wages, displacement, workload, bargaining power, or economy-wide productivity. The associations may also reflect developer selection, task mix, quality control, demand, discovery, or measurement timing; the study is not an all-firm causal welfare evaluation.

3c. A retained job can lose contribution-based meaning before automation eliminates it

The automation-and-work-meaning record is a theoretical NBER model in which workers value both output and knowing that their contribution is needed. A credible machine alternative can reduce that contribution-based meaning even while a firm retains the worker. If wages adjust fully, model compensation can offset part of the loss; with partial adjustment, workers bear part of it. The model also produces a feedback loop where reduced value of the human alternative makes automation more likely.

Supported subtheme: job retention, contribution-based meaning, compensation, and automation adoption are conceptually distinct. A headcount or employment-rate result alone cannot settle whether retained work retains the same social and psychological value. Not established: how many workers experience this mechanism, observed wage/health/identity or exit effects, actual automation adoption, productivity, displacement, or political action. Automation can also remove dangerous or tedious work and create different sources of agency or meaning; these are model comparative statics, not empirical estimates.

4. Temporary work can restrict both earnings security and the credit needed for adulthood

The temporary-work and credit record reports that temporary workers face higher income risk than permanent workers, including within occupation and at similar wages, and that lenders ration credit to them. The study frames mortgages, smoothing credit, and durable-good loans as a second barrier alongside labor-market insecurity.

It does not identify an individual application, credit score, lender decision, household support, desired contract, or later housing and family outcome.

5. Worker voice has a request stage, a grant stage, and a job-structure stage

The worker-request and control record reports that 5.9 million wage and salary workers ages 16–75—4.1% of employed people—requested or made a change to do their job better in July 2024. The most common requests concerned schedules (2.61 million), equipment (1.48 million), and tasks (1.41 million). Among schedule requests, 70.1% were fully granted, 21.9% partly granted, and 7.5% not granted.

The same record separately shows job structure: 53.5% of workers had an ability to pause work, 18.5% had self-paced workloads, and 48.3% had employer changes to days, times, or hours from week to week. A grant is not proof that a change was adequate, timely, durable, non-stigmatizing, or sufficient for retention.

6. Collective representation is limited and cannot stand in for practical control

The union-representation record reports 10.0% union membership and 11.2% representation among employed wage and salary workers in 2025. The 2025 annual estimate excludes October because of the federal shutdown, limiting strict year-over-year comparison.

Representation can matter while still leaving schedule control, appeal, autonomy, accommodation, and exit unmeasured.

7. Care shocks reveal how workplace tolerance conditions employment resilience

The wildfire-smoke and maternal employment record uses plausibly exogenous smoke variation to show school closures and absenteeism creating intermittent caregiving demand. The study finds substantial contemporaneous maternal labor-market costs and employment loss when shocks accumulated; losses were fully offset among mothers outside male-dominated industries. Industry composition is a proxy for workplace tolerance, not a direct employer-policy or bargaining measure.

Supported subtheme: the ability to keep a job during a care shock depends on the social and organizational design around the job, not solely on formal childcare availability.
Not established: the household income, care substitution, health, recovery, or national employment effects.

8. Voice and remedy have request, charge, resolution, receipt, and durability stages

The BLS worker-request record reports 5.9 million workers requested or made a change to do their job better; schedule requests were fully granted 70.1% of the time, but a grant is not proof it was adequate, timely, maintained, or sufficient to retain the job. Its separate job-structure data show pause control, self-paced work, and schedule variability are not evenly distributed.

The NLRB administration record records 19,754 unfair-labor-practice charges, 32.8% found meritorious, and 96.8% of meritorious cases settled; it separately reports representation petitions and Board-quorum constraints. Charges, merits, settlements, and decisions are administrative stages rather than measures of durable power.

The EEOC recourse record reports inquiries, charges, resolutions, and more than $528 million in prelitigation monetary relief. Aggregate relief does not show individual receipt, adequacy, employer compliance, retaliation, continued employment, or workplace change.

The OpenAI/Statsig PERM-recruitment settlement record puts that distinction inside one named access case. The August 2026 DOJ agreement identifies five OpenAI and one Statsig PERM-related recruitments in which U.S. workers had to apply by mail while others could apply electronically. It requires a $1.2 million Treasury payment, reserves $2 million for potential back pay, and specifies electronic posting/application, applicant-tracking, review, reporting, and three years of oversight controls. Those are formal route repair and potential recovery, not observed recovery: the agreement does not disclose affected-worker or applicant counts, payments made, completed implementation, or restored employment opportunity, and it is not an admission of liability.

The union-representation record reports 10.0% membership and 11.2% representation among wage/salary workers in 2025. Formal coverage contextualizes collective voice; it does not reveal whether a worker can alter pace, schedule, pay, safety, grievance outcome, or exit risk.

The USMCA rapid-response remedy record adds a named cross-border enforcement route at a San Luis Potosí facility. A petition reached the U.S. interagency review process in October 2025; after a U.S. request to Mexico, USTR reported two workers reinstated under their former terms with full backpay and benefits, plus union-access, neutrality, collective-bargaining, anti-retaliation, training, and Mexican-monitoring measures. The United States then resumed liquidation of unliquidated facility entries. The official announcement is not an independent worker follow-up.

Supported subtheme: worker power is a chain of practical conversion from need through request, institutional route, decision, remedy receipt, and a durable changed job—not a filing, settlement, or membership rate. Cross-border trade enforcement can reach a reported reinstatement-and-facility-rule stage without thereby proving durable worker power.
Not established: independent payment receipt, whether all affected workers were remedied, later non-retaliation, effective union access/bargaining, safety, recurrence, household security, trust, or general USMCA performance.

8a. AI-generated recruiting language can create an exclusion before an institutional correction reaches an applicant

The Elegant AI-hiring settlement record documents two AI-generated job advertisements at a Virginia IT-services provider that restricted some positions by citizenship status, including consideration only of H-1B, OPT, or H-4 visa holders, without legal justification. A February 2026 DOJ settlement imposed a $9,460 civil penalty, training, a three-year prohibition on future unlawful exclusion, bilingual rights posters, policy revision, attendance records, and a monitoring/cure process under which IER can request reports, inspect, interview personnel, and allow 30 days to cure a suspected violation before further enforcement.

Supported subtheme: recruiting automation can enter a worker-access route before a hiring decision: tool-generated language, ad posting, applicant exposure, institutional charge, correction rule, monitoring, and later job opportunity are separate stages. A settlement can create a concrete institutional control loop without proving an applicant's access was restored. Not established: ad reach, number of affected applicants, a particular job denial, whether the tool independently created the restriction, back pay, individual receipt, completed controls, later compliance, restored job consideration, or the prevalence of AI-mediated discrimination. The public agreement's charging party and affected positions are redacted.

8b. A recruitment remedy can complete individualized back-pay administration without restoring a job opportunity

The Apple PERM back-pay record tracks the implementation of a 2023 DOJ recruitment-discrimination settlement. DOJ reported that Apple had not posted PERM positions on its external site and required paper applications by mail while allowing electronic applications for other jobs, a practice it said deterred protected workers. The $25 million agreement included a $6.75 million civil penalty and an $18.25 million back-pay fund. In May 2026, DOJ reported the back-pay fund had been processed and exhausted after individualized review of thousands of potential claimants' lost income, with qualifying workers compensated. External posting, electronic-application, applicant-searchability, training, and three years of monitoring were required; DOJ reported some measures were implemented after the investigation began.

Supported subtheme: a recruitment barrier can travel through investigation, settlement, claimant review, individualized payment administration, and prospective access controls. Exhaustion of a fund is stronger payment-stage evidence than a reserved fund, but payment completion and restored opportunity remain different endpoints. Not established: number of workers paid, individual amounts, adequacy, restored consideration or hiring, recurrence, long-term compliance, worker voice, household security, or trust. The implementation statement is DOJ's official account, not an independent worker follow-up.

8c. A formal access-restoration order is stronger than a complaint route but weaker than verified earnings recovery

The comparative Rehman platform-remedy record preserves the complete public sequence for one Australian employee-like platform worker rather than implying that an order is a typical result. After an alleged failed background check, the worker's account was deactivated; following an application, the platform communicated voluntary reactivation on 20 February 2026. On 20 March, the Fair Work Commission issued concurrent formal reactivation and gross lost-remuneration orders. The remuneration order was A$7,096.96, calculated using a 9.5-week gap.

Supported subtheme: a digital-work exclusion can move through account loss, application, voluntary reactivation, adjudicated access restoration, and an ordered pay amount. Those steps make remedy architecture visible and separate from a complaint intake or a bare policy commitment.

Not established: payment receipt or net recovery, avoided expenses, alternative work, continued account access, recurrence prevention, household security, trust, collective action, practical exit, U.S. applicability, or platform-worker incidence. One Australian case cannot identify typical worker outcomes.

8d. A large worker-redress distribution identifies a payment route without showing restored earnings

The Handy worker-earnings redress record follows a U.S. worker-facing enforcement route through payment distribution. The FTC reported alleged deceptive earnings claims and undisclosed fees and fines that withheld money from Handy/Angi platform workers' wages. In July 2026, the agency reported sending more than $2.7 million in checks to 62,893 eligible people, with a 90-day cashing instruction. The cited recipient count is an enforcement-program population, not a prevalence estimate of platform worker exposure.

Supported subtheme: a remedy can move from alleged workplace practice to identified eligibility, administrator distribution, and a cashing window. A large check-sent count is materially stronger than a reserved settlement fund, but distribution, cashing, and recovered earnings remain separate stages.

Not established: individual payment amount, check receipt or cashing, remaining loss, net wage restoration, ongoing platform access, alternative work, bargaining power, recurrence, trust, switching, or exit. The FTC page does not publish an aggregate accepted, returned, or expired-check total.

9. Retraining can raise earnings by moving workers away from exposed work, not necessarily by making them more AI-intensive

The AI retraining record compares WIOA occupational-training users with matched job-search-only service users. For workers starting in highly AI-exposed occupations, estimated quarterly earnings returns rose from about $1,000 in 2012–2019 to about $3,000 in 2022–2024. The source attributes most gains to transitions into less AI-exposed occupations, with a smaller AI-complementary-training role.

Supported subtheme: adaptation to technological exposure can be an occupational-mobility route, rather than a simple upskilling-within-the-same-job route. Earnings are an observed endpoint, but transition feasibility is unequally distributed. Not established: training completion, causal gains for every participant, job quality, hours, benefits, stability, household security, or who could access and afford the transition.

9a. Public workforce participation does not ensure an equally usable training or transition route for older workers

The older-worker workforce-development record reports 769,265 participants age 55 or older across six selected federal workforce programs in program year 2023--24, about 22% of their participants. Older participants used training less often than younger participants in each compared adult program—for example, 23% versus 45% in WIOA Adult and 14% versus 25% in WIOA Dislocated Worker. Reported second-quarter post-exit employment was about 60% for older adults versus 69% for ages 40--54 and 73% for ages 16--39 in selected DOL programs; vocational-rehabilitation figures were similarly lower. Interviews with 41 older workers at 10 job centers give context on digital literacy, application, skills, openings, and possible age-discrimination challenges, but are not a prevalence measure.

Supported subtheme: a workforce system can reach many older adults while training uptake, post-exit employment, and local practice-sharing remain separate conversion stages. Entry and an administrative post-exit status do not establish a viable late-career transition.
Not established: age discrimination, program causality, training quality or completion, earnings, job quality, retention, retirement timing, or household security. The selected programs and sites do not represent every workforce route.

9b. An average long-run employment gain can conceal negative worker trajectories and uneven housing protection

The Nevada job-search-assistance record follows a randomized 2009 unemployment-insurance intervention through up to eight years of tax, UI, disability-insurance, and housing records. Among 32,751 UI recipients, 4,673 were offered a required individualized meeting combining eligibility review, skills assessment, work-search planning, resume help, and referrals. The offer increased long-run employment and earnings, reduced short-run UI receipt, and reduced homeownership loss on average; positive housing effects were concentrated among people with positive employment effects. Yet the paper estimates negative long-run employment effects for about 40% of the sample, corroborated by higher disability-insurance entry for that subgroup.

Supported subtheme: a work-search intervention can reach later employment, earnings, benefit, and ownership stages in linked administrative evidence, but an average gain is not a universal recovery pathway.
Not established: which component of counseling/enforcement/referral drove the effect; job quality, voluntary job choice, housing adequacy, health, care burden, durable security, or applicability beyond the Nevada UI setting.

9c. Extra job-search time can have a weaker earnings payoff where local employer power is higher

The UI-duration and concentration record uses quasi-random unemployment-insurance duration assignment during the Great Recession and matched employer-employee data. Extensions lengthened nonemployment by about one week. The paper reports economically meaningful but statistically nonsignificant earnings increases overall, while the positive earnings effect was significantly lower in more concentrated local labor markets. Local concentration did not change the extension effect on nonemployment duration.

Supported subtheme: a benefit can purchase additional job-search time without purchasing the same match-quality return in every labor market. Search duration, later earnings, and employer concentration therefore occupy distinct stages; a longer spell is not automatically a worse or better outcome.

Not established: a universal monopsony effect, the precise mechanism behind the concentration interaction, job quality, bargaining, household budget, health/care constraints, long-run mobility, or whether a worker's extra search time was voluntary and adequately supported. The result is a Great Recession working-paper estimate, not a current national UI effect.

10. The explanation for digital monitoring can matter as much as the monitoring itself

The digital-surveillance record randomizes monitoring and explanations in an online labor market. Surveillance had no statistically significant average performance effect, but failing to explain why monitoring was present or removed significantly reduced output; the response varied with the justification offered to lower- and higher-productivity workers.

Supported subtheme: managerial transparency is a work-control condition, not merely communications style. The same tool can carry different consequences depending on whether workers can understand the rule governing them. Not established: workplace prevalence, pay, autonomy, health, appeal, retention, collective action, or effects outside the online experiment.

11. Employer-linked dependent coverage can retain workers without revealing whether staying is voluntary or constraining

The dependent-coverage record uses an ACA birth-month eligibility discontinuity in employer-insurance claims. Additional dependent coverage increased both dependent take-up and parental job retention, with stronger effects for parents near a job-exit margin, families placing more value on dependent coverage, and employees with more employer insurance options.

Supported subtheme: health coverage is part of a job's practical exit cost. Retention can represent valued insurance continuity, constrained mobility, or both; employment retention alone cannot distinguish them. Not established: the job's pay/schedule/safety quality, family care use, premium/network burden, portability, household welfare, or the effect of a different coverage system.

12. A short public job can become a longer labor-market attachment for a bounded group

The temporary-public-hiring record links 2010 Census applicant/hiring records to W-2 outcomes through 2023. For applicants near the required test-score threshold, receiving a temporary Census job increased 2010 W-2 employment by 61 points and 2023 employment by 28 points, with nearly $6,000 higher annual W-2 earnings due to greater likelihood of working. Conditional wages did not significantly differ; the longer-run effect was concentrated among men, and broader-applicant effects faded by 2011.

Supported subtheme: a temporary public employment route can provide labor attachment, credentialing, or other pathway value beyond its duration for a specific marginal applicant group. Not established: a general job-guarantee effect, job quality, displacement of other work, equal benefit, household wellbeing, or political legitimacy.

13. Formal leave access can coexist with an unmet need for usable time

The leave-access record finds that 71% of wage and salary workers reported paid leave access, 78% unpaid access, and 94% either, yet 8% said they needed leave in an average month but did not take it. Among workers with unmet need, about 40% needed it for their own illness/care and 24% for a family member; 29% cited too much work, 14% feared job consequences or had a request denied, and 13% could not afford income loss. Paid-leave access ranged from 90% in management/business/ financial occupations to 45% in service occupations and from 81% of full-time to 32% of part-time single jobholders.

Supported subtheme: a policy listed as available is not necessarily usable at the moment of care need; workload, job risk, and income loss create a second practical-control layer.
Not established: why an individual did not take leave, whether care was resolved, or later health, job retention, family stability, and recovery.

14. A work incentive can redistribute family eldercare without a measured replacement

The EITC parent-care record estimates that a $500 increase in EITC generosity reduced care from eligible daughters to older parents by 13–24 hours per month, driven by less intensive (40+-hour) care. The study found no increase in financial transfers, other children's/spouses' care, or paid care that filled the observed gap. It uses a simulated-benefit approach in a working paper and does not measure care quality, safety, health, or long-run work effects.

Supported subtheme: work and income incentives can move unpaid care time across generations, with the replacement-care question distinct from the labor incentive itself.
Not established: that reduced care harmed parents, that all families lacked alternatives, or the policy's aggregate welfare effect.

15. Whistleblowing requires usable protection and processing capacity, not simply a reporting channel

The whistleblower-protection record finds selected agencies use different combinations of confidentiality, retaliation investigation/relief, and monetary awards. It reports that trust can encourage disclosure while fear of adverse outcomes discourages it, and that agencies face high report volumes and difficulty protecting identity when witness participation is needed. The review does not connect a reporter to case validity, retaliation, enforcement, correction, or later trust.

Supported subtheme: workplace/public voice is an institutional sequence of exposure, confidence, reporting, identity protection, triage, investigation, and correction—not a tip count.
Not established: reporting prevalence, retaliation rate, typical reporter safety, enforcement effectiveness, or durable institutional learning.

16. Workforce-program eligibility does not guarantee an accessible route to labor-market attachment

The disability workforce-access record finds 7% of participants in selected DOL programs reported a disability, but 43% of those did not report a disability type. Their post-exit employment and median earnings were lower in each core program; for example, second-quarter Adult-program employment was 60% versus 76% for participants not reporting a disability, and training use 33% versus 47%. Visits to 12 nonrepresentative job centers found interpreter and accessible off-site training challenges, and GAO found DOL did not routinely analyze local monitoring reports in aggregate.

Supported subtheme: legal eligibility and nominal accessibility sit before data visibility, accommodation, service completion, training receipt, and job attachment; each can distribute opportunity differently.
Not established: the causal role of access barriers, accommodation request or receipt, completion, placement, retention, or a specific participant's outcome.

16a. Multiple disability-employment funding streams can widen formal support while creating handoff gaps

The disability employment-coordination record examines the boundary between vocational rehabilitation (VR) and Medicaid home- and community-based services for people with intellectual or developmental disabilities. GAO cites about $3.7 billion in FY2023 VR grants and a separate estimate of about $1.5 billion in employment services for this population, mostly through Medicaid HCBS; the figures have different scopes. In three selected states, six of 12 interviewed providers reported service gaps from coordination burden or confusion and four reported reimbursement delays during transitions. A Washington provider association described some job-placement authorizations taking six to nine months rather than a week or less; a Georgia provider described continuing services for three months without reimbursement during a transition.

The report also supplies non-universal counterexamples: Georgia used specialist staff, dual providers, weekly case tracking, and monthly troubleshooting, while Pennsylvania allowed some cases to proceed with Medicaid HCBS employment services when VR had not decided eligibility within 120 days. These are selected practices, not comparative effectiveness results.

Supported subtheme: eligibility, service authorization, provider payment, and employment support can sit in different administrative systems. Coordinated funding may enlarge the formal menu while the transfer between systems becomes a practical access and continuity gate.

Not established: national gap prevalence, individual referral-to-service timing, job placement, retention, wages, provider solvency, whether a named delay caused a job loss, or durable autonomy and household security.

Cross-subtheme finding and next test

The AI exposure/adaptive-capacity record adds a distinct technology-risk boundary: the published revision estimates 3.3 million workers in highly AI-exposed occupations with lower expected adaptive capacity, concentrated in clerical and support roles. Exposure is potential task change, not realized displacement; the revision's changed coverage means its figures cannot be pooled with the earlier working-paper counts. Neither version measures layoffs, retraining completion, wages, bargaining power, or household security.
