The whole program

How society moves costs, choices, and power.

We are studying broad US societal, cultural, consumer, financial, political, institutional, firm, infrastructure, and geopolitical trends. Household records are one instrument—not the subject that replaces the wider map.

The end-to-end question

When a condition, price, rule, technology, or institutional decision changes people’s available choices, how does that change travel through households, consumers, workers, firms, places, culture, public systems, and political or state power?

We follow who pays, who waits, who can switch or appeal, who controls the next decision, and where the cost or gain ends up.

The shared research chain

condition or decision
  → exposure and available alternatives
  → money, time, access, control, or status change
  → household, consumer, worker, firm, place, or public response
  → distribution of cost, gain, risk, and ownership
  → cultural meaning, trust, political action, or state leverage

Each arrow needs its own evidence. Separate datasets can be aligned by question and time, but they are not silently treated as the same people, firms, or households.

The 14-theme map

1. Household room

How prices, payments, food, debt, and savings change what people can protect or sacrifice.

2. Time as price

How work, care, travel, waiting, and paperwork consume an unequal resource.

3. Consumer recourse

Who can get an answer, appeal, repair, refund, correction, or exit.

4. Platforms and attention

How data, ranking, source environments, and visibility shape choice and public life.

5. Work and bargaining

Who controls tasks, schedules, benefits, data, risk, and the gain from tools.

6. Care and social reproduction

Who absorbs health and care costs through bills, unpaid labor, work, food, or family time.

7. Housing and mobility

How risk, insurance, energy, transport, repair, and local services shape staying or moving.

8. Unequal exposure

How income, race, age, disability, family, tenure, place, and access change real options.

9. Trust and meaning

How experience becomes dignity, blame, belonging, identity, legitimacy, or distrust.

10. Public systems

How rules, eligibility, administration, interruption, and remedy change security and demand.

11. Political action

How interpretation becomes discussion, organizing, contact, voting, withdrawal, or policy response.

12. Firm and market power

Who can set prices, pass on costs, control data, restrict access, or make others absorb risk.

13. Infrastructure and dependency

How capital, energy, chips, cloud, skills, ownership, and replaceability shape capability.

14. Geopolitical consequences

How domestic capacity and dependence affect state choice, bargaining, alliances, and external power.

What has been added recently

  • Trust, meaning, and action: material experience does not become political action automatically.
  • Price, market power, belief, and action: direct and delayed cost paths meet household adaptation and political interpretation.
  • Capability, dependency, local distribution, and state power: infrastructure can build capacity and dependence together.
  • Care cost, work, family time, and security: health needs are paid through several currencies.
  • Place risk, housing security, mobility, and local life: staying is not automatically secure staying.
  • Optionality, unequal exposure, time, and exit: cash, time, alternatives, rights, support, voice, and practical exit are separate resources.
  • SHED panel persistence: the same respondents show repeated adaptations and unequal financial-condition persistence across years.
  • SHED adaptation by condition path: persistence and re-entry are compared across worsening, stable, and improving financial-condition paths.
  • Time, work, care, and social participation: work and care can displace social and civic availability, while schedule and location control are unequally distributed.
  • Economic adaptation, perception, and public action: consumer response, economic judgment, trust, cultural meaning, and political action remain separate arrows.
  • ANES panel judgment and action: financial worry is compared with institutional trust and later reported vote choice in a multi-election panel, descriptively.
  • CPS turnout and participation friction: voting, registration, and nonvoting reasons are separated into distinct population-level action barriers.
  • Material conditions to participation: the separate source layers are mapped into one societal connector without pretending they are one household panel.
  • Time and care to political availability: work, care, social time, and reported voting barriers are compared as distinct population measures.
  • Complaint-system scale and screening: received complaints, routing, response, publication, and remedy are separated as institutional stages.
  • Product-specific response asymmetry: explanations, relief, prior contact, and untimely response vary by financial product.
  • Consumer-financial cultural themes: complaint encounters reveal recurring questions of identity, access, clarity, human reach, time, dependence, and repair.
  • Public administration and benefit transitions: recorded entry/exit reasons receive uncertainty estimates while unclassified transitions remain visible.
  • Recourse visibility and remedy: digital access, narrative voice, response speed, company explanation, and verified relief are separate stages.
  • Platform data, attention, and agency: encounter, interpretation, action, and the ability to contest or leave are separate outcomes.
  • Firm and market power distribution: a firm decision can move price, time, data, risk, and control among customers, workers, owners, places, and public systems.
  • Infrastructure realization and control: planned capacity, operating milestones, ownership changes, local benefit, and state exit are measured separately.

What remains open

The map does not yet prove that these pressures are rising together, affect the same households, produce the same cultural meaning, or cause a specific vote or state response. The next depth work follows defined people, cases, firms, places, or institutions through time and seeks counterexamples at every important arrow.
