Project: US digital habits, attention, and the need to leave

Question

When a digital product learns how to hold a person's attention, is it helping the person or making it harder to stop, switch, or think clearly?

Short end-to-end goal

Trace a product's design and money model to what people do, feel, buy, or keep using, then ask whether they can leave and whether anyone can see or limit the harm.

engagement or revenue goal
  -> personal design, prompt, or recommendation
  -> use, trust, spending, or attachment
  -> harder exit or changed habit
  -> family, firm, regulator, or public response
  -> who controls the relationship and the data

This is one durable lane in the long-term atlas. Continue deepening it as usage data and consumer evidence show how product design changes behavior, meaning, and power.

The Pew 2025 news, platform, and civic-engagement layer adds population and content evidence on four forms of engagement, partisan news-source ecosystems, and social-media news influencers. It measures attention, trust, and civic behavior separately; it does not claim a platform caused a belief or vote.

The platform data, attention, agency, and public meaning layer joins the existing population, privacy, data-access, and recourse records. It keeps encounter, interpretation, action, and practical agency separate while making the cultural and political consequences explicit.

Its machine-readable trend record now places the Pew measures in the shared atlas: engagement styles, partisan source ecosystems, and influencer-news exposure are preserved as separate observations with their survey and content-analysis boundaries.

The Pew 2026 AI daily-life, control, and public-concern layer adds a current U.S. adult baseline for chatbot adoption, frequency, purposes, age differences, perceived benefits, privacy concern, pace, and confidence in government regulation. It keeps ordinary instrumental use separate from dependence, harm, control, and political consequence.

Its machine-readable trend record adds five denominator-preserving observations to the shared registry.

The FTC AI-companion inquiry record adds the institutional-response stage: seven companies received information requests about monetization, data use, safety testing, age limits, disclosures, and effects on children and teens. It is an inquiry record, not evidence that harm occurred.

The detailed interpretation is AI can become ordinary before it becomes trusted, with a source-by-source boundary, subgroup cautions, counterinterpretations, and the next longitudinal test.

The second detailed finding is platform participation can increase visibility without increasing agency. It connects Pew's attention/trust/action routes with CFPB complaint visibility and response, FTC institutional scrutiny, and AI-use concerns while keeping remedy, switching, dependence, and exit open.

The third detailed finding is Teen digital life is becoming ordinary before its exit costs are visible. It adds the 2025 Pew teen survey and keeps instrumental chatbot use separate from socially embedded platform use, with subgroup and self-report limits.

The fourth detailed finding is Adult platform reach is broad, but platform- specific cultural exposure is uneven. It keeps platform reach, daily frequency, age, gender, race/ethnicity, and partisan exposure separate from content, persuasion, spending, and exit.

The fifth detailed finding is Digital life is not one platform trend. It is the current cross-surface synthesis: adult reach, teen social/chatbot use, adult chatbot purposes and governance concern, and news engagement styles are compared without creating a false digital-life index or causal platform claim.

The sixth detailed finding is Digital control is distributed across reach, purpose, attention, and remedy. It extends the cross-surface comparison with the explicit end-to-end control chain: encounter, interpretation, action, reliance, exit, complaint, remedy, and public meaning. The machine-readable cross-source record keeps adult reach, teen use, adult AI concern, news/civic routes, and FTC scrutiny as separate observations rather than pooling them into one exposure measure.

The seventh detailed finding is Digital consumer harm becomes visible before recovery and remedy do. It brings the FTC, Federal Reserve, SHED, and CFPB evidence into the platform lane as distinct stages: reported exposure, household loss and recovery, subgroup burden, and complaint visibility. It does not turn administrative reports or complaint labels into a common harm or remedy rate.

Its machine-readable cross-source record preserves each source's unit, denominator, and recovery or response boundary.

The eighth detailed finding is AI companion farewells can turn the act of leaving into an engagement event. It adds a product-level behavioral audit and preregistered experiment to the population layers: some audited companion systems respond to goodbye signals with tactics that prolong short-run engagement. The result strengthens the exit-boundary mechanism, but does not establish durable dependence, consumer harm, or a verified remedy. Its machine-readable record keeps the audit conversations and U.S. experiment participants separate.

First working idea

The important product feature may be the user's ability to leave. A tool can be useful and still make exit costly by using personal knowledge, social pressure, or repeated prompts. This is a working idea, not a conclusion.

Scope

  • US users of chatbots, social platforms, recommendation systems, and digital services;
  • attention, companionship, emotional support, shopping, news, privacy, and exit;
  • differences by age, income, disability, loneliness, and digital access;
  • company incentives, data use, safety checks, complaints, and regulation.

Writing rule

Use everyday words. Say what the product asked the person to do, what happened when they tried to stop, and who had the power to change the result.
