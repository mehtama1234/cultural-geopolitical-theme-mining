Online health authority is built from credentials, relatability, and platform reach

The deeper finding

Pew analyzed 12,800 social-media accounts belonging to 6,828 prominent health and wellness influencers. The accounts had at least 100,000 followers on Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube, or belonged to hosts of top-ranked podcasts. Forty-one percent of these influencers described themselves as health care professionals, while 31% described themselves as coaches and 28% as entrepreneurs. Many also drew on parenthood or personal health experience.

Forty percent of U.S. adults—and half of adults under 50—said they ever get health and wellness information from influencers or podcasts. Among those consumers, 10% trust all or most of the information, 65% trust some, and 24% trust not too much or none. Still, 54% said the information helped them better understand how to be healthy; 26% said it made them more worried, while 12% said it made them more confused.

What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a cultural and consumer version of the health-information trust problem. Online authority is not simply replacing medical expertise. It is assembled from professional labels, business identity, parenthood, lived experience, visual familiarity, and audience scale. A person may turn to an influencer because the message is relatable or practical even while trusting only part of it.

The platform route matters. The source is encountered through recommendation, short-form video, or podcast attention, not necessarily through a deliberate search for a qualified clinician. That creates a distinct kind of public legitimacy: influence can be real before accuracy, credentials, commercial interests, or downstream effects are clear.

What the report does not establish

  • Profile categories are self-descriptions and do not verify credentials or scientific accuracy.
  • The 6,828 accounts are prominent, large-audience accounts, not all health creators or private communities.
  • The survey combines influencers and podcasts and relies on self-reported experience.
  • Trust, understanding, worry, or confusion do not show a medical decision or health outcome.
  • The study does not establish misinformation prevalence, sponsorship effects, or causal harm.

Next test

Follow one health claim from platform exposure through source identity, sponsorship, verification, clinician contact, care, treatment, spending, adherence, and outcome. Compare professional, coaching, entrepreneurial, and lived-experience messages while preserving the recommendation and audience context.

Evidence status: Pew documents influencer identity categories, audience reach, trust, and perceived effects; claim accuracy, medical action, and health outcomes remain open.

Reading rule

Do not treat audience size as expertise or skepticism as no influence. Ask what kind of authority the person presents, how the message reached the user, and what happened after exposure.
