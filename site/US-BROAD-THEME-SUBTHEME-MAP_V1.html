US broad cultural, social, political, consumer, and geopolitical theme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24 Purpose: make the project's broad discovery target explicit. This is a research map, not a claim that every subtheme is moving in the same direction.

What this map is for

The project is looking for deep patterns in how people, households, workers, firms, institutions, communities, and states experience and respond to change. A theme becomes a finding only when evidence identifies the unit, time, comparison, direction, uncertainty, and counterexample. The subthemes below are questions to investigate, not conclusions.

Start from the source corpus

The starting point is the available evidence, not a preferred story about what must be happening. Each candidate trend begins with a machine-readable record in the source registry: its source unit, geography, time period, denominator, measures, method, uncertainty, counterinterpretation, and source URL are retained before it is grouped into a theme. The current registry has 792 records and 1,867 observations; its machine-generated theme coverage view shows where those records are tagged across the 14 theme families.

Use the corpus in this order:

  1. Read a record's measured unit and boundary first. A source can establish a price, plan, allegation, administrative status, survey response, or operational action without establishing its presumed downstream effect.
  2. Group comparable or explicitly complementary records into a theme or subtheme. Do not pool their percentages or treat their source populations as one sample.
  3. State the narrow recurring pattern and its counterexample: what is visible, who is covered, and which stage is still missing.
  4. Promote only a source-traceable pattern into the reader synthesis. The evidence matrix, coverage matrix, and current theme pulse are the checks that keep the source, theme, and open link together.

This makes the 14 themes a navigation system rather than a filter that forces sources into predetermined conclusions. New available sources may sharpen an existing subtheme, contradict it, expose an unequal denominator, or reveal a new route altogether.

1. Household room and consumption

Core question: What can people still afford, and what do they quietly delay, replace, borrow for, or give up?

  • price pressure, inflation perception, and budget composition;
  • fees, subscriptions, hidden charges, and payment timing;
  • food adequacy, medical spending, utilities, repairs, and transport;
  • credit, debt, savings, liquidity, insurance, and financial buffers;
  • quality downgrading, smaller quantities, secondhand use, and non-use;
  • household bargaining over whose needs are protected or sacrificed;
  • median-versus-tail movement, percentile inequality, and whether aggregate improvement reaches the lower distribution;
  • consumer confidence, perceived fairness, and economic blame.

2. Time as a hidden price

Core question: Who gets time back from convenience, automation, or public services, and who pays through waiting and unpaid effort?

  • commuting, travel distance, and transport reliability;
  • caregiving, housework, shopping, and unpaid coordination;
  • waiting, paperwork, appeals, customer-service loops, and search effort;
  • schedule control, shift volatility, multiple jobs, and availability;
  • time poverty, sleep, leisure, social participation, and civic availability;
  • convenience for one actor created by labor or delay for another;
  • time-saving technology versus time intensification and surveillance.

3. Consumer power and recourse

Core question: Can a person understand the deal, challenge the decision, reach a human, obtain correction, and leave?

  • disclosure, comprehension, consent, and dark-pattern exposure;
  • complaint routes, documentation burden, identity verification, and appeal;
  • refund, repair, replacement, reimbursement, and account correction;
  • payment failure, fraud, unauthorized transactions, and dispute rights;
  • remedy reachability, returned or uncashed payments, and repeat contact;
  • switching costs, lock-in, practical exit, and forced dependence;
  • whether formal rights become usable power in everyday life.

4. Platforms, data, and attention

Core question: How do digital systems organize what people see, choose, share, believe, remember, and depend on?

  • ranking, recommendation, reviews, search visibility, and reputation;
  • identity checks, automated decisions, moderation, and deactivation;
  • privacy, data extraction, consent, personalization, and surveillance;
  • attention capture, notification load, compulsive use, and self-regulation;
  • AI companionship, emotional substitution, and digital dependence;
  • platform-owned products, interoperability, portability, and lock-in;
  • online participation, misinformation, public voice, and civic attention.

The current platform-control checkpoint also includes formal data rights and opt-out routes: a regulatory order can create a path to request, delete, or limit some vehicle data without showing that an individual user completed the route, received the requested result, trusted the provider, or could switch.

See the cross-domain administrative-legibility layer for the shared middle between eligibility, identity matching, correction, and usable access.

5. Work, control, and bargaining

Core question: Who controls the work process, captures productivity gains, and absorbs the risk when tools, schedules, or firms change?

  • wages, hours, benefits, job quality, and pay volatility;
  • monitoring, algorithmic management, evaluation, and worker privacy;
  • AI adoption, task redesign, training, deskilling, and augmentation;
  • schedule control, job lock, career mobility, and professional identity;
  • worker voice, organizing, ownership, representation, and retaliation;
  • employer dependence, contractor status, platform access, and deactivation;
  • firm productivity versus worker security, health, dignity, and bargaining.

6. Care, health, and social reproduction

Core question: What work keeps people healthy and functioning, and who quietly supplies it when markets and public systems fall short?

  • medical access, coverage, prior authorization, and delayed or forgone care;
  • prescription costs, medical debt, and treatment continuity;
  • disability access, mental health, communication, and dignity;
  • child care, eldercare, kinship care, and care-worker supply;
  • multigenerational living, paid/unpaid substitution, and care as social infrastructure;
  • unpaid family labor, caregiver strain, sleep, earnings, and career effects;
  • food, housing, transport, and health as linked constraints;
  • care autonomy, recipient choice, provider power, and continuity of support.

7. Housing, place, and mobility

Core question: Can people remain safely in place, move when necessary, or find a viable place to belong?

  • rent, mortgage, ownership, repairs, eviction, and displacement;
  • insurance availability, premiums, exclusions, claims, and rebuilding;
  • energy, water, heat, cooling, transport, and safe living conditions;
  • disaster exposure, recovery, relocation, and return;
  • neighborhood change, gentrification, segregation, and belonging;
  • migration, commuting, service distance, and local opportunity;
  • staying, moving, or exiting as choice, adaptation, or loss of access.

8. Unequal exposure and status

Core question: Which groups face more risk, less room to adapt, weaker voice, or different treatment for the same formal rule?

  • race, ethnicity, nativity, citizenship, language, and cultural belonging;
  • class, wealth, income volatility, and ownership;
  • age, life stage, gender, family structure, and caregiving role;
  • disability, health status, digital access, and accommodation;
  • geography, rurality, neighborhood, climate, and institutional distance;
  • status stigma, discrimination, credibility, and documentation burden;
  • administrative legibility: identity matching, data errors, verification, correction, appeal, and the risk that a person becomes invisible or misclassified to the institution controlling access;
  • intersectional exposure, protected buffers, and unequal recovery.

9. Trust, identity, and cultural meaning

Core question: When conditions change, what do people believe the change means about dignity, fairness, belonging, responsibility, and the future?

  • institutional trust, procedural fairness, and perceived competence;
  • dignity, humiliation, deservingness, resentment, and gratitude;
  • family, religion, race, nation, class, region, and generational identity;
  • loneliness, social support, community institutions, and solidarity;
  • goodwill, expected reciprocity, delivered aid, helper burden, and usable local protection;
  • blame, grievance, threat, hope, aspiration, and cultural pessimism;
  • changing norms around work, consumption, privacy, care, and success;
  • meaning-making after loss, recovery, exclusion, recognition, or repair.

Religious institutions are a distinct social-infrastructure surface here: their perceived community and anti-poverty role can be high even when membership, participation, trust, and acceptance of political influence are uneven. Treat presence, perceived usefulness, actual service, belonging, and public authority as separate stages.

10. Public systems and policy feedback

Core question: How do rules become actual access, and how do people's experiences feed back into future demand, avoidance, or policy change?

  • eligibility, cliffs, take-up, churn, recertification, and administrative burden;
  • application, notice, identity proof, documentation, and wait time;
  • data matching, interoperability, false positives, correction, appeal, and whether a household can remain legible across agencies and program changes;
  • frontline discretion, contractor delivery, fraud controls, and surveillance;
  • enforcement, courts, regulation, public investment, and remedy design;
  • federalism, state variation, local capacity, and unequal implementation;
  • benefit receipt, adequacy, interruption, replacement, and durability;
  • poverty improvement versus coverage continuity, eligibility, renewal, and administrative churn;
  • learning, avoidance, trust, complaint, political demand, and policy feedback.

11. Political judgment and collective action

Core question: When does private pressure become blame, affiliation, participation, organization, protest, voting, or withdrawal?

  • personal versus national economic perceptions;
  • party identity, ideology, issue ownership, and motivated interpretation;
  • turnout, vote choice, contacting, organizing, protest, and lobbying;
  • unions, churches, mutual aid, neighborhood groups, and social movements;
  • media selection, narrative frames, misinformation, and agenda setting;
  • political efficacy, representation, compromise, and institutional legitimacy;
  • political silence, nonparticipation, exit, and why these are not equivalent.

12. Firm, sector, and market power

Core question: How do firms allocate gains, prices, risk, information, and voice across workers, customers, suppliers, and communities?

  • concentration, consolidation, mergers, and dependency;
  • pricing power, markups, shrinkflation, fees, and pass-through;
  • labor strategy, subcontracting, automation, and workplace control;
  • investment, innovation, patents, supply chains, and supplier dependence;
  • bankruptcy, restructuring, ownership, and who bears losses;
  • firm political influence, lobbying, regulation, and public subsidy;
  • company success versus customer welfare, worker security, and local vitality.

13. Infrastructure, technology, and dependency

Core question: Which systems make everyday life and national production possible, and who controls their capacity, failure, and replacement?

  • electricity, grid capacity, water, housing, transport, and logistics;
  • broadband, cloud, AI compute, communications, and digital public goods;
  • critical minerals, chips, energy inputs, pharmaceuticals, and components;
  • resilience, redundancy, maintenance, outages, repair, and recovery;
  • public-private ownership, procurement, standards, and accountability;
  • cost allocation, local burden, environmental exposure, and consent;
  • public hearings, institutional land control, route vetoes, and who bears the consequence after a route changes;
  • technical capacity versus realized service, affordability, and replaceability.

The current infrastructure checkpoint separates physical realization from lived distribution: a transmission project can be completed and energized while household bill incidence, reliability, equal access, and durable affordability remain unmeasured.

14. Geopolitical and state consequences

Core question: How do domestic social conditions and productive capacity change state legitimacy, strategic autonomy, alliances, and leverage?

  • industrial policy, defense production, procurement, and readiness;
  • trade, sanctions, supply-chain exposure, and critical dependencies;
  • energy security, migration, technology competition, and financial power;
  • alliance burden-sharing, foreign reception, and legitimacy abroad;
  • alliance reliability, public consent, and whether legitimacy produces an observable cooperative, market, diplomatic, or refusal response;
  • domestic affordability, inequality, and room for strategic action;
  • state capacity, emergency response, territorial control, and credibility;
  • layered authority: national, state, local, school, utility, and community actors may hold different powers to approve, block, modify, or absorb a project;
  • export controls, coercion, substitution, resilience, and external behavior.

The current state-response checkpoint separates official alliance posture from strategic effect: a new deployment, command role, or solidarity statement can be observed before deterrence, public consent, adversary adaptation, or durable alliance behavior is visible.

Administrative legibility is a bridge subtheme across status, public systems, consumer control, care access, and platform governance. It asks whether the affected person or institution can be recognized, corrected, and heard by the system that controls the next step.

Cross-cutting questions

  1. Control: who can decide, refuse, appeal, switch, or set the terms?
  2. Conversion: how do money, time, data, status, or access become power—or fail to become power?
  3. Visibility: whose burden, adaptation, unpaid work, or non-use is counted?
  4. Substitution: what replaces the lost option, and who pays for it?
  5. Reachability: does a formal benefit, right, payment, or capability reach the intended unit and remain usable?
  6. Voice versus veto: who can speak, who can formally block or alter the decision, and who still absorbs the local consequence?
  7. Meaning: is the experience interpreted as necessity, dignity, betrayal, fairness, efficacy, belonging, or opportunity?
  8. Distribution: who gains, who loses, who is protected, and who carries residual risk across households, firms, places, and states?
  9. Time: what is immediate, delayed, and persistent after the original event?
  10. Scale: how does a local or household episode connect to firm, sector, national, or geopolitical consequences?

Shared conversion stages

The subthemes can be read through one shared stage model without assuming that one dataset observes every stage:

  1. Exposure: a price, rule, shock, technology, institutional decision, or capability changes the conditions facing a unit.
  2. Route: the unit encounters an application, market, workplace, service, social network, political channel, or strategic option—and may not use it.
  3. Adaptation: money, time, care, status, safety, data, or bargaining room is traded, protected, delayed, or sacrificed.
  4. Response: a firm, employer, agency, court, community, state, or foreign actor acts, fails to act, or changes the terms.
  5. Usable result: the unit receives and can use income, care, access, safety, correction, capacity, or a credible alternative.
  6. Durability: the result is adequate, continuous, replaceable, and stable rather than merely announced or briefly observed.
  7. Meaning and action: the experience becomes trust, blame, belonging, organizing, voting, non-use, switching, exit, legitimacy, or changed external behavior—only where directly measured.

Themes 1–8 emphasize exposure, route, adaptation, and unequal distribution; themes 9–11 emphasize meaning, interpretation, and collective response; themes 12–14 emphasize control, capacity, replaceability, and state or external response. The same stage labels therefore connect the map while preserving the different units and evidence standards of households, people, firms, places, institutions, and states.

These additions reinforce the map's common question: which resource, source, or institution becomes usable for whom, through what route, and with what residual burden? They are evidence-specific answers, not a replacement for the open-middle tests below.

Shared stage bottleneck across the 14 themes

The 97-ledger portfolio makes the common missing middle measurable. The open-middle blocker audit finds 163 explicitly open arrows. The largest gap families are:

Missing stage Open-arrow mentions Plain-language question
Alternative, non-use, switching, or exit 122 What could the unit do instead, and did it?
Later outcome or durability 116 Did the result last, or did the problem return?
Trust, meaning, or action 104 Did the experience change interpretation or behavior?
Receipt or implementation 80 Did the affected unit actually get and use the response?
Money, time, or adequacy 73 What did it cost, and was the result enough?
Unit, denominator, eligibility, or attribution 66 Can the outcome be tied to the same unit and actor?

These counts overlap: one open arrow can be missing several stages. They are therefore routing diagnostics, not prevalence rates. The broad finding is that the atlas is not mainly short of topics; it is short of observed conversion from formal capacity or response into usable, durable, and interpretable lived outcomes. This is why the next pass should prioritize one stable unit with a real alternative and later follow-up, regardless of which of the 14 themes it enters through.

The political lane adds a distinct interpretation-to-action boundary. The ANES worry/trust/vote ledger places pre-election financial worry before federal-government trust and a reported presidential vote in the same panel surface. It observes a temporally ordered meaning/action bridge, but not a dated material shock, actor attribution, remedy, policy response, recovery, or exit. A vote is an action-stage observation, not proof that a material problem was solved.

Current depth priorities inside the broad map

The atlas remains a 14-theme program. Depth priorities are simply the places where one additional stable unit would change the most claims:

  1. Household pressure and care: follow a dated bill, health, or care event into alternatives, work/time trade-offs, later security, and persistence.
  2. Consumer remedy and practical exit: follow one customer or account from access and complaint through correction, receipt, adequacy, continued use, switching, or non-use.
  3. Work and control: follow one worker or workplace from notice or system exposure through understanding, refusal/appeal, pay or safety response, bargaining room, household effect, and later job or exit.
  4. Care and public-system usability: follow one person or appointment from need through travel, price/coverage, completed care, continuity, and unpaid substitution.
  5. Infrastructure and state consequence: follow one identifiable project, contract, or capability from approval through acceptance, operation, replaceability, local burden, and an observable external or public response.

These priorities are complementary, not a new single research question. A valid dataset or event ledger may advance one priority while the other themes remain separate. The promotion rule is unchanged: do not call a formal action an end-to-end result until the affected unit's lived outcome is observed.

The machine-readable version is the depth-priority register.
