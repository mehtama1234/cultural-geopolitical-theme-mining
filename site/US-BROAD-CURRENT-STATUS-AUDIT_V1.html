US broad program current-status audit v1

The newest water-infrastructure depth artifact is the water infrastructure, funding access, household service, and health audit. It joins NBER water-quality and mortality evidence, GAO rural and Tribal funding reviews, UN SDG6 global coverage and financing, Brazil water-security planning, Northern Ireland household routines, and bounded infrastructure discussion. The finding is a funding-to-service gap: capital, coverage, or construction does not automatically become continuous, affordable, maintained household service.

The newest displacement-and-belonging depth artifact is the displacement, host capacity, livelihood conversion, and belonging audit. It joins UNHCR global stocks and funding, World Bank/UNHCR/CEGA Kenya panel evidence, UNHCR Uganda operational outputs, IOM migration-development analysis, Ipsos/UNHCR attitudes, and bounded discussion. The finding is a protection-to- capacity gap: status, aid, training, land, and return counts do not automatically become durable livelihoods, host legitimacy, or genuine future choice.

The newest firm-and-place depth artifact is the local firm formation, survival, place capacity, and practical access audit. It joins NBER business-formation stages, small-firm support crowd-out, disaster loan causal evidence, Gallup local-business sentiment, New York Fed firm capacity, Census nonemployer service/cultural scale, World Bank size-stratified establishment capacity, Pew place-retention evidence, and bounded discussion. The finding is a formation-to-capacity gap: applications and firm survival do not automatically become durable local services, good jobs, household choice, or belonging; nonemployer operators also represent a distinct local-capacity route rather than simply failed scale.

The newest education-and-AI depth artifact is the AI in education, learning, teacher agency, and equity audit. It joins NBER K–12 school-capacity evidence, OECD task-performance and learning research, Gallup teacher-control data, Chicago randomized outreach trials, Google teen-use evidence, RAND teacher well-being and retention pressure, GAO equity-implementation evidence, UNESCO governance, and bounded discussion. The finding is a performance-to-learning and implementation-capacity gap: access and polished output can grow faster than independent learning, teacher control, teacher continuity, and equitable institutional capacity.

The newest credit-and-household-room depth artifact is the credit cost, score feedback, household room, and exit audit. It joins Philadelphia Fed borrowing-cost evidence, NBER randomized credit information and score-feedback studies, randomized U.S. medical-debt relief, linked Federal Reserve credit records, CFPB market data, New York Fed expectations, and bounded finance discussion. The finding is a conversion gap: information or debt relief can improve price, score, or limit while credit access, repayment strain, health, and safe household room move in different directions.

The newest AI-service depth artifact is the AI customer service, context, human fallback, and remedy audit. It joins an HBS/Management Science randomized field experiment with FTC refund administration, EU platform review, UK Ombudsman routing, CFPB Ombudsman redress-friction evidence, public platform-worker remedy records, and bounded discussion. The finding is a context-to-remedy gap: faster or more pleasant replies do not by themselves prove restored money, access, income, recurrence prevention, or voluntary exit; even formal U.S. redress can leave recipient-side reissue and administrator friction unobserved.

The newest geoeconomic depth artifact is the geoeconomic coercion, firm spillovers, household costs, and attribution audit. It joins NBER coercion theory and bilateral trade-risk evidence with Census firm-level export-control spillovers, a named BIS Applied Materials settlement, Hygon reported adaptation and growth, CRS retaliation chronology, NBER tariff pass-through, Ipsos household attribution, and bounded public discussion. The finding is a translation gap: pressure can alter trade, payroll, supply chains, prices, and enforcement while strategic capability, household incidence, and legitimacy remain separate outcomes. The NBER tariff synthesis adds a strategic counterpoint: targeted industrial policy may be justified by security externalities, while recent U.S. tariff incidence remains concentrated among domestic importers, intermediaries, and consumers and supply chains can reroute without eliminating indirect China exposure. This keeps security benefit, domestic cost, and strategic independence as separate outcomes.

The newest AI-health depth artifact is the AI health navigation, trust, control, and continuity audit. It joins Pew health-chatbot and health-information evidence with GAO wearables, Ipsos task-specific diagnostic trust, WHO lifecycle governance, PwC continuous health tracking, the FTC Hims & Hers digital-health privacy/billing case, the NBER Medicaid-renewal outreach experiment, and bounded LinkedIn discussion. The finding is a control gap: speed and low-cost access can expand first-step agency while disclosure, verification, human fallback, care continuity, privacy, and remedy remain separate and mostly unobserved.

The newest health-cost depth update is the health cost as household political economy synthesis. It now adds GAO and NBER No Surprises Act evidence: patient liability protection can coexist with provider-favorable arbitration, changed network participation, and possible premium effects. The finding is a protection-to-access gap: formal billing protection, provider bargaining, network availability, patient care, and household affordability remain separate stages.

The newest ACA consumer-chain update is the ACA underwriting and practical- option finding. It now joins pre-ACA underwriting heterogeneity, CMS 2026 Marketplace scale and nominal affordability, and NBER mortality evidence. The NBER paper reports no beneficial mortality effect from the Medicaid expansions in its design but does report lower mortality from the ACA net of Medicaid expansion. This advances the coverage-to-health chain while keeping the mechanism, individual care pathway, effect size in the public abstract, and household financial endpoint open.

The newest material-to-meaning/action depth artifact is the repeated-respondent material pressure, trust, and action rotation audit. It compares HTOPS, CCES, CES, ANES, and Pew longitudinal surfaces and shows that persistence, institutional judgment, policy demand, turnout, contact, protest, and generalized social trust are distinct endpoints. The evidence supports identity- and attribution-conditioned translation, not a universal hardship-to-action path; a dated event, responsible actor, usable alternative, remedy, and later recovery remain open.

The newest payment-and-remedy depth artifact is the digital payment adoption, fraud recovery, and practical-exit audit. It compares NBER outage research with OECD authority reports, Federal Reserve household fraud data, UK open-banking activity, Brazil's Pix payment diaries, and ASEAN scam-recovery evidence. The route shows that payment scale and preference can coexist with unresolved loss, weak fallback, and incomplete recourse; actual reimbursement, restored financial room, and switching remain unobserved.

The newest U.S. consumer-agency continuation is the payment, AI, credit, and household-shopping agency synthesis. It now adds the NBER remote-work shopping counterexample: online and weekday shopping, prices paid, deals, minutes, and household shopping responsibility can shift together without an AI recommendation system. The finding is a conditional-agency gap: AI discovery, payment choice, verification, household time, price, return, and repeat-use outcomes remain separate stages. The newest workplace-control depth artifact is the AI implementation, worker-control, and security audit. It separates task activity, shipped output, firm expectations, firm/function/task diffusion, worker adaptation, training returns, organizational design, autonomy, outside options, and early employment counterevidence. Its U.S.-bounded continuation adds Census time-saved/time-cost signals, NBER exposure versus adaptive-capacity estimates, Gallup manager support and displacement associations, U.S. firm-size training capacity, NBER outside-option leverage, a CPS comparison finding no statistically significant summer-2026 unemployment increase for recent college graduates relative to comparison groups, and the full NBER W35684 firm-level paper behind the featured summary: its AI-skilled employment measure and job-description-based organization-capital measure are associated with recent productivity growth, while the featured summary reports 6.2 percentage points greater sales-per-worker growth in 2018–2024 for a one-standard-deviation increase, not in the prior eight years. The current evidence supports a conversion gap: measurable AI use or productivity can arrive before worker discretion, bargaining power, durable earnings, consumer remedy, or household security are observed. It now adds two named U.S. episodes: an AI customer-service field experiment with 138 agents and 256,934 chats, and the OpenAI/Statsig DOJ recruitment settlement with potential back pay and three years of oversight. The older NBER U.S. firm-composition comparator adds a workforce-design stage: AI-investing firms were associated with more educated, STEM, IT, and junior workers and fewer middle-management and senior workers. This is organizational recomposition, not proof of better pay, progression, voice, or security. The NBER job-mobility study adds a worker-side security price: employed U.S. workers required an average 1.63% pay increase for each additional percentage point of annual unemployment risk in a new job. This is a stated preference, not observed switching or realized security, but it helps explain why firm productivity and worker acceptance can diverge. It now adds a third named consumer episode: the FTC's announced Cleo AI refund distribution of 2,124,796 PayPal payments totaling more than $15.8 million; because the announced payment date is October 27, 2026, distribution and consumer receipt remain separate stages. The NYC follow-up now adds an October 1 citywide AI enforcement policy and an October 5 employee-impact reporting proposal covering displacement, salary changes, and new training. These close additional public-accountability stages but not individual receipt, restored opportunity, tool-level exposure, durable worker/customer outcomes, or household security. It also records two further committee-stage proposals for a DCWP AI oversight office/complaint portal and civilian AI enforcement with possible complainant recovery shares; neither is enacted or operational in the reviewed public record. Comparative cases remain mechanism checks, not substitutes for U.S. industrial or consumer outcomes.

The newest housing-risk depth artifact is the property shock, repair, staying, and moving audit. It compares wildfire destruction, FEMA housing assistance, eviction orders, rental-assistance lotteries, California wildfire claims and smoke-damage correction, GAO insurance availability, NBER credit-pricing, mortgage lock-in, and housing robustness. The evidence shows that a payment, partial claim, repair route, legal standard, or formal housing intervention can improve one stage while safe occupancy, return, mobility, household security, and durable place attachment remain unknown. It now adds California's September 2026 smoke- damage and disaster-recovery legal corrections, which sharpen the implementation test without proving household-level repair or safe return.

The NBER flood-disclosure study adds a consumer-market sorting stage: extreme- risk homes sold at a 3.3% discount, remained listed about a week longer, and attracted buyers with 5.3% lower income, more FHA financing, and older ages. This shows information changing price and buyer composition, not insurance, repair, safe occupancy, or durable household protection.

The NBER household-level flood-risk study adds a counterexample: from 1999 to 2023, people exposed to flood risk increased but the share of households exposed remained stable, with essentially no net migration to risky parcels. It reports households in floodplains as less disadvantaged and increasingly protected overall, while a vulnerable low-income uninsured homeowner group remains. This sharpens rather than closes the chain: aggregate exposure, household composition, insurance coverage, and post-flood recovery are separate stages.

The HBS AI automation-acceptance survey adds a U.S. industrial/consumer acceptance stage: 2,357 respondents evaluated 940 occupations, supporting automation for roughly 30% with current AI and about 58% when advanced AI was stipulated to outperform humans at lower cost, while 94% favored augmentation. This shows a human-touch boundary around replacement, not actual deployment, worker displacement, customer behavior, or durable trust.

Checked: 2026-09-30 Scope: the full 14-theme societal, cultural, consumer, institutional, financial, political, firm, infrastructure, and geopolitical program

Program orientation: this is a broad cultural-social-consumer-political atlas, not a single health-cost, consumer-remedy, or AI-work study. The empirical core is U.S.-anchored, while comparative and global evidence is used to test capability, inequality, institutional capacity, dependence, state power, and geopolitical consequences. Those layers are kept separate: a global comparison is context or a mechanism test, not a U.S. household result; an observed domestic capability is not automatically external leverage. The program's central task is to map how conditions, rules, prices, technologies, and shocks move through money, time, access, control, status, culture, institutions, political action, firm strategy, infrastructure, and state power—and to mark the missing links rather than collapse them into one story.

The shared end-to-end model

condition / price / rule / technology / shock
  -> money, time, access, control, status, health, or security changes
  -> people, consumers, workers, firms, agencies, and places adapt
  -> the next actor responds and redistributes cost, risk, data, or power
  -> trust, identity, culture, public demand, political action, firm exposure,
     sector capacity, or geopolitical leverage changes

For every arrow, record the unit, date, geography, source, weight, method, uncertainty, and status: observed, reported, estimated, compared, inferred, or open. A cross-source pattern is useful context; it is not a same-person or same-firm join unless the identifiers and design support that claim.

What we should pursue next

  1. Execute the material/time/care linkage pass: acquire or identify a same-unit design with a dated bill, care need, or work change, alternatives and schedule control, paid/unpaid time response, and follow-up outcomes.
  2. Follow one dated consumer or public-program case from first contact through effort, response, remedy, abandonment, and later trust or exit.
  3. Use SHED panel variables that genuinely repeat to test whether adaptation reverses after financial improvement, and preserve the nonresponse limits.
  4. Add a matched place or firm comparison for ownership, local distribution, service quality, and practical replaceability.
  5. Build a longitudinal political instrument that measures exposure, attribution, source, identity, trust, consumer action, civic action, and vote separately.
  6. Seek counterexamples: households that recover without the predicted loss of trust, agencies that switch providers, firms whose gains diffuse locally, and places where infrastructure adds capability without dependence.

Stop conditions

  • Do not treat one household or one current dataset as the program.
  • Do not call a complaint response a remedy without a verified outcome.
  • Do not call consumer adaptation political action without measuring meaning and action.
  • Do not infer culture, polarization, or distrust from hardship alone.
  • Do not call planned infrastructure operational or sovereign.
  • Do not join separate surveys by matching geography and call them the same respondents.
