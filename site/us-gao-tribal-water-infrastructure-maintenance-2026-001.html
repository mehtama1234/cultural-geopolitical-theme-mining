Finding 001: Building tribal water infrastructure can leave eligibility and maintenance unresolved

Status: bounded tribal infrastructure, public-health, and institutional-capacity finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

GAO's September 2026 review says federal agencies help Tribes build drinking- water and wastewater infrastructure, but the route from construction to durable service has two gaps.

First, IHS interprets its authority to cover only certain homes and buildings. Homes owned by a spouse or grandparent who is not a tribal member, some homes rented to tribal members on leases shorter than five years, and some homes rented to nontribal community service providers can be excluded. Tribes and IHS staff then have to find other funding for those properties. IHS officials reported spending more than 80 hours helping one Tribe obtain an $8,000 grant and complete its reporting.

Second, after construction, Tribes are responsible for operation and maintenance. Limited finances and difficulty hiring and retaining certified water operators can cause systems to deteriorate or fail early. IHS generally does not fund routine O&M, and GAO says IHS has not assessed whether such funding could reduce federal replacement costs and improve tribal health. IHS estimated that about $6 billion was needed as of November 2025 for all tribal communities to have safe-water access.

The route is:

federal construction assistance
  -> property eligibility and multi-agency application
  -> project construction
  -> tribal operator, energy, and maintenance capacity
  -> functioning water/wastewater service [not established by construction]
  -> health and cost outcome [not measured here]

What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a tribal and infrastructure version of a recurring subtheme:

Public investment can create physical capacity while shifting the hardest costs—eligibility, paperwork, staffing, energy, and maintenance—to the local institution expected to keep the system alive.

It also shows that “access” is partly a legal and administrative question. Homes inside the same community can face different routes because of ownership, lease, or agency rules.

Limits and counterevidence

  • GAO reviewed agency data and documents, visited Alaska and Arizona, and interviewed Tribes and officials; it did not estimate a national rate of failed tribal systems.
  • The $6 billion figure is an IHS estimate of need, not a verified cost of closing every service gap or a health-benefit estimate.
  • The 80-hour/$8,000 example is a named administrative case, not an average.
  • Construction and maintenance problems do not mean that every federally supported system is failing.
  • Agencies already collaborate through task forces and joint projects; the finding is that processes and authority remain fragmented and costly.

The next decisive artifact is a named tribal system followed through property eligibility, construction, operator staffing, O&M spending, outages or service quality, household reach, and later health or replacement cost.
