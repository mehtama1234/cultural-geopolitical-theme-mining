A short argument · US prices, fees, and choice

The first price can shape the choice before the full price appears.

A shopper cannot compare a cost that is hidden until late in the purchase.

The argument

The opening number attracts attention and starts a commitment. If mandatory costs appear after time and effort have already been spent, the customer is no longer comparing equal offers.

The problem is often the timing of information, not the existence of a separate fee.

Follow the price

A traveler chooses a room by its opening price. A mandatory fee appears after dates and payment details are entered. Leaving is possible, but alternatives may now cost more or be gone. The first number helped create the commitment.

headline price → attention and commitment → full-price reveal → buy, switch, downgrade, abandon, or borrow

The important distinction

Keep four questions apart: was the fee mandatory, was it visible early, did it change the choice, and what was finally received? A changed purchase is not automatically a welfare gain or loss. The source-backed point is that late information changes the comparison.

The argument has limits

The experiments show changed behavior, not that every changed purchase is harmful or that disclosure alone improves quality, trust, or household finances.

Next test

Reading rule

Ask whether the full mandatory cost was clear before the customer committed time, attention, or money.
