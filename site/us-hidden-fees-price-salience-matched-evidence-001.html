Connected US finding · fees and customer choice

The first price shapes the choice before the full price appears

A shopper cannot compare a cost hidden until late in a purchase. The timing of information changes who enters the comparison and how much effort is already sunk.

The argument

A customer who sees the full mandatory price early can leave or compare. One who sees it late may accept a worse deal because backing out now costs time, a deadline, a trip, or a needed service.

Price display is part of the market, not decoration around it.

Follow the price

A traveler compares rooms by the opening price. After choosing dates and entering payment details, a mandatory fee appears. Alternatives may now cost more or be unavailable. The first price helped create a commitment.

opening price → attention and search → time and commitment spent → late fee or full price appears → stay, exit, or accept a worse comparison → final cost and trust change

The deeper finding

Keep four questions apart: was the fee mandatory, was it visible early, did it change the choice, and what was finally paid and received? Late information makes the customer pay a search cost before learning what is being compared.

What remains unknown

The experiments show that price display changes behavior. They do not show that disclosure alone improves quality, trust, or household finances, or what purchase the customer made instead.

Next test

Join first price, final price, fee timing, quality, cancellation, refund, and what the purchase displaced. Compare customers who saw full prices early with those who saw them late.

Reading rule

Do not call a fee hidden because it is separate. Ask whether the full mandatory cost was clear before the customer committed time, attention, or money.
