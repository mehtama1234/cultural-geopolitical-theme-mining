Finding 010: Eviction can become a school and homelessness shock for children

Status: bounded housing-to-child-outcome finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

A Census Center for Economic Studies/NBER study linked eviction court records to school, homelessness-service, and Census records in Chicago and New York. Because cases were assigned to judges with different eviction tendencies, the researchers could estimate the effect of an eviction order separately from some of the economic distress that led to court.

The study finds that eviction increased children's moves, homelessness, school absences, and school changes, and reduced high-school graduation. In the case year, eviction increased residential moves by about 13 percentage points. In the following year, the estimated homelessness increase was 7.0 percentage points in Cook County and 3.1 points in New York.

The route is:

rent or housing dispute
  -> eviction court and eviction order
  -> move, homelessness, doubled-up housing, or school disruption
  -> attendance, credits, graduation, and later mobility

This is a real housing-to-life bridge, not just a count of eviction filings. But it is a study of linked children in two cities, not a national claim about every renter.

Why it matters for the broader atlas

The finding shows how housing instability can cross into education and family life. The cost of eviction is not only rent debt or a changed address; it can also be lost school continuity, homelessness, and a longer educational path. Family support can moderate the damage, which makes social networks part of the practical housing system.

It also sharpens the program's central distinction: a household may appear in a court record before the later human consequences are visible. Administrative systems can identify the legal event and the school effect without showing which preventive aid, legal help, family support, or alternative housing would have prevented it.

Limits and counterexamples

  • The causal evidence comes from Chicago and New York, not the whole country.
  • The estimates apply to cases affected by judge assignment and may not fit every eviction or informal move.
  • Eviction order is not the same as every filing, informal displacement, or homelessness experience.
  • The study does not identify which policy would prevent the effects.
  • Census CES papers are working papers and do not represent an official Census Bureau position.

Next test

Link a named prevention route—legal aid, rental assistance, mediation, or school-based housing support—to eviction court, housing stability, school continuity, and later educational or household outcomes. Keep formal eviction, informal displacement, assistance receipt, and family support separate.
