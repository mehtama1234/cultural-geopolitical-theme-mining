When staying does not mean being satisfied

Short answer

People can keep using a service for different reasons. It may help, be hard to replace, hold needed records, or make leaving tiring. Continued use is therefore a weak measure of satisfaction.

The opening evidence points to a sharper test: a product can improve a quick answer while leaving a repeated or serious problem harder to solve. If a person cannot get a clear reply, human review, or a remedy, a private service problem can become a wider question about trust and public rules.

The customer’s real outcome may come after the pleasant part of the interaction. A fast message can reduce waiting, but the customer may still need a refund, a corrected record, a safe account, or a decision that can be appealed. A service can improve the first minute and worsen the whole case if it closes the conversation before the problem is fixed.

What the sources show

An HBS account of a customer-service field experiment reports that AI suggestions helped 138 meal-delivery support agents respond 22% faster across 256,934 chats and raised measured customer sentiment by 0.45 points on a five-point scale. The improvement was larger for less-experienced agents. The study found less improvement for repeat complaints and some confusion after very fast transfers from a chatbot to a person.

Separate research summarized by HBS reports engagement tactics in more than 37% of farewell conversations across six AI-chatbot platforms, with longer engagement afterward. Pew surveys show broad use of AI chatbots and a majority of adults reporting little or no control over AI's role in their lives. The FTC has asked companies about data use, safety, age limits, monetization, and negative effects of AI companions.

These sources do not prove that longer use is harmful, that a faster reply solves less, or that service problems change a person's political view.

The missing piece is the customer's next move. A person can stay because the service is useful, because the account holds needed records, because switching costs money, or because there is no clear route to a person. A survey answer or renewal can hide these different reasons.

Imagine a customer whose delivery is missing. The chatbot answers in seconds, then sends the customer back to the start when the refund does not arrive. The customer opens three more chats and keeps the app because the order history and payment record are there. Continued use now means dependence and unresolved loss, not satisfaction. Another customer gets the refund in one contact and leaves because the service is no longer needed. The same retention number can contain both stories.

The connection

The cost of leaving may be spread across the household. A parent may keep a delivery app because it stores a grocery list, a worker may keep a bank account because payroll is connected, and a patient may stay with a portal because its records are difficult to move. The service can be disliked and still be the least risky option. A clean exit requires a usable replacement, a way to carry the record, and confidence that the old account will not keep charging.

Time can make dependence grow quietly. A customer who has already learned the menus, stored payment details, and built a history may tolerate a small failure rather than start over. The firm may call that loyalty. The customer may call it the cost of switching. These are not the same thing and need separate questions.

product need or service problem
  -> reply, recommendation, delay, or exit barrier
  -> time spent and repeat contact
  -> answer, remedy, continued use, or inability to leave
  -> trust in the firm and the service
  -> demand for complaint, safety, or exit rules

The important split is between staying by choice and staying because the next option is costly, unclear, or unavailable.

That split changes how a company reads its own numbers. A high renewal rate may show value, habit, dependence, or a lack of alternatives. A high satisfaction score may reflect a pleasant first reply even when the refund, correction, or safety action never happened. A long conversation may show support, sales, companionship, or a failed attempt to leave. The measure has to follow the customer to the next concrete result.

The four outside views

Household: A person may keep an app because it helps with work, care, shopping, or company. The same person may spend more time because the answer did not arrive or because leaving risks losing data or access.

Company and sector: Faster first replies can lower cost and help simple cases. Firms still need to measure repeat contact, reversal, refund, human review, and whether a customer can leave cleanly.

The distinction is especially important for repeat complaints. A first contact can be handled well by a script. A second or third contact often means the first answer did not solve the problem. If the company counts each contact as a completed interaction, more automation can look like better service while the customer spends more time reaching a remedy.

The measurement should follow the case, not the conversation. “Answered,” “transferred,” and “closed” describe system events. They do not establish that the customer understood the answer, received the money, corrected the record, or could prevent the same problem. A short interaction can be good service, but only the later result tells us whether it was enough.

Money and finance: Time is a real household cost. It can become a missed work hour, a delayed claim, a lost refund, or another payment while the person tries to fix the first problem.

Public power: Complaint routes, data deletion, child safety, disclosure, and simple exit rules decide whether a customer has a usable remedy. Public concern should follow measured harm and failed remedy, not use alone.

This is how a private service problem can become a public issue. The customer first spends time, money, or trust trying to fix it. If the firm cannot correct the record or provide an exit, the customer may complain to a regulator, platform, employer, school, or elected official. That path is plausible, but these sources do not establish its size or political effect.

There is a useful counterpoint: a customer may prefer an automated route because it is private, available at night, or less stressful than explaining the problem to a person. Human contact is not automatically better. The relevant comparison is whether the chosen route solves the case, offers a real appeal, and leaves the customer free to continue or exit.

What would change the finding

  • The customer's first question, repeat contacts, wait time, and final resolution.
  • Whether a person could reach a human, appeal, get a refund, delete data, or leave.
  • Use frequency, paid conversion, records held, and reasons for continued use.
  • Time, money, work, care, health, or privacy costs after an unresolved case.
  • Differences by age, income, disability, language, loneliness, and digital access.
  • Complaints, enforcement, rule changes, and trust measured before and after the service event.
  • Whether the customer confirmed the final remedy, rather than the system simply marking the case closed.

Next test

The political meaning can appear when private exit fails repeatedly. A person may not want a new law after one bad chat, but may support complaint rights, data portability, refunds, or a human review rule after discovering that no private route works. That is a possible progression, not evidence from the current sources. To study it, the record must show the failed remedy before the public demand rather than infer it from dissatisfaction alone.

Follow the same customer from first contact through 30 days. Compare automated and human paths on answer quality, repeat contact, time, remedy, exit, and later use. Ask directly whether the person stayed because the service helped, because it held something they needed, or because leaving was difficult. Include simple requests, repeat complaints, refunds, safety reports, and cancellation attempts. Define success as a confirmed remedy or informed exit, not a closed chat, and record whether the customer could appeal without starting over.

Add an exit test: give customers a clear export of their records, a no-penalty cancellation path, and a human appeal option, then measure whether they leave, stay by choice, or continue because switching remains costly. Record repeat contacts, fees after cancellation, data retention, replacement-service cost, and the time needed to rebuild the account elsewhere.

Reading rule

Do not read continued use as approval. Do not read a friendly reply as a remedy. Measure whether the person got an answer, could challenge it, and could leave.
