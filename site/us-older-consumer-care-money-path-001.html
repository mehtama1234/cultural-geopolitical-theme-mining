When age hides different kinds of need

People over 65 are not one customer group. Health, money, mobility, living arrangement, digital access, and care need can differ more than age itself.

The argument

“Simple” is not the same as accessible. A service can remove steps for one person and remove control for another.

Do not use age as a shortcut for need.

Follow one purchase and the help after it

An older customer living alone buys a phone with an emergency feature. The device is easy to buy but hard to set up, so the work moves to a daughter. Another customer wants it for travel and does not want a support plan. A delivery service preserves independence only if it fits mobility, payment, and digital access.

age, health, wealth, and living situation → actual need and safe ability to pay → product design, service, and targeting → independence, care burden, or bad advice → household money and family decisions

The deeper finding

A customer may need less complexity and more control at the same time. The useful design question is whether the person can understand the choice, use the service, get help when wanted, and remain free to refuse it.

What remains unproven

The sources do not show whether need-based targeting improves outcomes without raising price, exposing private information, or steering customers to a costly product. They do not settle how family help changes control.

Next test

Follow older customers for two years across one product or care decision. Join health, mobility, living conditions, price, service, digital access, family help, care hours, complaints, refunds, money, and independence.

Reading rule

Do not call lower spending a problem or a success until health, money, choice, and independence are visible.
