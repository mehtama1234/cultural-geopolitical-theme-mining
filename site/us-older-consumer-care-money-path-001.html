Connected US finding · older customers and care

Age hides different kinds of need

People over 65 are not one customer group. Health, money, mobility, living arrangement, digital access, and care need can differ more than age itself.

The argument

One older customer may value simplicity and have money to pay. Another may need help with transport, vision, memory, or fraud protection and have little room for an error.

“Simple” is not the same as accessible.

Follow one purchase

An older customer living alone buys a phone with an emergency feature. It is easy to buy but hard to set up, so the work moves to a daughter. Another customer wants the same phone for travel and does not want a support plan.

If the customer cannot understand renewal or cancel the service, the simple product has created a new dependence.

age, health, wealth, and living situation → actual need and safe ability to pay → product design and targeting → independence, care burden, or bad advice → household money and family decisions

The deeper finding

The useful design question is whether the customer can understand the choice, use the service, get help when wanted, and remain free to refuse it. Family help may preserve access while reducing the customer’s control.

If a product assumes family help, it treats unpaid care as part of the service. Customers without nearby family then face a higher price or fewer workable options.

What remains unknown

The sources do not show whether need-based targeting improves outcomes without raising price, exposing private information, or steering customers to a costly product.

Next test

Follow older customers for two years across one product or care decision. Join health, mobility, living conditions, price, service, digital access, family help, care hours, complaints, refunds, money, and independence.

Reading rule

Do not use age as a shortcut for need. Do not call lower spending a problem or a success until health, money, choice, and independence are visible.
