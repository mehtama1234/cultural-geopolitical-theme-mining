When protection becomes part of the price of a home

A home can become harder to keep even when the mortgage payment has not changed. Disaster risk and credit can raise the insurance bill.

The argument

Insurance is often paid through mortgage escrow, so a premium increase can look like a sudden mortgage increase even when the loan’s interest rate and principal are unchanged. An owner may cut repairs, borrow, accept less coverage, or go without insurance.

A premium increase is not automatically a forced move, but it can make a home less secure and harder to finance.

Follow one renewal

An owner in a disaster-exposed area receives a renewal notice $300 higher per month. The owner can pay, reduce coverage, shop for another insurer, borrow, delay a repair, or sell. Shopping may fail if insurers have left. Less coverage lowers the bill while leaving the owner unable to rebuild after a loss.

disaster exposure or weaker credit record → higher insurance price → larger escrow payment or less coverage → repairs, borrowing, sale, or move choice → future housing and credit access

What the sources establish

EvidenceWhat it means
NBER uses more than 74 million mortgage-linked insurance observations and finds premiums rose sharply between 2020 and 2024.Protection became a larger household cost in the studied data.
The link between local disaster risk and premiums grew stronger; in the most exposed ZIP codes, higher premiums were linked to more than $40,000 less home-price growth.Insurance and property value can move together.
A newer NBER study using about 70 million policies reports credit scores affect homeowners-insurance premiums alongside disaster risk.The financial record may enter the protection price.
The Federal Reserve reports 6% of homeowners went without insurance, with higher shares in lower-income areas and among owners with income below $50,000.Coverage is harder to keep where money is tighter.

Sources: NBER disaster risk, NBER pricing protection, and Federal Reserve housing findings.

The deeper finding

Insurance is a condition of housing security, not just another bill. A home can remain occupied and still become less protected, less valuable, or harder to finance.

What remains unproven

The studies do not show how many owners lose coverage, miss escrow payments, cut repairs, sell, move, or borrow because of the increase. They do not follow one owner through premium, coverage, credit, property value, and housing outcome.

Next test

Join mortgage escrow, insurance, credit, property, and nonrenewal records. Compare owners with similar homes but different credit paths. Keep price, coverage, payment trouble, repairs, sale, and moving separate.

Reading rule

Do not call a premium increase a forced move. First show whether the owner paid, changed coverage, borrowed, sold, or stayed.
