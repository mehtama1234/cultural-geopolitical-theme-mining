GAO 2026 kinship families and intergenerational care-support layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-107658 Reference window: nationally representative Census and HHS data for 2019–2023; community visits and caregiver interviews; report published December 10, 2025 Status: family-structure, care, and public-support finding; not a causal estimate of kinship-care outcomes or program effectiveness

Why this update matters

Kinship care is a large form of social reproduction that often sits outside the most visible child-welfare pathway. Relatives or close family friends may raise children when parents cannot, preserving family, community, cultural, and educational connections while absorbing housing, childcare, health, legal, financial, and emotional work. The public system may recognize and support a kinship family differently depending on whether the child is formally in foster care and on state requirements.

This layer broadens the aging-care map in both directions. Older grandparents may become primary caregivers while facing disability or labor-force exit; children may have greater health or special-education needs; and the same household can simultaneously need elder-care, child-care, housing, income, and health support. It is a useful cultural and political trend because “family” is not only a value or identity category—it is also a variable-capacity care institution.

Recorded findings

Surface Result Boundary
Population scale About 2.4 million U.S. children lived in kinship families in 2023 The definition is children raised by relatives or close family friends because parents cannot care for them; it is not all multigenerational households
Economic exposure Kinship families were more likely than all families to live in poverty This is a descriptive comparison, not a causal effect of kinship care or a measure of every family’s resources
Caregiver structure Households were typically headed by grandparents, who were more likely than the general population to have a disability and be out of the labor force Grandparent headship does not identify the full caregiver network, work history, or care intensity
Child needs Children living with grandparents more often had physical or mental health issues and special-education needs The result does not establish whether the living arrangement caused, revealed, or responded to these needs
Foster-care split For every one child in foster care, about 18 kinship children were not in foster care Formal foster-care status determines access to some resources; the ratio is a population comparison, not a resource-need ratio
Unequal eligibility Foster-care kinship families may access financial and other resources if caregivers meet state requirements, while other kinship families may be ineligible Eligibility and actual receipt are separate; state rules and caregiver circumstances vary
Persistent challenges Affordable housing and childcare needs persisted for some kinship families after the pandemic; some officials and most of 16 interviewed caregivers also reported worsened cost-of-living or mental-health challenges Site visits covered three counties and three Tribes in Mississippi, New Mexico, and Oklahoma; interviews are not representative
Local resource variation Resources and supports varied across the six communities reviewed Six communities cannot describe the national service landscape or rank states
Federal role HHS leads awareness and collaboration efforts and administers funds through programs states may use to support kinship families Federal funding availability does not establish local awareness, eligibility, capacity, or successful support
Data method GAO used nationally representative Census/HHS data for characteristics and site visits/interviews for challenges and resources The national descriptive data and qualitative local evidence answer different questions and should not be pooled

The central mechanism

parental absence or inability to provide care
  -> relative/friend assumes child-rearing role
  -> household recomposes housing, income, work, school, health, and childcare
  -> family continuity and stability, but also hidden time/cost/capacity burden
  -> formal foster-care or non-foster route changes eligibility and support
  -> caregiver/child security, mobility, health, and later family decisions

Kinship care can be both a protective social institution and a transfer of responsibility. A grandparent may keep a child connected to family and school while leaving the labor force, drawing down savings, modifying housing, or coordinating special education and health care. A relative outside foster care may provide the same daily care as a foster caregiver but face different financial and service pathways. The formal category therefore changes the available menu even when the underlying care work looks similar.

The 18-to-1 foster/non-foster comparison is a particularly important boundary. It means the highly visible foster-care system is not the whole kinship-care population. Yet the ratio does not mean that non-foster families need the same intervention or that foster-care benefits would automatically fit them. The right comparison follows need, caregiver capacity, legal status, child health, housing, school, and service use together.

Kinship care also exposes the cultural meaning of family as an operational resource. Family connection can be a source of resilience, but relying on it can obscure whether the arrangement is chosen, safe, supported, and sustainable. Public narratives that celebrate family stepping up should be paired with measurement of who pays, who leaves work, who supplies transportation, and who can obtain respite or formal help.

What this changes in the broader trend map

This layer connects child welfare, aging, disability, poverty, housing, childcare, health, education, family culture, and public-program eligibility. It strengthens the atlas’s view that households are adaptive institutions but not unlimited buffers. A family can preserve belonging and continuity while also absorbing costs that the state, employer, school, or health system does not record as care delivery.

It complements the child-care work-adaptation layer and assisted-living financing layer. Child-care evidence measures household work responses; assisted living measures care-and-housing payment fragmentation; kinship evidence shows how family composition reallocates care across generations and legal statuses. These are linked mechanisms, not a pooled estimate of family burden.

What remains unproven

GAO does not estimate caregiver hours, out-of-pocket cost, housing moves, employment changes, school outcomes, health outcomes, placement stability, caregiver exhaustion, or later reunification/adoption. It does not show whether formal foster-care status improves outcomes, whether state supports reduce poverty, or which services are most effective.

The nationally representative data describe 2023 characteristics, while the challenge and resource evidence comes from selected communities and 16 caregiver interviews. “More likely to live in poverty” does not identify the counterfactual household path. Family, friend, foster, and informal caregiver categories can overlap operationally but should not be treated as identical.

Next test

Build a family-year care episode ledger linking:

  • child entry into kinship care, relationship, legal/foster status, duration, reunification or other exit, and caregiver network;
  • caregiver age, disability, health, employment, income, housing, savings, work-time change, and unpaid/paid care hours;
  • child health, special education, school continuity, childcare, transportation, and developmental or safety outcomes;
  • benefits, subsidy, foster payments, Medicaid/SNAP, housing support, respite, legal assistance, and actual receipt; and
  • community/provider capacity, state eligibility, caseworker contact, administrative friction, family belonging, stress, and later stability.

Compare foster and non-foster kinship routes only after conditioning on need, legal status, caregiver capacity, and place. Include grandparents with and without disability and households that preserve care without formal support. The strong result is not simply more family care; it is stable, safe, supported child-rearing that preserves relationships without making older or lower-income caregivers absorb an invisible and unsustainable second household.
