Finding 015: A new gathering place can support local startup formation where social infrastructure was missing

Status: bounded firm, place, and cultural-infrastructure finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

An NBER study uses U.S. business-registration data to compare census tracts that were scheduled to receive a Starbucks with comparable tracts where the planned café did not arrive. In neighborhoods without an existing coffee shop, tracts receiving Starbucks later saw about 9.1% to 22.5% more startups—roughly 2.9 to 6.4 additional firms per year—over the following seven years.

The effect was larger in an under-resourced-neighborhood partnership involving Starbucks and Magic Johnson. But where cafés already existed, the study found no significant entrepreneurship effect.

The route is:

new neighborhood gathering place
  -> possible networks, foot traffic, or growth signal
  -> more local startup registrations
  -> [open] firm survival, jobs, wages, customer benefit, and resident access

This is a measured place-to-firm connection. It does not mean that Starbucks created every startup, that the new firms lasted, or that existing residents benefited equally.

What this adds to the broader atlas

The cultural and economic point is that a “third place” can be part of local productive infrastructure. A café is not only a retail outlet: it can provide a place where people meet, exchange information, and notice a neighborhood as a place for business.

The counterexample matters just as much as the headline result. When a neighborhood already had cafés, adding Starbucks did not produce the same entrepreneurship increase. The relevant condition may therefore be missing social infrastructure, not simply the arrival of a large chain.

Limits and counterexamples

  • The public abstract does not provide the full sample, all effect sizes, standard errors, or the exact selection process for scheduled locations.
  • Startup registration is not the same as survival, jobs, wages, profitability, or owner-household security.
  • The study does not measure customer prices, coffee affordability, worker conditions, housing change, displacement, or who could use the café.
  • Starbucks entry may signal expected growth or bring customers rather than create networks directly.
  • The finding is about neighborhoods without existing cafés; it should not be generalized to every retail opening or every place with a coffee shop.

Next decisive test

The next useful evidence would join tract-level startup registrations to firm survival, employment, wages, founder characteristics, customer prices, housing change, and actual use of the gathering place. Until then, the safe claim is that a new third place can coincide with more local startup formation when a comparable social venue was previously absent.
