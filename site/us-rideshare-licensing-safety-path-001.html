Connected US finding · rideshare licensing and safety

When customer protection changes who can provide the ride

A license rule may protect riders, but its first effect may be on who can enter the work and how many rides are available.

The argument

A rule can raise entry costs, reduce supply, or improve checks without changing the ratings or driving measures researchers observe. “Licensed” is not a complete customer result.

Follow entry, supply, quality, fare, wait, and safety separately.

What the source establishes

Using trip-level Uber data, driver ratings, and telematics-based driving measures, NBER finds no consistent evidence that licensing improves consumer outcomes in the studied settings. In Houston, drivers entering after deregulation looked like previously licensed drivers on ratings and driving behavior.

The study does not measure fares, wait, worker income, entry costs, serious incidents, or every customer outcome. It covers two settings.

NBER Working Paper 35635

Follow the rule into the ride

licensing rule → entry cost, checks, and enforcement → driver pool and trip supply → fare, wait, route, rating, and behavior → safety, access, and worker income

Licensing may protect riders through checks not captured by ratings. Its effect may differ where enforcement is weak or other transport is scarce.

Keep these apart

  • Licensing and actual safety.
  • Driver entry and trip supply.
  • Ratings and serious incidents.
  • Fare and customer access.

Next test

Follow cities, drivers, and riders for one year around a licensing change. Join entry, fares, wait, cancellations, routes, incidents, worker income, and customer access.

Reading rule

Do not judge a rule from its purpose or entry count alone.
