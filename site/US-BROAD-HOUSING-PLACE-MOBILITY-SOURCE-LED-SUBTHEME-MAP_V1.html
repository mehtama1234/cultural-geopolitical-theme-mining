Housing, place, and mobility: source-led subtheme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: 191 current registry records carry the housing_place_mobility program-theme label. This map clusters recurrent structures in that material. It does not treat being in a dwelling, a completed court case, a move, or a neighborhood-quality measure as proof of durable security or wellbeing.

What the source material supports

The records repeatedly distinguish:

payment from housing stability; an avoided court order from affordability; staying from freely chosen staying; physical-risk reduction from an equal relocation; and a neighborhood association from the effect of assigning a child or family to that place.

1. Assistance can change the immediate rent-payment route without settling stability

The emergency rental-assistance record evaluates five lottery-assigned programs in four cities that distributed more than $200 million during the pandemic. Assistance increased rent payment and reduced reported eviction concern, with suggestive self-reported health gains, but had little average effect on housing stability or financial distress in that specific setting. Moratoria, expanded safety-net programs, and a softer rental market are part of the study's interpretation, not nuisances to erase.

Supported subtheme: rent payment, fear, health, displacement, and lasting financial room are separate stages; a payment intervention can move some without moving all.
Not established: current-program effects, durable affordability, landlord behavior, court access, subsequent rent, credit, work, or family outcomes.

1a. Disaster credit can prevent a financial-collapse endpoint without establishing complete place recovery

The emergency-disaster-credit record links Federal Disaster Loan applications after uninsured damage from federally declared disasters to credit records before and after the shock. Exploiting an approval discontinuity at a 40% debt-to-income ratio, the paper estimates that credit provision reduced bankruptcy filing likelihood 61% over the following three years. Additional quasi-experimental interest-rate variation supports a liquidity interpretation, and provision increased car purchases even three years after receipt.

Supported subtheme: disaster assistance can operate through a credit and liquidity route, where debt capacity changes whether a shock becomes a bankruptcy event and may later change a mobility-relevant durable purchase. Financial collapse, transportation asset acquisition, repaired housing, and durable place recovery are separate outcomes. Not established: the exact damaged asset, repair completion, insurance or other aid, repayment burden, missed essentials, job or care continuity, housing stability, health, or whether loans are better than grants or direct repair. The result applies to disaster-loan applicants, not people unable to apply or outside that program.

1b. Temporary shelter can create public-health and safety gains without a durable-housing result

The Los Angeles temporary-shelter record uses 2014–2019 daily site-level service data and winter-shelter availability shocks. In the study, reducing unsheltered homelessness significantly reduced crime and psychiatric emergency-room visits; entering shelter reduced short-run mortality. But the paper found no evidence temporary shelter reduced future homelessness more than street outreach or other non-shelter services. It does not disclose the full analytic denominator or effect sizes in the retained abstract.

Supported subtheme: shelter has separable public-good, health, and individual-housing stages. A short-run protection can benefit people entering shelter, nearby residents, and health systems while not establishing a durable housing route relative to alternatives. Not established: individual service adequacy, shelter access or selection, housing quality, later tenancy, resident consent, trust, political action, or a general shelter effect. Area crime and hospital outcomes are not a complete measure of any individual person's recovery.

1c. An assistance exit is a classified transition, not a housing-security outcome

The HUD HEARS exit-type record links two King County housing authorities' administrative exit records to wage, Medicaid, behavioral-health, homelessness-service, and HMIS records. Among 8,266 heads of household with classified nondeath exits, 13.5% were positive, 54.9% neutral, and 31.6% negative. In the linked individual sample, a homelessness or unstable-housing event within the following year was recorded for 3.1% after a positive exit, 14.5% after a neutral exit, and 25.4% after a negative exit. Negative exits had 2.10 times the adjusted odds of a behavioral-health crisis versus neutral exits; positive exits had lower emergency-department-use odds than negative exits. The wage gap is also visible, but the reported $2,000–$2,500 quarterly premium for positive-exit households does not make exit type a causal earnings intervention.

Supported subtheme: assistance exit reason/type → same linked person's following housing stability, health, behavioral-health, and wage surfaces. An aggregate count of program exits cannot be classified as either independence or failure without this later-outcome distinction. Not established: a causal effect of exit type, national prevalence, assistance adequacy, rent burden, exit alternatives, housing quality, later trust/action, or durable security after the one-year window. Existing health, income, housing-market, and administrative conditions can select people into the exit classifications and account for part or all of the observed gap.

2. Court procedure is a consequential part of housing security, not a side detail

The eviction-order record uses judge-assignment variation in Cook County and New York City. It finds that, after an already adverse pre-court trajectory, an eviction order added homelessness and hospital visits and reduced earnings, durable-goods consumption, and credit access in the first two years; longer-run indebtedness rose and credit scores fell. Reported housing and labor effects were driven by female and Black tenants in that study.

The separate tenant-representation record finds that New York City tenants gaining Universal Access counsel were less likely to receive possessory judgments or eviction warrants, be evicted, or face as large a money judgment; effects were larger in poorer places and places with more noncitizens. It does not follow affordability after court.

Supported subtheme: the legal route can determine whether an earlier rent or income shock becomes an added housing, health, work, and credit shock.
Not established: that counsel or avoiding an order ends arrears, prevents an informal move, or creates long-run security.

3. Mobility can be constrained by a housing-finance position even without displacement

The mortgage-lock-in record reports a 40% fall in existing-home sales between 2022 and 2024 and estimates a 25% causal reduction in borrower mobility under mortgage lock-in, with moves down the housing ladder disproportionately reduced. Its calibrated model projects higher aggregate house prices and rents relative to a no-lock-in counterfactual; those are model results, not observed household bills.

Supported subtheme: staying can be an economically induced outcome rather than a simple preference, and one household's constrained move can affect the ladder facing renters, buyers, sellers, and downsizers.
Not established: that a given stay is unwanted or harmful, or the effect on care, job access, repair, insurance, belonging, or wealth for any person.

4. Risk reduction and redevelopment can redistribute the gains from leaving a place

The flood-buyout record finds nationwide that households of color received at least 6% less buyout compensation relative to fair-market value than White households. It reports White participants moving to higher-quality neighborhoods relative to White nonparticipants, while Black participants moved to worse neighborhoods relative to Black nonparticipants. Those comparisons do not reveal each household's counterfactual options or total wellbeing.

The HOPE VI relocation record models large displacement welfare losses in Chicago; vouchers and counseling partly offset them, while redevelopment raised prices and reshaped neighborhood composition with gains concentrated among higher-income homeowners. Modelled welfare and citywide equilibrium gains are not observed resident recovery.

Supported subtheme: reducing physical risk or renewing a place does not determine who can convert departure into an improved destination.
Not established: a universal racial mechanism, complete resident recovery, or later health, school, work, safety, trust, and belonging outcomes.

5. Place is an opportunity system, not merely an address

The neighborhood-and-firms record finds that the between-neighborhood intergenerational-earnings relationship was about twice the within-neighborhood relationship after correcting parent earnings measurement error. In its model, neighborhood parent earnings explained over 80% of variation in unrestricted neighborhood effects, and children's establishment premiums were related to parents' and neighbors' establishment premiums. These are linked-data relationships, not randomized relocation effects.

The NHTS mobility record adds a descriptive daily-access layer: in 2022, 35.23% of urban households in the under-$35,000 income band had zero vehicles, versus 3.54% in the $75,000+ band; mean recorded shopping-trip time was 34.01 versus 20.71 minutes. A travel-day record cannot reveal all unmet trips, fares, cancellations, or care substitutions.

Supported subtheme: local opportunity is jointly organized through family, neighbors, establishments, vehicle access, distance, and time—not a single “good neighborhood” score.
Not established: a causal effect of a move, an explanation of racial earnings gaps, or whether recorded travel time is voluntary, bearable, or unmet need.

5a. Opportunity can be underpriced when housing markets cannot finance or coordinate a child's future gains

The neighborhood opportunity and missing-markets record reviews U.S.-centered experimental and quasi-experimental evidence that childhood neighborhood exposure can affect later earnings and other long-run socioeconomic outcomes. It identifies several mechanisms by which housing markets can undersupply opportunity: families cannot fully borrow against children's future earnings, neighbor spillovers are not fully priced, households face behavioral/search frictions, and income sorting can persist. The review points to customized mobility services paired with vouchers and to mixed-income place-based policy as different response routes.

Supported subtheme: a household's location decision can reflect a missing market for a child's longer-run opportunity, not merely current rent or stated preference. Price, search, subsidy, move, school/community exposure, and adult outcome remain distinct stages; mobility policy and place-based policy should not be treated as interchangeable interventions.

Not established: one pooled effect size, a common family denominator, whether every move or voucher succeeds, same-family search and lease-up, transport/care/network costs, immediate stability, or a universal effect by race, disability, local market, or cohort. This is a review across studies and settings, not one new experiment.

5b. Redevelopment can improve children's later opportunity without raising current adults' earnings

The HOPE VI redevelopment and child-outcomes record evaluates 262 distressed public-housing developments receiving $17 billion in revitalization. Its matched difference-in-differences, mover-exposure, and sibling-comparison designs report lower neighborhood poverty after revitalization and no detected causal earnings impact for adults living in public-housing units. Children raised in revitalized units had higher adult earnings and college attendance and lower incarceration; the study reports increased interaction with surrounding-neighborhood peers as a mechanism surface, with larger earnings gains for subgroups exposed to higher-income peers.

Supported subtheme: redevelopment, changing neighborhood composition, current-adult earnings, children's long-run opportunity, and peer interaction are separable outcomes. A place intervention can produce an intergenerational gain without making a universal adult-income or resident-prosperity claim.
Not established: resident retention or displacement, housing quality, rents, service access, adult wellbeing, complete community prosperity, a universal redevelopment effect, or whether peer interaction alone produced the child outcomes. The public working-paper material does not disclose the full site-selection or subgroup-result ledger.

6. Insurability can become a stay-or-move constraint before a claim is ever filed

The GAO homeowners-insurance record finds a modest 3% real national premium increase from 2019–2024, while high wind risk was associated with 58% higher premiums relative to medium wind risk and some southern coastal areas saw 25%+ inflation-adjusted increases. National means and modeled premiums are not household bills, coverage adequacy, or availability.

The California FAIR Plan record reports policies grew from 242,440 in 2021 to 642,010 in 2025 and about 696,562 by June 2026; insured exposure grew faster still. A residual-market policy is not proof of an individual private-market denial, affordability, adequate coverage, or housing displacement.

The wildfire-claims record reports 41,727 claims and $25.2 billion paid to date after the Eaton and Palisades fires, alongside violations in a selected State Farm review and a new smoke-damage legal response. Partial payment, law enactment, repair, safe return, renewal, and stable recovery are separate stages.

The wildfire damage and return record adds a linked property/income/occupancy pathway across more than 100 fires. Occupants of destroyed homes had lower income for three years, with cumulative losses equal to 26% of pre-fire annual income; four years after a fire, half of destroyed addresses remained unoccupied, and reoccupied addresses housed substantially higher-income residents than before. The paper found no income or employment effect for residents without direct property damage. It does not identify insurance, aid, rebuilding, debt, health, or why particular households returned or did not return.

Supported subtheme: climate and insurance pressure reaches household place through premium, availability, residual-market, claim, repair, and renewal routes; direct damage can also produce a distinct income-and-community composition path. Neither an aggregate payment nor a policy count establishes the ability to remain safely housed.
Not established: property-level affordability, coverage adequacy, repair, health, safe occupancy, move/stay decision, return mechanism, or later security.

7. A voucher is usable only after a private-market search converts it to a lease

The voucher lease-up record uses Housing Choice Voucher administrative search events. It reports successful lease-up for about 65% of searches in 2018–2020 and about 58% in 2021–2022, with search time increasing. Success and duration varied substantially by public-housing agency: rural counties generally had lower success, while high-rent counties had higher success rates but longer searches. The data do not reveal individual search effort, landlord refusal, unit quality, rent paid, or later continuity.

Supported subtheme: nominal assistance has a practical conversion stage— search and landlord-market clearance—whose time burden and success vary by place.
Not established: why a particular search failed, whether a lease supplied affordable or suitable housing, or later stability, mobility, trust, or wellbeing.

7a. Nominal affordability shrinks further when a unit must also be vacant and adequate

The HUD affordability, availability, and adequacy record adds a national rental-stock constraint that precedes any individual search. In 2023, HUD reports 65.8 rental units affordable to extremely low-income renters per 100 such renter households. The ratio falls to 38.4 units when availability is required and to 33.7 when physical adequacy is also required. For very low-income renters, the affordable-available-adequate ratio was 52.0 per 100; the affordable-and-available ratio ranged from 45 in the West to 74 in the Midwest.

Supported subtheme: the housing route begins before a voucher-holder or other renter applies. Listed affordability, vacancy/availability, and physical adequacy are separate supply conditions, so a nominally affordable unit count overstates the pool that could plausibly enter a household's search.

Not established: a particular household's chance of leasing, local submarket conditions, search time, landlord screening, accessibility, transportation fit, moving costs, rent actually paid, safe occupancy, stay or move, or later housing stability. A stock ratio is not a household outcome.

8. Court-ordered eviction has a linked child housing-to-school pathway

The eviction-and-children record links court, school, homelessness-service, and restricted Census records in Chicago and New York, using judge assignment as an instrument. An eviction order increased address changes by 13 percentage points in the case year—about doubling the non-evicted mean—with effects persisting through two following academic years. It increased homelessness by 7.0 percentage points in Cook County and 3.1 points in New York after filing, increased absences and school changes, and reduced high-school graduation; elementary and middle-school test scores showed little impact. The estimates apply to cases marginal to judicial leniency in the linked two-city samples.

Supported subtheme: an eviction decision can propagate through residential movement, homelessness services, attendance, school continuity, and later education; court outcomes are not only adult housing events.
Not established: a national effect, informal displacement effects, the mechanism in every family, or which intervention prevents the later harms.

9. Credit scoring can enter the cost of staying protected from disaster

The home-insurance credit record studies about 70 million mortgage-linked policies matched to property risk. It finds low-credit-score homeowners paid about 24% more than high-score owners for identical coverage—an insurance-price contribution about as large as disaster risk in the study. Washington State's credit-information restriction substantially weakened that price relationship. The paper does not follow individual renewal, escrow, deductible, coverage, claim, sale, or move paths.

Supported subtheme: physical exposure and a household's financial record can jointly shape the recurring cost of keeping a home protected, before a disaster claim occurs.
Not established: that a premium change forced a coverage cut or move, or that a pricing restriction reduces total housing risk or improves recovery.

10. A positive intergenerational housing association can disappear under causal tests

The parental-homeownership record links childhood American Housing Survey data to adult tax outcomes. Growing up with parent ownership was associated with roughly $2,000 higher adult income and a 6-percentage-point higher chance of adult ownership. But after tests for less-correlated unobservables and sibling comparisons, the study found no evidence of causal effects. It is a Census CES working paper, and its null is specific to its linkage, measures, and identification tests.

Supported subtheme: tenure can mark inherited resources, stability, neighborhood, and family selection rather than isolate the causal engine of later adult outcomes.
Not established: that housing conditions never matter for children, that all ownership effects are null, or that later ownership equals wellbeing.

10a. Property-tax design can reallocate modeled housing access across generations without proving a reform outcome

The property-tax and generational-allocation record uses an overlapping-generations model rather than an observed California reform. In its California-to-Texas tax-rate counterfactual, the property-tax rate rises from 0.8% to 2%; modeled house prices fall 12.6%, modeled homeownership among adults age 75 and older falls 7.6 percentage points, and modeled homeownership rises among adults age 25–34. The model attributes the shift to lower capitalized prices and downpayments for constrained younger buyers alongside higher holding costs for older owners. A separate modeled step-up-basis change also lowers elderly homeownership.

Supported subtheme: tax design can create an intergenerational housing allocation tradeoff through price capitalization, downpayment constraints, and holding costs. Younger buyer access, older-owner security, price movement, and local fiscal capacity are separate outputs.

Not established: an observed California policy effect, individual displacement or wealth change, rental-market adjustment, exemptions, neighborhood or service effects, political feasibility, or distributional fairness. All stated counterfactual effects depend on model assumptions about supply, tax incidence, financial constraints, demographics, and bequests.

10b. Housing wealth can change women's labor supply through a childcare-linked route

The housing-wealth and women's labor-supply record uses geographically linked SIPP and housing-market data with a homeowner exposure instrument. The working paper estimates a 0.098-percentage-point decrease in women's labor-force participation for each 1% increase in housing wealth, with no statistically significant effect for men. The negative effect is strongest among mothers of young children and among women reporting child-related reasons for not working; the paper reports that income effects, precautionary saving, and liquidity constraints do not fully explain it.

Supported subtheme: housing wealth can enter labor supply through a gendered care and human-capital route, rather than simply expanding household security. A change in homeowner balance-sheet room can coincide with a change in paid-work participation without revealing whether that change is chosen, constrained, or welfare-improving.
Not established: childcare availability, unpaid-care hours, job quality, household income adequacy, later security, applicability to renters or all housing markets, or political meaning. This is an instrument-defined homeowner-margin estimate in a working paper, not a general account of women's employment choices.

11. Place-based tax investment can be completed without showing resident improvement

The Opportunity Zone record reports $108 billion in Qualified Opportunity Fund assets across nearly 9,000 tracts, covering roughly 10% of the U.S. population. States and selected funds generally did not track incentive-specific jobs, housing, or other outcomes; among 24 states aware of investment, 14 reported effects on jobs/housing, while most were unsure of overall effects. Of 16 selected funds followed up, 12 had completed all planned projects. Development completion and program scale do not measure affordability, displacement, resident benefit, or counterfactual investment.

Supported subtheme: place investment has separate capital, project, operation, and resident-outcome stages; completion is meaningful realization but not proof of community improvement.
Not established: national causal effects, job quality, affordable housing, displacement, tax incidence, or durable security.

12. Housing-assistance entry and exit mark changing work trajectories, not a simple welfare effect

The HUD assistance and work-trajectory record links HUD and LEHD records. Among first-time entrants, earnings fell about $112 per year in the prior four years and rose about $174 annually afterward, with a $286 post-entry slope change; eventual leavers' earnings/employment grew before exit and then flattened. The paper compares entrants with low-income ACS renters and leavers with HUD stayers, but calls the patterns suggestive rather than causal. It does not measure rent burden, quality, location, care, job quality, or household wellbeing.

Supported subtheme: housing assistance and labor-market trajectories are linked over long horizons, while entry and exit can reflect changing need, opportunity, loss, or household circumstances—not one program effect.
Not established: that assistance caused work improvement or exit caused decline, or that earnings translated into durable housing security.

13. New residents can expand local demand while local housing and public capacity remain separate questions

The immigration, demand, and place record keeps two NBER source units separate. An older local-market study estimates about 1.2 local jobs per immigrant, 62% in non-traded services, while a recent national model of the 2021–24 surge finds supply and demand effects roughly offset for inflation. Neither source observes current local rents, crowding, school/health capacity, transport, wage quality, or belonging.

Supported subtheme: population change can expand workers and customers at the same time; national inflation and local demand results cannot determine a particular place's housing, service, or social-capacity outcome.
Not established: current local housing effects, public-capacity pressure, resident experience, or political response.

14. The measured local shock determines the measured mobility story

The local demand-shock record harmonizes commuting-zone shock measures and finds they are generally weakly correlated. Trade-related shocks showed little employment or migration response, while broader Bartik-style shocks showed economically meaningful changes. The working paper does not give complete formulas or prove which measure captures a single true event; weak migration can coexist with commuting, sector switching, or household adaptation.

Supported subtheme: place-based employment and movement claims are measurement-sensitive; “the local shock” is not one interchangeable exposure.

Not established: a universal migration response, individual motives, household welfare, or causal mechanism for every shock definition.

Cross-subtheme finding and next test

rent, rate, hazard, redevelopment, local-access condition, population change, or family housing history
  -> payment, application, search, court, insurance, investment, move, or daily trip route
  -> staying, displacement, destination, or foregone trip
  -> debt, credit, work, health, care, school, neighborhood, and social ties
  -> security, belonging, opportunity, and later institutional judgment

The corpus has credible evidence at several early and middle stages, but only some linked sequences. The decisive next evidence joins a household's prior dwelling and constraint; aid, court, insurance, buyout, or move process; destination and housing quality; travel and service access; debt and credit; work, care, health, and school continuity; and subsequent security and belonging. It must retain non-movers and unsuccessful applicants, not only completed moves or cases.
