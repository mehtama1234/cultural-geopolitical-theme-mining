A short argument · US spending data, households, and measurement

The data can show groups, but not yet the same family over time

BLS data can compare groups, but separate averages are not a family history.

The argument

The useful work is to build a careful low-income renter, higher-income renter, low-income owner, and higher-income owner comparison without pretending that a table follows the same person.

Follow the basket

A published table shows average spending for a group. It cannot tell us whether one renter's rent rose, which food was cut, or what happened after a missed bill. The dimensions exist, but they must be joined honestly.

income group + housing tenure → average spending pattern → price categories and real wage → possible household pressure → choice or trust

BLS CE tables · BLS CE microdata

The deeper finding

A demographic table can identify where to look, not what every family lived. The next layer needs the product, size, quality, rent, income, and time of the same consumer unit where possible.

What remains unproven

The public files do not yet create a true panel following one consumer unit through all years or connect its spending to trust and voting.

Next test

Use permitted CE files to build four groups and label every cell observed, estimated, suppressed, or unavailable. Add income, tenure, rent, food, transport, health, debt, and savings.

Reading rule

Do not call a demographic table a household panel. State whether the number describes a group, a consumer unit, or the same consumer unit over time.
