Connected US finding · household basket and evidence

The data can show groups, but not yet the same family over time

The Consumer Expenditure Survey can compare spending groups. It cannot by itself tell the story of one renter’s basket, substitution, debt, and later political response.

The argument

The hard problem is not finding more categories. It is joining them without making a group average pretend to be a family’s lived sequence.

A clean table is not permission to invent the missing household story.

Follow four households

Tables can show broad differences between renters and owners or low- and high-income groups. They cannot tell us whether a low-income renter paid more for transport because of a long commute, whether a higher food bill reflects better quality, or whether a household changed its basket after a job loss.

income group + housing tenure → average spending pattern → price categories and real pay → possible pressure → substitution, debt, trust, or political response

Only the first two arrows are supplied by the basic tables. The rest require linked data.

The deeper finding

An average is useful when it answers a bounded question. It becomes misleading when used to narrate a family. Renters can spend less on a category because they are poorer, or because the landlord pays it. The table does not settle the cause.

If spending is connected to trust or voting, preserve the distance between observed spending, inferred pressure, and later response.

What remains unknown

The public tables do not show the same consumer unit’s income, tenure, purchases, debt, savings, substitutions, stress, trust, and vote over time.

Next test

Obtain permitted BLS files and produce a reproducible table for low-income renters, higher-income renters, low-income owners, and higher-income owners. Mark every cell observed, estimated, suppressed, or unavailable.
