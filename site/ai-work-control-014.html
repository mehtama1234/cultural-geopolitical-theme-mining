Finding 014: Infrastructure can build capability and dependence at once

Status: provisional
Last checked: 2026-09-11
Project: AI, work, and control

Finding

Data centres, cloud migration, and public financing can give a country more digital capacity while also tying it to particular operators, contracts, equipment, and power systems. The important question is not whether infrastructure is good or bad. It is whether the project creates local skills, data control, provider choice, and public oversight at the same time as it creates a physical asset.

What we directly know

  • The World Bank Group says many countries lack connectivity and reliable power, compute and secure cloud, quality local data, and skills. It says pilots often fail to scale when these foundations and rules are not coordinated. World Bank AI readiness
  • In Romania, the World Bank reports that the government is migrating up to 60 applications—including electronic payment, public procurement, and early-warning systems—to the cloud, supported by a new regulatory and policy framework. World Bank AI readiness
  • In Malaysia, the World Bank reports that IFC helped de-risk early financing for a 300 MW, 72.5-acre data-centre campus in Johor Bahru, later acquired by Vantage Data Centers. The Bank says the campus is expected to attract investment, create local jobs, strengthen data sovereignty, and produce regional spillovers. World Bank AI readiness
  • Romania's Authority for Digitalization describes a PNRR-funded cloud-migration project with a target of at least 30 public applications migrated by June 2026 and an eligible value of about €177.05 million. Romanian Authority for Digitalization
  • IFC's project disclosure records up to US$150 million for a 96 MW greenfield Johor data centre through Yondr's wholly owned Malaysian project vehicle. IFC project disclosure
  • IFC and six international lenders announced more than US$900 million for the first 98 MW phase of the Johor campus. Yondr later announced the completed sale of the campus to Vantage, and Vantage linked the acquisition to a US$1.6 billion APAC investment led by GIC and ADIA. IFC financing · Yondr sale · Vantage investment
  • The World Bank says readiness depends on synchronizing supply, demand, and rules: data centres and connectivity; government cloud migration and enterprise uptake; and data protection, interoperability, and cybersecurity. World Bank AI readiness
  • The IEA reports that data-centre expansion faces grid connection delays, equipment and mineral supply constraints, and energy-security risks. IEA energy security

The Romania and Malaysia records are project accounts. “Expected” jobs, sovereignty, and spillovers are not yet measured outcomes.

Causal chain under test

public finance or policy support
  -> physical data centre, cloud migration, or connectivity
  -> public and private users adopt the service
  -> local data, skills, jobs, and applications grow
  -> provider choice, public capacity, and bargaining power change
  -> costs, energy use, and dependence are distributed across firms, households, and states

Every arrow needs a separate check. Building the asset does not prove that local capability or bargaining power increased.

These cases are not a ranking. Romania is a public-service migration case; Malaysia is a large physical-infrastructure case.

Cultural and social meaning

Infrastructure becomes part of daily life when it carries payments, procurement, alerts, health, education, work, or business data. A data centre can be described as an investment while the public experiences it through service reliability, bills, privacy, local jobs, land, water, and the ability to contest a decision. “Data sovereignty” has meaning only if institutions and people can exercise control, not merely if a facility is located inside national borders.

Geopolitical meaning

Development finance can act as a bridge between public goals and private infrastructure. That can widen access and reduce entry costs. It can also make a provider, long-term contract, power system, or foreign equipment chain important to public services. The geopolitical question is therefore about replaceability:

who finances it -> who owns it -> who operates it -> who supplies power and equipment
-> who holds the data -> who can switch -> who bears the interruption

This is an inference from the project records, not proof that either Romania or Malaysia is coerced or unable to exit.

Strongest challenge

External operators may deliver reliable services at lower cost than a locally built alternative. Foreign capital and expertise may create jobs and skills that would not otherwise exist. Public commitments can be a way to create a market, not a loss of sovereignty. The test must compare service quality, cost, local learning, and exit options against realistic alternatives.

What would change our mind

  • Large infrastructure projects consistently create local skills, data control, provider choice, and public bargaining power without special safeguards.
  • Long-term contracts do not affect prices, switching, data access, or public policy when the service becomes important.
  • Project ownership and equipment concentration do not predict who captures jobs, revenues, or strategic leverage.
  • Smaller or locally adapted systems deliver no better access, resilience, or accountability than concentrated facilities.

Next tests

  1. Obtain the Romanian migration contracts, data-location terms, service levels, and portability rules.
  2. Verify Malaysia’s realized jobs, energy and water use, tax and revenue effects, local suppliers, and ownership structure.
  3. Map operators, cloud providers, chip and power-equipment suppliers, lenders, insurers, and public anchor clients.
  4. Compare public-cloud migration with interoperable or locally operated alternatives.
  5. Test whether local workers and firms gain reusable skills and bargaining power.
  6. Add a case where infrastructure failed, was delayed, or was replaced.
