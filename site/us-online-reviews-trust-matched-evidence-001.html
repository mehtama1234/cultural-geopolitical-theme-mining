Reviews can reveal quality and distort choice at the same time

The argument

Reviews are a substitute for examining a product or meeting a seller. That makes them valuable, but also makes the review system part of the sale. The platform decides which reviews count, where they appear, how they are ranked, and what sellers can do when a review hurts them.

The deeper issue is not whether reviews are real or fake in the abstract. It is whether the review system helps a customer predict the result of a purchase. A reward can bring more feedback and may help good sellers become visible. The same reward, ranking rule, or removal process can also make the visible signal different from the underlying experience.

The signal has several parts that are easy to collapse into one star score: who bought the item, when it was used, what problem the reviewer had, whether the review was rewarded, and whether the seller answered. A five-star rating can mean the item worked once, arrived quickly, or met a low expectation. A two-star review can describe a shipping mistake that the seller fixed. The useful question is not “what is the average score?” but “what outcome does this review predict for this buyer?”

Follow one buying decision

A customer compares two similar products. One has a higher rating and appears first. The customer cannot inspect either item, so the rating becomes a shortcut. After delivery, the item is poor quality and the return is costly. The customer leaves a review, but its visibility depends on the platform. The next buyer sees a signal shaped by the first buyer’s experience and by the platform’s rules.

The first buyer's ability to review is unequal. A customer may spend an hour writing a detailed account, take photographs, or contact support. Another may return the item and move on because time is scarce. A buyer with a small loss may not bother to report it; a buyer with a serious loss may not have the energy to navigate the process. Review averages can therefore overrepresent people who have time, strong feelings, or a reason to remain engaged.

Sellers respond to the visible signal. They may improve quality, ask satisfied customers for feedback, challenge a review, offer a replacement, or spend more on placement. Those responses can improve the product or make the public record less complete. If a seller resolves a problem privately, the next buyer may see a high rating but not know how often the problem occurred. A review system can reward service recovery while hiding the need for recovery.

The platform's ranking rule decides which evidence has force. Recent reviews may reflect a changed product, but they can also drown out a long record. Verified-purchase labels may reduce one kind of fraud while excluding legitimate gift or used-item experiences. Text can reveal a pattern that stars hide, but only if the customer can find and read it. Design choices turn scattered experiences into a market signal.

review incentive and ranking rule → visible signal → belief about quality
→ purchase and seller effort → return, complaint, repeat purchase, or exit
→ trust and competition change

What the sources actually establish

Step Evidence Meaning
Information Reviews help customers judge sellers they cannot inspect directly. The signal can reduce search effort.
Incentive NBER finds rewarded feedback produced nearly 30% higher sales and was linked to higher-quality sellers in the studied setting. More feedback can change which sellers are seen.
Certificate NBER’s eBay study finds that a certificate redesign changed which sellers were certified. A platform label can change market position.
Manipulation FTC rules prohibit fake reviews, certain paid sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, deceptive review sites, and some review suppression. The public rule treats the signal as economically important.
Customer result FTC advises shoppers to check patterns, detail, timing, and incentives rather than rely on stars alone. A rating needs context to guide a decision.

Sources: NBER review rewards, NBER eBay quality certificates, FTC final rule, and FTC review guidance.

The deeper finding

Review volume and customer trust are not the same thing. A platform may increase the amount of feedback while leaving customers unable to tell which feedback predicts quality. The connected outcome is seller competition after purchase: who gains visibility, who can recover from a bad product, and whether a customer can make a mistake without bearing the full cost.

There is a feedback loop between reviews and access. A seller with early positive feedback becomes easier to find, sells more, and gets more chances to collect feedback. A new seller may offer a good product but remain invisible because it lacks history. A bad early review can also make later sales too few to recover, even if the seller fixes the defect. Reputation is therefore partly a record of quality and partly a record of who received the first opportunities to be seen.

This makes reviews a form of private regulation. They can warn buyers, discipline sellers, and help a platform sort millions of listings. But the platform also controls the rulebook and may benefit from more transactions, not only better matches. Its interests can diverge from the customer when a complaint reduces conversion or when a review exposes a repeat defect. Public rules against fake reviews matter, but the larger question is whether the system makes accurate negative information usable before the purchase.

Trust is restored by what happens after the mistake. A customer may accept one bad product if the return is simple, the refund is fast, and the seller is reachable. Another may lose trust after a small problem if the platform denies the claim or removes the review. The review is therefore both a quality signal and a record of the platform's response. A marketplace that measures only purchase conversion misses the cost of unresolved errors.

What remains unproven

The studies support effects on sales, seller labels, and review rules. They do not follow the same US customer from review exposure to product quality, return, complaint, and later trust. They also do not show how ranking and removal affect different income groups or people with less time to compare.

They also do not show whether more reviews improve prediction or merely increase confidence. A customer can feel informed and still choose a poor product if the ranking favors volume, if negative reviews are hard to find, or if the product changed after the earlier reviews. We need actual delivery quality, return friction, and later choice—not only clicks, sales, or reported trust.

Next test

Link review exposure to purchase, delivery, return, complaint, refund, and repeat choice. Keep the reviewer’s purchase status, incentive, history, edits, removal, appeal, seller ad spending, price, and actual product outcome.

Test the same listing with different review displays: stars first, recent detailed reviews first, verified-use outcomes, or a clear record of resolved and unresolved complaints. Follow buyers who saw each display and compare prediction error, return cost, time spent, seller response, and repeat purchase. Audit removed and edited reviews, not just those still visible. The useful result is whether the system helps a buyer avoid a bad match or recover cheaply when the prediction fails.

Reading rule

Do not treat review volume or star rating as quality. Ask who wrote the review, what the platform showed, what the seller did, and what happened to the customer afterward.
