AI capability is distributed through adoption conditions, not exposure alone

Status: provisional cross-source synthesis; worker-to-household and local-capacity joins remain open
Checked: 2026-09-13
Project: AI, work, and control

The finding

The program now has evidence at several different levels: worker-task adoption from NBER, firm and state-industry outcomes from BEA and BIS, modeled wage and wealth distribution from IMF, capability conditions from the World Bank, and public analytical capacity from OFR. Taken together, these sources do not produce one “AI effect.” They support a narrower proposition:

AI capability is distributed through the conditions of adoption—task fit, access, learning, capital, data, infrastructure, institutional capacity, and replaceability—not through occupational exposure or model ownership alone.

This is a structured synthesis, not a pooled estimate. The sources use distinct populations, periods, units, outcomes, and methods.

What the sources establish

Source Unit and method Direct contribution Boundary
NBER W35677 13,920 employed RPS respondents; 55,634 worker-task observations; weighted survey and task indexes Adoption is widespread but shallow; exposure explains only part of adoption variation; similar work contains different adopters No pay, autonomy, bargaining, or household outcome
BEA WP2026-18 75,292 Gallup worker observations linked to state-industry outcomes Frequent worker-reported use is descriptively associated with output and employment paths Non-causal; employer-only specification differs; no incidence
BEA WP2026-8 1,586 industry-year observations across 61 industries AI-intensive industry patterns include price, labor-share, and capital-share movement Industry accounting is not consumer pass-through or worker power
BIS firm study More than 12,000 EU/US firms; matched firm data Adoption is associated with short-run labor-productivity gains and no short-run employment reduction in that design Firm result does not establish durable job quality or distribution
IMF AI Adoption and Inequality Calibrated household/task model, 2014–2048 scenario Modeled wage Gini can fall while wealth Gini rises under firm-choice adoption Scenario, not realized US inequality; ownership and politics are assumed/open
World Bank WDR 2026 Comparative development framework and case evidence Adopt/adapt/advance requires skills, data, infrastructure, capital, and institutions; concentration creates dependency risks Framework does not identify a particular US outcome
OFR FY2025 report Federal institutional self-report AI/data infrastructure expansion coexists with staffing, budget, and platform changes No causal AI productivity, service, or stability estimate

The mechanism

technical exposure or provider availability
  -> access, task fit, training, capital, data, and institutional permission
  -> worker/firm/agency adoption
  -> productivity, time, price, labor-share, or service-capacity change
  -> who receives the gain and who bears error, cost, or dependence
  -> ability to inspect, correct, switch, bargain, or regulate
  -> practical capability, legitimacy, and state/market power

NBER directly adds the worker-level middle step that exposure indexes miss. BEA and BIS add partial firm and industry outcomes. IMF models one possible distributional pathway. World Bank identifies the complement and dependency conditions. OFR shows why public capacity must be measured alongside tools and data. The final incidence, legitimacy, and geopolitical power arrows remain open.

Why this is culturally and politically important

The public category “AI-exposed worker” can conceal unequal experience. Two people doing similar work may have different access, training, permission, learning time, or responsibility for checking errors. A firm can report productivity while workers receive more monitoring rather than more discretion. A government can acquire a powerful system while losing the staff or replaceability needed to contest it. A locality can gain tax revenue and technical capacity while bearing electricity, water, fiscal, or legitimacy costs.

These are mechanisms to test, not conclusions imported from the frameworks. The cultural object is the changing meaning of competence, responsibility, security, and fairness. The political object is who can define the metric, control the system, correct a decision, and exit a dependency.

Contradictions that must remain visible

  1. NBER finds shallow adoption within similar work, while broad exposure scores are often treated as if they identify worker use.
  2. BIS finds productivity gains without short-run employment loss, while BEA finds labor/capital composition movement in industry accounts.
  3. BEA worker-reported utilization and employer-reported utilization do not produce identical output patterns.
  4. IMF modeling permits lower wage inequality and higher wealth inequality at the same time.
  5. World Bank emphasizes local complements and dependency, while external tools can also expand capability by lowering entry costs.
  6. OFR reports new analytical/data capability alongside lower staffing and budget, so “efficiency” cannot be read as a simple capacity increase.

Counterinterpretations

  • Adoption differences may reflect task fit, quality, privacy, or preference, not unequal power.
  • Capital returns may fund new investment or broad wage gains rather than concentration.
  • Imported tools can be more reliable and more contestable than local systems.
  • Public-sector staffing reductions may reflect mission reprioritization rather than automation.
  • Industry price or output associations may arise from demand, management, intangible capital, or selection rather than AI.

What would change the finding

It would weaken if linked US evidence showed that occupational exposure reliably predicts individual adoption after access and workplace rules are measured, that productivity gains consistently reach labor and households across firm sizes, and that provider or infrastructure dependence does not alter inspection, switching, or public accountability. It would strengthen if adoption remained uneven within comparable work and gains accrued disproportionately to owners or high-capacity institutions while costs and correction burdens were shifted to workers, households, or local governments.

Next empirical program

  1. Reproduce NBER’s public task indexes and obtain subgroup-capable RPS data or compatible worker microdata.
  2. Join worker adoption to training, monitoring, autonomy, mobility, pay, and voice using a common worker or workplace unit.
  3. Pair BEA/BIS outcomes with firm size, ownership, labor share, job flows, and consumer-facing prices.
  4. Measure public-system capacity through staffing, workload, review, error, correction, data coverage, procurement, and switching indicators.
  5. Extend local infrastructure cases to electricity cost, water, revenue, service burden, ownership, and resident meaning/action.
  6. Compare US and international cases by practical alternatives and replaceability, not model nationality alone.
