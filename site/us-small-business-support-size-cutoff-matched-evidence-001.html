A short argument · US public demand, small firms, and local choice

A wider small-business rule can push the smallest firms out

When a public program lets larger firms qualify as small, it may reach more firms while giving less room to the smallest ones.

The argument

The real question is what the program is trying to buy: low price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, innovation, or resilience.

Follow one changed cutoff

A government raises a size limit. A larger firm now qualifies and wins a contract. A small local firm loses the work, delays hiring, and closes. Customers may get reliable delivery, or lose a nearby supplier. “More firms qualify” does not tell us the result.

eligibility rule changes → larger firms gain public demand → smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival → wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change

The deeper finding

Eligibility is a demand-allocation rule. It decides which firms get the first chance to grow, and that can alter jobs, prices, service, innovation, and local ownership.

What remains unproven

The study does not show the exact program, contract, customer, worker, or owner household behind each effect, or whether scale offset small-firm losses.

Next test

Follow one changed program for two years. Identify firms that gained and lost contracts, then measure prices, quality, delivery, pay, hours, innovation, owner cash, and local alternatives.

Reading rule

Do not call a program fair because more firms qualify. Ask which firms gained, which lost demand, and what customers and workers received.
