When a wider small-business rule crowds out the smallest firms

A public program can reach more eligible firms while leaving less room for the very smallest ones. The eligible list gets wider, but opportunity may become more concentrated.

The argument

The right measure depends on what the program is buying: low price, reliable delivery, local ownership, new firms, good jobs, innovation, or resilience.

Public reach and public value are different measures.

Follow one changed cutoff

A government raises a size limit. A larger firm qualifies and wins a contract. A small local supplier loses the work, delays hiring, and closes. Customers may get reliable delivery—or lose a nearby repair, supplier, or specialist. The public contract has changed the local market even if the immediate purchase succeeds.

eligibility rule changes → larger firms gain public demand → smallest firms lose revenue, investment, or survival → wages, innovation, customer choice, and local ownership change

The deeper finding

Eligibility is a demand-allocation rule. It decides which firms get the first chance to grow, and that can alter jobs, prices, service, innovation, and local ownership. A program can serve more eligible firms while making it harder for new firms to become competitors.

What remains unproven

The study does not show the exact program, contract, customer, worker, or owner household behind each effect. It does not establish whether larger-firm scale or reliability offset small-firm losses, or how the result differs by industry and place.

Next test

Follow one changed program for two years. Identify firms that gained and lost contracts, then measure prices, quality, delivery, worker pay and hours, innovation, owner cash, customer travel, and local alternatives.

Reading rule

Do not call a program fair because more firms qualify. Ask who gained demand and what customers and workers received.
