Finding 002: Firm recovery and customer welfare can diverge by neighborhood

Status: case-specific consumer-welfare evidence · Checked: 2026-09-17

The bounded finding

The authors' Hurricane Harvey study uses payment-card transactions and a consumer-demand model across 12 large store categories. It reports that about 1% of observed firms permanently exited and another 3% temporarily closed for at least two months. After major closures, affected cardholders traveled about 15% farther to shop; same-chain trips rose about 50% in distance.

Entry was not a simple replacement: entrants contributed more modeled consumer surplus than exiting stores, but entry did not fully replace exits in the hardest-hit neighborhoods. The authors estimate average Houston-MSA welfare losses around 0.29% of pre-storm retail spending, with much larger losses in the most affected tracts. Their price appendix reports no meaningful medium- or long-term price effect beyond a short-lived landfall-week spike.

Event chain

Hurricane Harvey flooding
  -> store closure and local firm turnover
  -> consumers travel farther or substitute to other stores
  -> average MSA welfare loss can look small while tract-level losses are large
  -> [open] income-specific burden, time sacrifice, worker effects, public
         services, political attribution, and durable place change

Policy implication Targeted aid can differ from subsidizing every damaged firm in the model Actual aid receipt, public trust, political action, and long-term local capacity

This is the customer-side complement to the disaster-liquidity firm record. It shows why employment, revenue, or firm survival cannot stand in for customer access: even with no lasting aggregate price movement, closure can impose time and distance costs on a subset of neighborhoods. It also supplies a counterpoint to a blanket “keep every firm open” rule: replacement quality and location matter, not only the number of surviving firms.

Interpretation rule

no long-run price effect != no consumer burden
entrant surplus     != entrant accessibility
average MSA loss    != equal neighborhood experience
store closure       != household hardship for every customer
targeted aid model  != observed policy receipt or legitimacy

The payment-card design is valuable because it observes realized shopping movement, but card coverage and model-based surplus limit the claim. Cash users, non-retail services, people who stopped shopping, and households without a vehicle may be underrepresented or differently represented.

Next decisive test

Align store closure and flood exposure with customer income, vehicle access, trip purpose, prices, and missed purchases; then connect firm aid or insurance receipt to worker retention and owner-household recovery. A small tract-level transaction summary or lawful replication table is sufficient; do not acquire the raw payment-card panel by default.
