Connected US finding · privacy, service and public trust

When privacy protection increases the data exchange

A privacy rule can make people more willing to share, which may improve a service while giving a firm a richer picture of them.

In one sentence

Feeling protected can increase sharing, but sharing more is not the same as being protected more.

Keep trust, consent, data use, customer value, and firm power separate.

What the source shows

Users shared more after privacy rules took effect

An HBS analysis of nearly 16,000 receipt-and-reward app users reports that users in California and Virginia submitted 9% more information after privacy rules took effect, including 1.5 additional receipts per month, 5% more unique store visits, and 4% more retail categories.

The study covers one platform. It does not show whether users read the notice, understood later uses, received better service, or gained equal bargaining power.

HBS More Trust, More Data

The connection

privacy rule and clearer permission → person feels more protected → more receipts, visits, or categories shared → firm gains a richer customer record → offers, sorting, pricing, or exposure may change → trust, choice, and power move together

More sharing can be useful when the purpose is clear and the reward is real. Consent is not the same as equal bargaining power.

The four outside views

Household: A customer may share because the service feels safer or the reward matters. The person may not know how long data stay, who receives them, or whether sharing can be undone.

Company and sector: A richer record can improve targeting, fraud checks, product design, or service, while also supporting higher prices, exclusion, or an unexpected use.

Money and finance: Data may be payment for a discount. Count value received, time spent, privacy risk, price, and any later cost of being sorted.

Public power: Privacy rules set permission, access, deletion, notice, and enforcement. A long notice does not create equal power by itself.

What would change the finding

  • Notice comprehension, consent, deletion, retention, sale, and later use.
  • Receipts, location, health, financial, or identity data shared.
  • Offers, prices, service quality, fraud outcomes, and complaints.
  • Results by age, income, race, disability, language, and digital access.
  • Whether sharing stayed higher after a breach or unwanted use.
  • Which firms gained the data and whether customers could refuse or switch.

Next test

Follow users for one year before and after a privacy rule. Vary the notice, permission, reward, and deletion controls, then measure what people understand, share, receive, and can stop.

Reading rule

More sharing is not proof that privacy improved or failed. Ask what the person knew, what the firm did, and whether the customer could say no.
