When the first price is not the final price

A shopper often decides using the number easiest to see: the headline price, monthly payment, reward, or “free” claim. The full cost may arrive later or be spread across shoppers.

The argument

Price display and payment design are part of the product. The useful test is whether the customer can see the final cost, understand the trade, and choose another route before the charge becomes hard to reverse.

A fee is not automatically unfair; an unseen cost is harder to judge.

What the sources establish

NBER clickstream evidence finds that hiding price information makes comparison harder and leads consumers to spend more. Another NBER study estimates that interchange fees transfer about $30 billion a year from cash and debit users to credit-card users, depending on how merchants pass costs into prices. NBER payment-card data also show more cancellations when replacement cards force subscribers to actively renew.

These are separate mechanisms, not one tracked household result.

Follow the displayed price

headline price, reward, or monthly charge → limited comparison and attention → fee, shared price, or renewal appears later → purchase, cancellation, or payment method changes → different households carry different costs

A card reward may be real value and a fee may fund a service. The customer still needs the final price and a workable choice.

Keep these apart

  • First price and final price.
  • Direct fee and cost spread through prices.
  • Convenience and affordability.
  • Did not cancel and could not cancel.
