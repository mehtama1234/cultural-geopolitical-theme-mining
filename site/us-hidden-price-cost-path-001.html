Connected US finding · price design and household cost

When the first price is not the final price

A shopper often decides with the headline price, monthly payment, reward, or free-delivery promise. The full cost may arrive later or be spread across everyone.

The argument

Price display and payment design change what customers can compare, how quickly they decide, and which households carry the cost.

A customer needs the final price before the choice becomes hard to reverse.

Follow one ordinary purchase

A shopper sees a low monthly price. Taxes and a service fee appear at checkout. The shopper pays with a rewards card because the reward is visible and cash feels less convenient. Months later the service renews without a fresh decision. The shopper may still value it; another customer may have forgotten it. The merchant has priced card acceptance into goods sold to everyone.

headline price, reward, or monthly charge → limited comparison and attention → fee, shared price, or renewal appears later → purchase, cancellation, or payment method changes → different households carry different effective costs

What the sources establish

EvidenceWhat it means
NBER clickstream evidence finds hiding price information makes comparison harder and leads consumers to spend more than otherwise.What is hidden can change a purchase before the customer weighs the full cost.
An NBER study estimates interchange fees transfer about $30 billion each year from cash and debit users to credit-card users.A reward system can redistribute cost through general prices.
NBER payment-card data across ten products show a sharp rise in cancellations when replacement cards force subscribers to actively renew.Inattention can keep a service alive when an active decision would end it.
The subscription study estimates inattention raises seller revenue by 14% to more than 200% versus immediate cancellation after value falls below price.The possible effect is large, but it is a model range, not a loss for every household.
A reward may provide real value, a fee may fund a real service, and a non-canceling customer may still want the product.Design evidence does not by itself prove unfairness.

Sources: NBER price information, NBER payment fees, and NBER subscriptions.

The deeper finding

The first price shapes the decision; the final price shapes the household. A customer with cash room may absorb a fee or forget a renewal. A customer near the edge may skip the purchase or carry debt. The hidden issue is unequal ability to wait, compare, cancel, and choose.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not follow one household across hidden information, payment choice, renewal, and later financial stress. It does not show whether disclosure changes behavior for long or how firms respond.

Next test

Choose one everyday fee or recurring service. Record the first and final price, payment method, reward, renewal, cancellation steps, use, and later household outcome. Test an all-in price shown at the beginning.

Reading rule

The first price is not the final price. “Did not cancel” is not the same as “did not value.”
