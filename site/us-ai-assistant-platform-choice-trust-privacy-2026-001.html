Finding 001: AI trust and privacy action are shaped by use, task fit, and knowledge—not concern alone

Status: bounded U.S. consumer-AI platform-choice finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

Jennifer Zou’s June 2026 survey and embedded choice experiment covers 1,999 U.S. adult AI-assistant users, with estimates weighted using external adoption benchmarks. ChatGPT was the primary assistant for 58.2% of respondents and Gemini for 25.4%; smaller platforms occupied task niches, including a much larger Claude share for coding than its overall share. About 43% reported routing different tasks to more than one assistant.

Trust was not simply a reputation judgment. Claude ranked above ChatGPT and Gemini in the reported paired comparisons, and Claude trust was substantially higher among its users than among aware non-users. Privacy concern was common, but knowledge appeared more closely associated with protective-tool use than concern in the study’s bounded regression. The choice experiment also found that respondents valued avoiding human review more than several other data-use features, with higher stated value for more sensitive tasks.

The safe interpretation is:

Consumer AI choice is a task-specific and experiential form of agency. People can be highly concerned about data use yet lack the knowledge, price tolerance, or alternatives needed to convert concern into protection.

Evidence chain

AI task and platform exposure
  -> primary choice, multihoming, and task routing
  -> use-based trust and privacy knowledge
  -> protective tools or hypothetical privacy valuation
  -> [open] actual settings, durable switching, exposure reduction, wellbeing, and power

What it adds to the broader atlas

This adds a current U.S. platform-choice mechanism to the exit and privacy lanes. It distinguishes nominal platform plurality from task-level replaceability and separates concern, knowledge, protective behavior, and stated willingness to pay. It complements the account-exit and California DROP records without treating those different control surfaces as one user history.

What the source does not show

  • The study does not provide platform telemetry or verify reported task routing, privacy settings, or data handling.
  • Cross-sectional trust differences do not establish that use caused trust or that knowledge caused protective action.
  • Hypothetical willingness to pay is not a purchase, a provider change, or reduced exposure.
  • The sample and weighting do not establish effects for non-users, workers, children, or all U.S. households, nor do they measure wellbeing or durable switching.

Next decisive test

Link task-level provider use to verified settings, privacy requests, provider switching, price and service trade-offs, and repeated outcomes over time. Test whether policy explanations or portable controls change actual protective behavior, retention, and practical exit for users with different task needs.
