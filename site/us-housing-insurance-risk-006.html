Finding 006: A housing voucher can exist while the private-market search remains the practical gate to using it

Status: bounded housing-assistance and place-mobility finding · Checked: 2026-09-21

The bounded finding

HUD's summary of a 2025 NYU Furman Center study shows that receiving a Housing Choice Voucher is not the same as successfully using it. The study estimates that about 65% of voucher searches ended in a lease-up from 2018 through 2020, but the rate fell to about 58% in 2021 and 2022. Search times also increased.

The practical route is therefore:

voucher issuance
  -> private-market housing search
  -> landlord, price, place, transportation, and paperwork constraints
  -> lease-up or failed/unfinished search
  -> [open] affordability, housing quality, continuity, stay/move, and security

The place pattern matters. Rural counties generally had lower success rates. High-rent counties had higher success rates but longer searches. A higher lease-up rate therefore does not automatically mean an easier, cheaper, safer, or more durable housing outcome.

What this adds to the broad atlas

This is a clean example of the program's central interface rule:

A public benefit can be real while the private market still decides whether the benefit becomes usable protection.

The missing work is not another count of vouchers issued. It is the search middle: how long the household searched, which units were reachable, what landlords or agencies required, what transport or care constraints applied, what rent and quality resulted, and whether the household stayed housed.

The finding also connects housing to time and unequal optionality. A household with more time, transportation, information, cash for applications or moving, and fewer care constraints may be better able to convert the same voucher into a lease. The public study does not measure those mechanisms, so this remains a testable interpretation rather than a causal estimate.

Limits and next test

  • The rates are administrative estimates of search events, not a survey of all eligible households.
  • The public summary does not identify each household's search effort, landlord refusals, notice timing, disability, care, or transportation constraints.
  • Lease-up does not establish rent affordability, unit quality, neighborhood opportunity, continuity, or later housing stability.
  • Rural and high-rent comparisons are descriptive; they do not explain the causes of place variation.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected voucher search ledger joining voucher issuance, search contacts, landlord response, application and denial, travel/time cost, inspection, rent and subsidy terms, lease-up, unit quality, and later stay/move or recertification. Until that exists, the safe claim is that voucher usability is partly a private-market search and time problem.
