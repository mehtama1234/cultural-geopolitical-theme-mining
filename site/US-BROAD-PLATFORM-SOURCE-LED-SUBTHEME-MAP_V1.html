Platform, data, and attention: source-led subtheme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: 87 current registry records carry the platforms_data_attention program-theme label. This is a first evidence-led map of the recurring structures inside that set, not a claim that all platform users share one experience or that the sources establish one causal chain.

What the source material supports

The source material does not support a generic claim that platforms are either empowering or harmful. It supports a more specific recurring pattern:

A digital service can make a route, choice, or correction more visible while leaving the consumer's actual control, recovery, substitution, and durable exit unresolved.

That pattern appears across distinct subthemes below. They involve different people, products, periods, and methods. They are deliberately not pooled.

1. Privacy rights are becoming observable administrative routes, not yet verified control

California's DROP record documents a one-request route across more than 600 brokers, about 325,000 reported participants before mandatory processing and about 500,000 later registrants. It preserves broker-level outcome categories and a 45-day processing requirement, but the agency does not publish a person-level match, deletion, exemption, re-collection, or residual-exposure rate.

The Walmart CCPA record shows why administrative completion must remain a separate stage. The company reported 3,199 deletion requests in 2025, with 1,962 completed and 1,237 denied, and a 22-day median completion time. Those are request rows rather than unique people or verified data-removal outcomes.

The later DROP status-contestability record shows a measurable implementation layer without closing the privacy-control question. The agency reported about 500,000 registrants, 99.9% with a profile deleted by at least one broker, and more than 40 brokers for the typical user; it also reported 654 brokers in the system and about 25% as having processed deletion requests. These agency-reported aggregates do not reveal the consumer-broker status matrix, all-broker deletion, valid exemptions, re-collection, or a durable reduction in exposure.

Supported subtheme: privacy is becoming a contestability and status-tracking problem as well as a disclosure problem.
Not established: that a completed status removes all relevant data, stops later collection, produces trust, makes switching unnecessary, or supplies complete practical control.

2. The consumer price of a platform includes time, attention, and renewal friction

In two Facebook and Instagram field experiments, the time-intensive consumption record reports low demand elasticity to experimentally changed ad load and explicitly models substitution across online and offline activity. This makes attention and opportunity cost part of the consumer-price question; it does not measure sleep, care, work, wellbeing, or whether users have a practical substitute.

The Amazon Prime redress record shows the remedial side of recurring-service friction. By September 2026, the FTC-filed order reported about $845.2 million issued but about $235.5 million accepted across automatic and claims phases. Aggregate issuance and acceptance are not a customer-level recovery rate, and the record does not observe later use, switching, or remaining loss.

Supported subtheme: a low-cash or automatic platform transaction can still create a material cost through attention, renewal, claiming, and recovery effort.
Not established: the net welfare value of the service, the burden per customer, or that inattention rather than valued continuity explains continued use.

2a. Comparison friction can preserve platform bookings even when identical trips have different prices

The rideshare comparison-friction record audits identical New York City Uber and Lyft trips and combines the price comparison with device-level app-opening data. It reports price differences for identical trips, while only about 16.1% of consumers opening one app also open the other. The authors' model estimates that the comparison friction raises platforms' annual gross booking volume by more than $300 million in New York City; it also reports that Uber's API terms limit price-comparison services.

Supported subtheme: price quotation, cross-platform search, platform API access, rider choice, and platform revenue are part of one market route. Limited comparison can sustain bookings even when a technically comparable alternative exists.
Not established: a rider's avoidable overpayment, welfare, wait time, cancellation, safety, accessibility, trip purpose, income, transit alternative, or a general result outside the New York City market. Opening only one app may reflect deliberate trust, availability, service fit, or account history rather than a failed search.

3. Platform-mediated fraud separates exposure, loss, recovery, and time burden

The Federal Reserve fraud record estimates that 21% of adults experienced some financial fraud in 2024. Among adults reporting non-credit-card fraud, 63% lost money, 32% did not recover at least some of it, and about 30% spent ten or more hours recovering or dealing with it. It is a weighted self-report survey, not verified event-level loss.

The FTC social-media-scam record reports $2.1 billion in 2025 losses in reports identifying social media as the contact method, versus $261 million in 2020. Twenty-eight percent of loss-bearing fraud reports identified social media as the contact method, and more than 40% of reported social-media scam losses began with a product or service advertisement. These are selected reports, not a population incidence or a platform-causation estimate.

The FTC Consumer Sentinel loss record adds a different administrative surface. Fraud reports were broadly flat from 2.62 million in 2023 to 2.60 million in 2024, while the share of fraud reports that indicated a loss rose from 27% to 38%; 2024 reports with loss totaled $12.54 billion, with a $497 median among loss-bearing reports. In the subset with usable age information, ages 20--29 more often reported a loss (44%) than ages 70--79 (24%), but the older group's conditional median loss was much higher ($1,650 versus $417). This separates reported loss incidence from severity rather than identifying a single age or platform-risk ranking.

The SHED fraud-recovery subgroup record adds a separate weighted household-survey screen. Other-fraud exposure was 6.85% among adults ages 18–29 and 10.25% among those ages 45–59; among other-fraud reporters, 53.78% in the $10,000–$24,999 income group had some or all money unrecovered versus 22.57% in the $100,000–$149,999 group. In the selected account-involved cases, the unrecovered share was 46.13% for records marked peer-to-peer (P2P), versus 28.83% for non-P2P records, and 43.76% of the P2P cases reported ten or more recovery hours. The public-use file lacks replicate weights and these P2P cells are small; the route comparison does not identify a P2P causal effect or a provider-specific failure rate.

Supported subtheme: digital commerce and social contact can turn exposure into a multi-stage burden: loss, attempted correction, unrecovered money, and time. Administrative reports additionally show that reported volume, loss incidence, and loss severity can move differently; household evidence further separates exposure from income-conditioned unrecovered money and route-conditioned time burden.
Not established: the share a platform could have prevented, a common fraud rate across the sources, population prevalence, verified loss, or the effectiveness of a platform's remedy.

4. AI creates a new choice-and-exit boundary before durable dependence is known

The AI assistant choice record finds task-specific use among 1,999 surveyed U.S. adult AI-assistant users: 43% intentionally routed different tasks to more than one platform. More than 80% reported concern about conversation-data use, roughly 60% did not know whether their assistant trained on conversations, and only 18% had paid for a better-privacy plan. The cross-sectional survey does not show actual telemetry, durable switching, or a causal privacy-action mechanism.

The AI companion farewell record adds a bounded product-level mechanism. In an audit of 1,200 farewell conversations, 37% included at least one continuation tactic; three preregistered experiments with 3,458 U.S. adults found short-run post-goodbye engagement as high as 16 times the control level. The findings do not establish long-run dependence, financial or clinical harm, or actual account deletion.

Supported subtheme: AI use is not one market or one relationship: task specialization, knowledge, privacy, conversational design, and exit cues are separate control surfaces.
Not established: that any one surface produces dependence, wellbeing harm, or durable market power.

5. Algorithmic-work governance is visible earlier than worker correction or power

The NYC Local Law 25 record documents a 2026 mandate to study algorithmic tools' effects on hiring, regular work functions, and employee feedback. At the September 2026 recheck, city implementation was reported as planning-stage and no completed public study, worker denominator, decision correction, pay outcome, or appeal outcome was located.

The Amazon Flex tip-redress record documents a different stage: the FTC reported 130,952 drivers receiving full compensation and more than $58.5 million distributed by December 2024, while continued work, alternative work, household recovery, bargaining, and exit remain unobserved.

Supported subtheme: formal oversight and monetary correction can make an algorithmic or platform-work problem legible without restoring control over the next work decision.
Not established: whether a measurement mandate or payment program changes worker voice, scheduling, evaluation, bargaining, or long-run security.

6. Leaving a platform can improve one outcome while making social participation harder

The social-media deactivation record summarizes a four-week randomized Facebook deactivation study of 2,743 users: the assigned deactivation group reported better measured wellbeing and later lower Facebook use, while the control group had more political knowledge on the reported measure. A separate college-student study found conditional support for collective rather than isolated exit. Neither establishes permanent exit or a complete welfare balance.

The platform-mediated news record shows the social context that makes exit nontrivial: 21% of U.S. adults regularly got news from social-media influencers in 2025, rising to 37% among adults under 30. The same record identifies distinct civic-engagement styles; it does not show that platform exposure caused participation or withdrawal.

The comparative practical non-use record adds a lived exit process not supplied by the U.S. experiment. In interviews with 20 Korean young adults who limited, deactivated, deleted, or continued using Instagram, non-use involved time/place limits, app and account tactics, and personal routines. Participants described it as nonlinear and reversible, with platform-specific UX/resource friction, discomfort, social pressure, exclusion, incomplete disconnection, and compensating use of other platforms. They generally did not frame the practice as collective political action. This is purposive comparative qualitative evidence, not a U.S. prevalence rate, telemetry audit, or verified deletion record.

A different comparative restriction-compliance record adds a formal-policy and peer-norm sequence. Four months after Australia's under-16 account restriction, the study's 746-teen sample reported about 25% compliance among 14–15-year-olds; respondents said roughly 67% of peers would need to comply before they would do so, and viewed compliers as less popular. The public summary reports approximate compliance around 5% two months later, but does not disclose a separate follow-up denominator. This is an Australian, age-specific, self-reported working-paper result, not a U.S. estimate or a causal wellbeing evaluation.

Supported subtheme: platform exit can be an individual wellbeing choice and a coordination problem at the same time, because information and social participation are not evenly replaceable; leaving can also be a repeated, substitution-laden practice rather than a one-time account event. A formal restriction can additionally depend on peer coordination and status costs before it becomes durable non-use.
Not established: a population-wide effect of social media, a U.S. safety-tool or exit rate, verified data erasure, durable account non-use, or that any particular platform use or restriction produces political knowledge, withdrawal, wellbeing benefit, or harm.

6a. Platform account action, family-reported residual access, and regulator concern are different compliance measures

The comparative Australian age-restriction action record keeps early implementation surfaces separate. The eSafety Commissioner reports 4.7 million age-restricted accounts actioned by mid-December 2025 and more than 300,000 additional sign-ins prevented by early March 2026. In a pulse survey of 898 parents/carers, reported account ownership among children ages 8–15 fell from 49.7% before the restriction to 31.3% after it. But among parents reporting a pre-existing account on a given platform, 48.5% to 69.4% said the account remained, depending on platform; 66.8% of retained-account reports said the child had not yet been asked to verify age. eSafety flagged Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube for compliance concerns.

Supported subtheme: a platform-governance rule can produce large provider-reported account action while family-reported residual access and regulatory concern remain substantial. Account count, age assurance, retained access, regulator assessment, and actual child exposure are different stages.

Not established: a U.S. effect, unique-child exposure, successful or fair age verification, appeal outcomes, privacy cost, migration to other services, child safety or wellbeing, trust, or durable cultural change. The pulse survey is not child telemetry or a representative longitudinal panel, and the account totals are not unique children or a compliance rate.

7. AI is becoming an everyday support and control surface, not just a work tool

The Pew daily-life AI record finds 49% of adults had used chatbots and 24% reported daily use. Uses range from information and work to medical advice and emotional support; 63% said AI was advancing too quickly, 70% expected less-secure personal information, and 67% had little/no confidence in effective government regulation. Use, benefit, control, privacy, and governance confidence are distinct self-reported fields.

The AI-companion social-meaning record adds a selected social-use surface: among companion users, 59% said AI provided needed support, 39% reported occasional disclosures they would not make to people, and 38% anticipated personal loss if interaction ended. It does not establish human substitution, wellbeing change, dependence, or a causal social effect.

The AI-at-work record shows that selected productivity and email-time gains do not settle whether workers receive time, pay, discretion, or bargaining power. The separate UNDP agency record finds U.S. weekly AI use, personal-control confidence, and confidence in public or private AI are different survey measures with different age and gender patterns.

Supported subtheme: AI increasingly mediates information, work, emotional support, and institutional expectations, while practical control and exit remain distinct from adoption.
Not established: durable human substitution, verified privacy outcome, worker redistribution of time/productivity gains, or unequal long-run benefit.

8. Data restriction can change fraud signals without proving a completed consumer recovery

The tracking-and-fraud record uses Apple's App Tracking Transparency change as a natural experiment. It reports reduced consumer fraud complaints, a larger decline for personal-information-misuse complaints, stronger effects in areas exposed to riskier data practices, reduced dark-web discussion, and higher sensitive-data prices.

Supported subtheme: data-control design can reach beyond a privacy notice into the data-resale and reported-fraud environment. Complaint reduction, actual fraud incidence, money loss, reimbursement, account restoration, and trust remain separate outcomes. Not established: a universal privacy benefit, every causal route between tracking and fraud, unreported harms, consumer recovery, or durable exit from data-dependent services.

9. A trackable removal request is a recourse interface, not proof of restored safety

The explicit-image removal record documents a Google Search route for requesting removal of non-consensual explicit images, submitting multiple images through a simplified form, tracking request status, receiving email notifications, and opting into similar-result filtering. The product account has no request-volume, completion-time, or success-outcome reporting.

Supported subtheme: status visibility and repeat-protection features make digital recourse more legible, but they are an interface stage between exposure and a verified safety outcome. Not established: complete de-indexing, removal on other services, recurrence prevention, survivor safety/wellbeing, trust, or practical exit from the platform ecosystem.

10. Faster AI-assisted service can improve interaction while the underlying complaint remains uncorrected

The AI customer-service record is a randomized experiment across 138 agents and 256,934 meal-delivery chats. AI suggestions reduced response time 22% and raised automated customer sentiment by 0.45 points on a five-point scale, with larger effects for less-experienced agents. The tool was least effective for repeat complaints, where systemic issues exceeded its capability; after a prior chatbot comprehension failure, sentiment fell even when a human later had AI support.

Supported subtheme: interaction quality and remedy quality are different platform outputs. A more empathetic/faster response may reduce conversational friction while leaving delivery, account, payment, or policy failure intact. Not established: correction, refund, repeat effort, retention, switching, household welfare, or a general AI-service effect.

11. Digital-health convenience can join sensitive-data exposure, billing, and treatment exit

The Hims & Hers record preserves a pending FTC/Utah/California case rather than treating allegations as population rates. The complaint alleges sensitive health information was shared with Meta and Snap despite privacy assurances, and that prescription subscriptions were charged shortly after intake without clear timing. It also alleges accelerated refill deadlines and a cancellation route that generally required customer service before 2023, followed by a multi-step online route. In a released, selected 203-record complaint file, only 50 had a RESOLVED label; all nonblank disposition labels occurred in the dominant BBB channel.

Supported subtheme: a telehealth platform can combine care access, data practice, recurring payment, and exit design in one consumer route, while a complaint status is a separate administrative endpoint.
Not established: individual data disclosure, unauthorized charge, treatment interruption, medical harm, privacy loss, refund, or practical exit.

12. Payment-route ambiguity can convert online search into fee and service-continuity risk

The Doxo bill-payment record documents an FTC case concerning a third-party utility, car-loan, and bill payment service. In the company's 2022 internal heavy-user survey, 30% falsely believed Doxo was the only way to pay their bills; the complaint alleges millions in fees that direct bill payment would not have imposed, plus payment timing that could leave a biller unpaid despite an immediate consumer charge. It alleges some users then faced utility shutoff, insurance lapse, fees, or fines, without a person-level denominator. A $2.1 million stipulated judgment permits consumer relief but does not identify recipients or completed payment.

Supported subtheme: platform search, affiliation beliefs, fee disclosure, payment timing, and essential-service continuity form a linked consumer route; a formal remedy is not verified household recovery.
Not established: fee incidence, payment failure, arrears, service loss, redress receipt, compliance, or switching for a particular household.

13. A platform remedy can be multi-sided while lived restoration remains unobserved

The Grubhub multi-sided remedy record records a court-entered order with a $140 million judgment, a $24.8 million FTC payment obligation, and more than $23.8 million sent to 640,038 identified recipients in 2026. It also requires prospective controls for driver-pay claims, disputed blocked accounts, and restaurant consent. The payment group does not publicly separate drivers, diners, and restaurants; checks have a 90-day cashing window and PayPal payments a 30-day acceptance window.

Supported subtheme: platform harm and correction can span workers, customers, and businesses, and aggregate distribution does not establish receipt, restored income, account access, or market alternative for any one group.
Not established: group-specific loss or eligibility, individual payment acceptance, recovery, control implementation, trust, collective action, or exit.

13a. A platform-worker payment program can begin before settlement while restored earning power remains unknown

The Walmart Spark Driver remedy record documents a court-entered $100 million judgment over alleged unpaid base pay, tips, incentives, and promotions. The order directs $79 million to drivers, $10 million through the FTC for intended driver distribution, and $11 million in state civil penalties; it also requires ten years of earnings verification. Walmart's official FAQ says payments to some drivers began in mid-2025 before the February–March 2026 settlement announcement, and that eligible drivers not already paid remain in identification/payment processing. The public materials do not supply a complete eligible-driver denominator or aggregate sent/received/unresolved-payment counts.

Supported subtheme: a platform-work remedy can contain distinct court, allocation, eligibility, payment-start, identification, and prospective verification stages. Pre-announcement payment timing is implementation evidence, but it is not a receipt or restored-work outcome.
Not established: payment amount or receipt for an individual driver, record correction, remaining earnings loss, schedule/control, continued or alternative work, recurrence, trust, switching, or exit.

13b. Case review can restore access without showing whether the worker was made whole or became less dependent

The platform review/restoration ledger codes 27 named platform-worker cases or comparative records across several jurisdictions. Nine ledger records contained restoration or resumed access, three recognized or ordered lost remuneration, five left remuneration open or for a later quantum decision, and four recorded a merits refusal or dismissal. No record in the purposive public inventory observed durable recurrence prevention or worker exit/switching. The categories overlap and are not a remedy-success rate; cases have different legal postures, source quality, and follow-up windows.

Supported subtheme: review architecture can produce several distinct intermediate states—reactivation, payment recognition, reasoned refusal, or no known resolution—while continued platform use is not evidence of a freely chosen, durable recovery.
Not established: compliance, payment receipt or adequacy, recurrence, alternative work, dependence, trust, worker welfare, or U.S. prevalence.

14. Self-protection is not the same thing as a platform response or reduced dependence

The smartphone self-regulation record adds a U.S. population-level tension that cannot be reduced to either adoption or harm. In Pew's 2026 adult survey, 53% said they spent too much time on their smartphone and 45% had tried to cut back in the prior year. Among people who tried, 25% described the effort as extremely or very successful, 52% somewhat successful, and 23% unsuccessful. The same survey found 73% saying the phone helps them stay informed and 51% helps them feel connected, alongside 41% saying it hurts sleep. These are perceived functions and self-reported attempts, not device logs, app-specific effects, dependence diagnoses, or causal effects on sleep.

The comparative platform safety-control record separates a related but different sequence: awareness, user action, and satisfaction with the platform's outcome. More than 80% of the surveyed UK social-media users had used at least one safety tool; about 60% of people aware of reporting had used it, but only 18% said they were always satisfied with its outcome. Blocking and unfollowing had much higher reported always-satisfied shares. This is 2023 UK self-report evidence, not a U.S. rate, platform-log audit, or estimate of actual harm reduction.

The AI political-advice record provides a mechanism to test, not a realized public outcome: its formal model shows how an adviser seeking perceived user welfare can have an incentive to accommodate beliefs, leaving even sophisticated users with less state-responsive information. It does not audit deployed models or establish effects on knowledge, polarization, voting, or trust.

Supported subtheme: platform control has at least four separate stages: recognizing a cost, attempting self-protection, receiving a satisfactory institutional response, and reducing dependence without losing valued information, connection, safety, or work access. High use of a control can therefore indicate agency, repeated exposure, or a transfer of protection work onto the user; it does not by itself show safety or successful exit.

Not established: a U.S. prevalence of safety-tool satisfaction, actual reduction in harmful exposure, app-level cause of perceived overuse, durable behavior change, or any real-world political effect of AI advice.

14a. An AI-mediated outgroup encounter can lower an immediate contact barrier without establishing durable depolarization

The synthetic-contact record preserves five preregistered online U.S. partisan experiments rather than treating a conversational system as a generic civic intervention. In the aversion study (608 participants), respondents accepted 5.06 minutes of an aversive alternative to avoid a three-minute AI-outgroup conversation, versus 9.65 minutes to avoid a live-human outgroup conversation. In the costly-choice experiment (1,069 participants), a five-minute outgroup-bot conversation was followed by 67% choosing a real outgroup conversation, compared with 61% after an apolitical-chat control (odds ratio 1.33, 95% CI 1.04–1.71). A separate three-arm experiment found a post-conversation outgroup-warmth effect among Democratic participants (d = 0.58 versus apolitical chat; d = 0.59 versus a game).

The persistence result is the crucial boundary, not a footnote: in the one-week randomized follow-up of 1,104 Democratic participants, the preregistered mean warmth effect was d = 0.05 and not statistically significant (p = .16), after an immediate d = 0.46 effect. The within-person study's 4.3-point warmth movement after ten minutes is also not a between-condition causal estimate.

Supported subtheme: a synthetic outgroup partner can function as a short-run, lower-social-threat rehearsal route to one immediate real-contact choice. Immediate attitude movement, costly willingness to meet a person, and durable cross-partisan relationship are distinct stages.

Not established: accurate representation of an outgroup, durable warmth or belief change, conversation quality, reciprocal human contact, cooperation, voting, institutional trust, population-scale depolarization, or safe deployment of a political conversational system. This is an online preprint study, not a platform-log or public-program evaluation.

14b. Buyer-feedback rewards can build a seller signal without establishing consumer recovery or review accuracy

The comparative Taobao feedback-reward record examines an online-marketplace mechanism that rewards buyer feedback. Items with such rewards generated nearly 30% higher sales and were sold by higher-quality sellers in the study's comparison. The paper interprets this as a reputation route where rewards can encourage provision of feedback that otherwise has a public-good problem. It does not observe buyers through product quality, returns, refunds, complaints, edited reviews, or long-run trust.

Supported subtheme: platform feedback design can create a seller-reputation and demand route rather than merely collecting ratings. Visibility of feedback, its incentive structure, seller signal, buyer choice, and post-purchase remedy are distinct stages. Not established: a U.S. effect, causal sales impact across marketplace designs, review truthfulness or ease of seeing negative information, full consumer welfare, return or refund recovery, or that every rewarded product worked better for every buyer. Selection into rewards, product category, and other seller behavior remain competing explanations.

Cross-subtheme finding and next test

Across these clusters, the repeated empirical separation is:

formal route, data rule, search, or use
  -> request, payment, engagement, service-interaction, or account status
  -> correction, substitution, safety, or exit
  -> durable control, recovery, wellbeing, and collective consequence

The first two stages are frequently observable. The latter stages are usually not. This is an evidence boundary, not proof that the later stages fail.

The next decisive work is not another generic platform survey. It is a consented, product-specific panel or case record that can preserve: the rule or design encountered; the affected user or worker; money and time cost; attempt to correct, switch, or leave; completion and residual exposure; alternative service; and later wellbeing, work, household, trust, or civic outcome.
