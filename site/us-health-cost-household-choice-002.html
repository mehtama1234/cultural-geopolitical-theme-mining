Baseline health spending marks different health and work paths, but it is not a burden score

Status: provisional MEPS Panel 27 longitudinal conditioning finding · Checked: 2026-09-15

The bounded finding

The MEPS Panel 27 longitudinal file shows that people in different baseline health-expenditure bands do not have the same later health or work profile. Among people followed across the panel with valid 2022 and 2023 expenditure and perceived-health fields, the weighted 2022 total-health-expenditure quartiles were cut at $259, $1,421, and $5,433. The bands are descriptive conditioning groups, not treatment groups.

*Employment measures use their own valid endpoint-status universe within each baseline expenditure band; the table should not be read as if the employment and health denominators were identical.

The first pattern is a health-direction gradient with overlap: the highest baseline-spending group has a higher share reporting improved perceived health than the lowest group, while the lowest group has a higher share reporting worsened health. But the differences overlap substantially, and every group contains improvement, worsening, and no change.

The second pattern is a work-continuity gradient that cannot be named a cost effect: 69.2% of the lowest-spending group were employed in both endpoint rounds compared with 49.8% of the highest-spending group. The high-spending group is not simply “losing jobs”—its employed-to-not-employed share is 2.6%, similar to the other groups, while its not-employed-to-employed share is lower. Age, disability, illness severity, retirement, baseline employment, insurance, and provider use could all help produce this pattern.

Utilization does not move as one block

The same expenditure conditioning produces a treatment-utilization counterexample. Among people with valid 2022 and 2023 utilization fields, the lowest-spending group had increases in office-based and outpatient visits, while the highest-spending group had declines in both. Prescription fills rose in every band, although the highest-spending estimate is imprecise.

These are changes in recorded annual utilization, not access or quality. A decline in visits can mean completed treatment, fewer unmet needs, attrition, coding or reporting differences, or reduced care. More prescriptions can mean continuing treatment, new illness, substitution away from visits, or more medication need. The utilization fields have their own valid denominator and should not be interpreted as the health-status denominator in the first table.

Out-of-pocket protection is concentrated differently from total spending

The same panel separates total expenditure from the person's recorded out-of-pocket payment. In the lowest three baseline-spending bands, out-of-pocket spending rose between 2022 and 2023. In the highest band, it fell by $950.72 per person. The person-level out-of-pocket share of total expenditure was also lower in the highest band in both years, although it rose slightly there from 17.0% to 18.4%.

This is a protection-and-composition signal, not proof that insurance improved for high-spending people. The highest band may contain people with intensive care whose payer mix changed, people completing a large treatment episode, or people who left the panel or had different service composition. The OOP share is a person-level ratio among positive-total-expenditure records; it is not the share of a household budget, income, or liquid resources.

Why baseline expenditure is not household burden

Total health expenditure is the amount paid for care across payers and sources, not the amount the person or family personally sacrificed. High expenditure can mean serious need with strong insurance protection, a planned procedure, older age, or intensive treatment. Low expenditure can mean good health, lack of access, delayed care, or unmeasured unmet need. The expenditure bands therefore organize the next comparison; they do not rank welfare, affordability, or financial distress.

The paired panel reinforces this distinction. Across all valid paired persons, 21.0% improved in perceived health, 22.8% worsened, and 56.1% were unchanged. The same people also had a separate employment and wage surface. A panel can show that these outcomes coexist or move differently; it cannot, by itself, identify whether a bill, health event, treatment choice, employer decision, or care responsibility produced the later movement.

What the evidence supports

  • Baseline health spending is a useful conditioning variable for a material- health/work analysis because health-cost exposure is distributed across different later health and employment profiles.
  • Health experience is not one-way. Improvement, worsening, and stability are present in every expenditure band.
  • Employment continuity is lower in the highest spending band in this paired descriptive comparison, while entry into employment is also less common; this is compatible with a need/age/retirement composition story as much as a work-disruption story.
  • A later outcome needs its own denominator. A valid paired health sample is not automatically a valid employment, wage, time, or care sample.
  • Total spending and out-of-pocket payment can move in opposite directions; payer protection and utilization therefore need separate measurement.

What it does not support

This comparison does not show that health spending caused employment exit, that high spending harmed health, that low spending reflects affordability, or that insurance protected one subgroup. It does not measure the household's out-of-pocket burden, liquid room, unpaid care hours, transport, waiting, schedule control, debt, food or housing trade-offs, trust, political action, or geopolitical consequences.

The baseline expenditure cut points are sample-specific weighted quartiles, and the expenditure distribution is skewed. The normal-approximation intervals are design-based Taylor-linearized intervals using the MEPS longitudinal weight, stratum, and primary sampling unit. They do not remove attrition, nonresponse, field missingness, or composition differences.

End-to-end implication

health need and coverage
  -> total spending and out-of-pocket exposure
  -> visits, prescriptions, unpaid care, work, travel, or delay
  -> health, earnings, employment, food/housing, and family-time outcomes
  -> recovery, institutional judgment, trust, or action

This pass deepens only the middle of that chain. It says that a health-cost panel should be conditioned on baseline need/cost and should keep health and work outcomes separate. The next stronger design must add treatment continuity, out-of-pocket burden, unpaid care/time, household resources and debt, baseline employment/retirement, and a dated event or attribution. The PSID acquisition gate remains the stronger route for repeated family resources and typical-week work/care measures; MEPS remains the stronger health-cost panel.
