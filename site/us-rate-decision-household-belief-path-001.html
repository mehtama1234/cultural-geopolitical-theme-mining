When a rate decision becomes a household belief

A central-bank rate decision can reach a household first as a belief: borrowing may cost more, prices may rise, or a large purchase should wait.

The argument

Consider a renter, a saver, and a homeowner with a variable-rate loan. The same rate move can help the saver and hurt the borrower. The message around the decision also matters, but actual contract exposure decides the size and direction of the result.

A rate expectation is not yet a payment.

What the sources establish

An NBER survey of more than 25,000 US households with randomized information finds that people expect higher rates to raise borrowing costs and inflation. They report reducing spending, especially on durable goods, and inflation expectations help explain portfolio changes.

A separate randomized survey finds that information about current and next-year rates changes expectations, while information beyond one year adds little. These studies measure reported beliefs and choices, not each household’s later records.

Follow the decision

rate decision and public message → belief about prices, jobs, and borrowing → spending, saving, debt, or portfolio choice → payment and financial room → trust in the message

The same move can help one household through deposit income and hurt another through a loan. One average response hides that split.

Keep these apart

  • A rate expectation and an actual payment.
  • A planned purchase and a completed purchase.
  • Borrower, saver, renter, and owner exposure.
  • The policy rate and the rate in a contract.

Next test

Follow households for 18 months around a rate decision. Join the message received to loan and deposit terms, prices, income, spending, saving, debt, and large purchases.

Reading rule

Do not describe one household response when borrowers, savers, renters, and owners may face opposite effects.
