Finding 002: The first payoff may be movement, not pay

Status: provisional · Last checked: 2026-09-11

Finding

The strongest early labor-market signal is not a general pay increase. It is movement: some workers use AI to handle tasks or enter roles that once required scarce experience, while the average pay and hours of adopters remain close to the path of comparable non-adopters.

That movement could be an opening for workers, a way for firms to redraw jobs, or both. The evidence does not yet tell us which.

Evidence read more closely

The Denmark study links large worker and workplace surveys to administrative records. It finds no detectable average effect larger than 2% on earnings or recorded hours two years after ChatGPT's launch. It does find that adopters worked about 4% more of a full-time equivalent in their most recent occupation by December 2024. NBER Working Paper 33777

The customer-support study reports higher measured productivity and suggests that some workers continued performing better when the tool was unavailable, while warning that outage periods are rare and may not be random. NBER Working Paper 31161

Causal chain under test

AI assistance -> faster learning or lower entry barrier -> movement into a different task or occupation -> job structure changes before pay changes -> power depends on whether movement is chosen, rewarded, or required

The first two arrows have evidence. The last arrow is open.

Why this matters socially

If AI lowers the experience needed to enter a role, it may widen access for some workers. It may also weaken the value of experience for workers already inside the role. The same tool can therefore help newcomers and unsettle incumbents.

Strongest challenge

Workers who are already more mobile may both adopt AI and change occupations. Controls reduce this concern, but association is not proof that AI caused every move.

Next tests

  1. Compare the Denmark mobility result with U.S. worker and firm evidence.
  2. Add direct measures of promotion, task assignment, monitoring, and job quality.
  3. Search for workers who do not adopt, cannot adopt, or are required to adopt.
  4. Compare public-tool users with workers bound to employer systems.
