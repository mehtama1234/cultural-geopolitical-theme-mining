Finding 004: Efficiency does not settle the control question

Status: provisional · Last checked: 2026-09-11

Finding

Cross-country evidence suggests that a workplace can gain efficiency from software and still leave basic control questions unanswered. Managers report problems with accountability and opaque decisions. Case studies of logistics and health care show that algorithmic management can streamline work while also raising concerns about surveillance and job quality.

The practical test is simple: when a system affects a person’s work, can that person know it is being used, understand the basis of the decision, correct a bad record, and reach a responsible human?

What we directly know

  • The OECD studied more than 6,000 firms in six countries. Managers report concerns including unclear accountability, difficulty following decision logic, and protection of worker health. OECD Algorithmic Management
  • An ILO/JRC study examined logistics and health care in France, Italy, India, and South Africa. It reports efficiency benefits alongside risks to job quality and concern about intrusive surveillance. ILO case studies
  • A randomized NBER study found surveillance alone did not significantly change average performance, while unclear explanations for surveillance changes reduced output. NBER Working Paper 33348

Accountability checklist

  1. Notice: Are workers told what is collected or automated?
  2. Purpose: Is the purpose stated plainly?
  3. Basis: Can the worker see the facts used in a decision?
  4. Correction: Can a wrong record be fixed?
  5. Override: Can a person override the system?
  6. Appeal: Can the worker reach a responsible human?
  7. Responsibility: Is an institution accountable when the system is wrong?
  8. Boundary: Are there limits outside work time or the original purpose?

Causal chain under test

software measures or assigns work -> decision scales -> responsibility becomes harder to locate -> uncertainty and stress rise -> trust, effort, job quality, and bargaining change

The evidence supports concern about the middle link. It does not yet establish the size of effects on workers.

Strongest challenge

Employer concerns and case-study risks may overstate harm if good governance works. We must test whether notice, explanation, correction, and appeal reduce harm rather than assume they do.

Next tests

  1. Read the OECD tables and worker evidence it cites.
  2. Extract the ILO case methods, worker groups, and settings.
  3. Find public workplace policies specifying notice, correction, override, and appeal.
  4. Compare a unionized workplace, contractor workforce, and platform workforce.
