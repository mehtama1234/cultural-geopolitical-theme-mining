Finding 006: Technology becomes a worker benefit through the social bargain

Status: provisional · Last checked: 2026-09-11

Finding

Two non-Western studies show why “technology adoption” is too broad a measure. In Indian garment factories, a communication tool did nothing by itself but improved productivity and earnings when paired with better HR incentives. In a Chinese food-delivery platform, an AI text-to-speech tool helped deaf or hard-of-hearing workers and closed part of their hourly-pay gap.

The tool matters. But the result also depends on who can use it, what problem it solves, who responds to worker information, and how the gain is shared.

What we directly know

  • A randomized trial in Indian garment factories found no effect from a worker-management communication technology alone. Pairing it with HR incentives increased productivity by 5%, reduced absenteeism by 13%, and raised worker earnings by 3%. NBER Working Paper 35445
  • A study of a Chinese food-delivery platform found that an AI text-to-speech tool improved deliveries, ratings, labor supply, retention, and profits for deaf or hard-of-hearing workers, closing one-third of the disability hourly-pay gap. NBER Working Paper 35372

Causal chain under test

worker barrier or missing information -> tool introduced -> managers, workers, or customers respond better -> work becomes more accessible or coordinated -> output and worker outcomes improve -> gains depend on incentives, access, and capture

The studies support the importance of fit and organizational response. They do not show that every AI tool improves inclusion or pay.

Why this matters

A tool can be technically capable but socially useless if no one responds to the information it produces. It can also be valuable because it removes a barrier that a broad productivity average would miss.

Strongest challenge

Neither study is a general test of workplace AI. One studies a communication intervention in Indian factories; the other studies a specific accessibility tool on a Chinese platform. Their value is in showing mechanisms and neglected groups, not a universal effect size.

Next tests

  1. Find worker-level evidence by disability, gender, age, and contract type.
  2. Compare tools that remove barriers with tools that evaluate or discipline.
  3. Test whether worker reporting leads to action or merely creates more data.
  4. Add Latin American, African, and Southeast Asian workplace evidence.
  5. Track who captures gains when inclusion also raises firm profit.
