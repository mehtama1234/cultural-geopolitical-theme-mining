A short argument · US real pay, prices, and political choice

The vote may respond more to lost buying power than to inflation alone

A local price rise and a loss of buying power are not the same thing.

The argument

Two counties can have the same inflation rate while pay rises in one and falls behind in the other. Lost real pay is a better starting point for political research than the inflation rate alone, but it does not prove why an individual voted.

Follow the local pay gap

One county's prices rise six percent while pay rises seven percent. Another has the same price increase but pay rises two percent. Workers in the second county cut purchases, use savings, borrow, or give up care. A county result is a clue, not a household diary.

local prices and pay → real buying power → household cuts, borrowing, or sacrifice → economic judgment and blame → turnout, vote, or no political change

The deeper finding

Keep local price growth, nominal pay, real pay, vote share, and turnout apart. The strongest claim is only that lost buying power deserves priority in the next political test.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not join a person's local prices, pay, rent, debt, purchases, economic views, blame, turnout, and vote. Other local changes may have moved the result.

Next test

Use individual or household data to join local prices, pay, rent, debt, purchases, economic views, policy knowledge, party identity, turnout, and vote choice where legally and ethically possible.

Reading rule

Say “real pay fell,” “local prices rose,” or “the county vote changed.” Do not collapse all three into “voters punished inflation.
