When the small-business rule changes the local market

An eligibility line can change which firms receive public credit or contracts. If larger firms gain access, the smallest firms may lose revenue, jobs, innovation, or local choice.

The argument

A small firm may depend on one public customer to keep workers, build a record, and obtain private financing. Losing that contract can reduce future options even if a larger firm supplies the service well.

A firm just below a threshold may face a cliff: less public revenue, less lender confidence, and less ability to survive the next year.

Firm size is not the same as need, quality, or local value.

Follow one eligibility change

A public program expands access to firms above a size threshold. A larger company wins a contract. A younger small firm loses revenue, delays hiring, and cannot show the cash flow a lender wants. It exits a year later. Customers still receive the service, but now face fewer providers. In another market, scale may lower price or improve delivery.

eligibility rule changes → larger firms gain access → contracts and support move → smallest firms lose revenue or exit → wages, innovation, customers, and local choice change

The customer result depends on service quality and alternatives, not only on which firm won.

The local chain

Firm

Revenue loss can delay hiring, reduce investment, or end the business.

Household

Owners may draw less and workers may lose a nearby job or wage.

Customer

The service may continue while local choice and resilience shrink.

Scale can still be valuable. A larger firm may have lower unit cost or stronger delivery. The point is to measure the service result and future market rather than assume size alone is fair capacity.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether larger firms delivered better service, how prices changed, or whether customers lost a meaningful local option. It does not show whether the threshold created a financing cliff or whether firms could recover through private customers.

Next test

Follow firms, owners, workers, and customers for five years around an eligibility change. Join support, contracts, loans, revenue, exit, wages, hours, patents, prices, quality, service access, household income, and local entry. Compare firms just below and above the line.

Reading rule: keep firm size, need, scale, competition, jobs, and customer value separate. A broader program is not automatically a fairer program.
