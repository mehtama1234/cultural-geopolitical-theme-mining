Connected US finding · care, money and public life

From family time to money and economic views

Family care is work, but it is often counted first as private time. A family may keep someone safe while the caregiver loses room in the rest of life.

The argument

Care can protect a family while also taking time, income, savings, or future choices from the person providing it. The same care need has a different money effect depending on wages, health, distance, and how many people can share the work.

Care can be valuable and costly at the same time. Measure both.

What the sources show

BLS reports that 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24. Fifty-five percent were women, and care providers spent an average of 3.9 hours on care days. An NBER study links reported family caregiving to administrative earnings records over 25 years and finds different labor effects by gender.

The long period matters. A short leave may become a slower wage path, fewer promotions, or a later return to work. This does not show that every caregiver loses earnings or leaves work.

BLS eldercare release · BLS time-use data · NBER Working Paper 31450 · HHS caregiver tax-credit review

The connection

need for family care → unpaid time, paid care, or both → work hours, earnings, and travel change → savings, debt, health, or housing room changes → view of employers and public support changes → demand for a different care rule

Care can be chosen, loving, and useful. The question is who can absorb the time and who must give up work or pay for help. A benefit that exists but is hard to use may not change the burden.

The full household account

Time

Hours spent driving, supervising, arranging care, and handling emergencies.

Money

Lost earnings, paid care, transport, medical costs, debt, and savings used.

Value

The safety, comfort, and continuity the care provides to the family.

A caregiver may keep every bill current while losing sleep, work hours, savings, or the ability to change jobs. A family may avoid a paid-care bill by moving in a relative or using unpaid time. The cost has moved into one person’s future earnings and health.

If leave cannot be taken without losing income, it may look generous on paper but be weak in practice. If a tax credit arrives after work has already been reduced, it may not restore the lost path.

What remains unknown

The sources do not measure the same person’s full care cost, savings, debt, health, housing, trust, and political action. They do not show how care is divided among relatives, how much paid care would have cost, or whether employer and public support changed later household outcomes.

Next test

Follow families for two years from the start of a regular care need. Record who provides the hours, what work changes, what money is spent or lost, sleep, health, transport, savings, debt, paid-care use, the timing of benefits, and whether the family asks for a public or employer response.

Reading rule: do not call family care an economic loss without measuring what it provided. Do not call it free without measuring whose time, income, and future choices carried it.
