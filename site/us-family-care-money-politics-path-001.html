Connected US finding · care, money and public life

From family time to money and economic views

Family care is work, but it is often counted first as private time.

In one sentence

Care can protect a family while also taking time, income, savings, or future choices from the person providing it.

Keep care provided, care paid for, work change, money change, and political response separate.

What the sources show

Unpaid eldercare is common and care can connect to work

BLS reports that 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023–24. Fifty-five percent were women, and providers spent an average of 3.9 hours on care days. An NBER study links reported family caregiving to administrative earnings records over 25 years and finds different labor effects by gender.

This does not show that every caregiver loses earnings or leaves work, nor does it measure the full household cost.

BLS eldercare release · NBER Working Paper 31450 · HHS caregiver tax-credit review

The connection

need for family care → unpaid time, paid care, or both → work hours and earnings change → savings, debt, health, or housing room changes → view of employers and public support changes → demand for a different care rule

Care can be a burden, but it can also be chosen, loving, and useful. The question is who can absorb the time and who must give up work or pay for help.

The four outside views

Household: A family may trade work time for care, ask another relative for help, or buy care. The money effect depends on wages, savings, distance, and health.

Company and sector: Employers set schedules, leave, remote-work options, and benefits. Support may keep a worker in a job, but a benefit that cannot be used at the needed time may have little value.

Money and finance: The full account includes lost earnings, paid care, transport, debt, savings, medical costs, and the value of care provided.

Public power: Tax credits, leave, care supply, health coverage, and work rules shape whether care stays inside the family or becomes a paid or public need.

What would change the finding

  • Care start and end dates for the same person and family.
  • Work hours, earnings, job changes, leave, and promotion over time.
  • Paid care, travel, health, debt, savings, and housing costs.
  • Employer support, public benefits, caregiver credits, and actual use.
  • Differences by gender, income, race, age, disability, family structure, and place.
  • Trust, complaints, local policy, and political action after a care shock.

Next test

Follow families for two years from the start of a regular care need. Record who provides the hours, what work changes, what money is spent or lost, which support is available, and whether the family asks for a public or employer response.

Reading rule

Do not call family care an economic loss without measuring what it provided. Do not call it free without measuring whose time, income, and future choices carried it.
