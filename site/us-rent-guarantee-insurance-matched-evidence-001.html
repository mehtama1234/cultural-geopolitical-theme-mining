A short argument · US rent guarantees, insurance, and housing

Rent insurance may protect a home before the crisis, but access is the problem

Protection before a rent shock may help, but the people who need it most may be least able to buy it.

The argument

Insurance can reduce a deposit or landlord loss, yet still leave the tenant with a premium, exclusions, an indemnity, or a collection demand after a claim.

Follow one insured tenant

A renter buys a guarantee instead of paying a large deposit. Illness reduces income. The provider pays the landlord under the policy, but the tenant still owes under the agreement. If the claim arrives after a cure period or court step, the payment may not preserve the home.

income or health shock → rent cannot be paid → insurance claim, public aid, legal help, or no protection → landlord payment or case → housing, credit, premium, and tenant debt

The deeper finding

Insurance can reduce a landlord's loss, lower an entry deposit, preserve a lease, or create later tenant debt. The comparison is who could buy protection, what the claim paid, and whether the tenant remained housed.

What remains unproven

The model is not a measured US market result. We do not yet have real premiums, exclusions, take-up, denied claims, repayment, or two-year housing outcomes.

Next test

Follow a real pilot for two years. Compare insured, publicly supported, and uninsured renters after similar income or health shocks.

Reading rule

Do not call a model a market. Ask who can buy protection before the shock and who is left with the loss.
