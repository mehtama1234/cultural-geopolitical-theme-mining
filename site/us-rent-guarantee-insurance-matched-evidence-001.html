Connected US finding · rent protection and housing risk

Rent protection can start before the crisis, but the hardest question is who can buy it

A guarantee may lower the move-in hurdle. It is not yet clear that it keeps the renter housed after the shock.

The argument

The useful test is not “does insurance cover rent?” Ask who gets protection before the shock, what triggers payment, and who carries the loss afterward.

Follow one renter

A renter can pay this month's rent but cannot produce a large deposit or family guarantor. A guarantee lowers the entry cost. Six months later, illness or lost work hours creates four possible paths: a fast claim that preserves the lease, slow public aid, a denied or delayed claim, or a landlord payment followed by renter repayment.

lease entry → premium and conditions → income or health shock → claim, public aid, legal help, or no payment → housing, credit, repayment, renewal

The deeper finding

Rent protection has two clocks. The first asks whether the renter can enter the home. The second asks whether the household can remain housed when income falls. A product can succeed on the first clock and fail on the second. A landlord sees stronger payment protection; a renter may see a lower deposit, a premium, and a later debt.

What remains unproven

Public evidence does not yet link premium, claim timing, denial, recovery, collections, eviction, renewal, and housing at 12 and 24 months for the same renter.

Next test

Follow a pilot against renters using a deposit, personal guarantor, direct aid, or no added protection. Measure entry cost, claims, repayment, credit, eviction, moves, and whether the renter is housed one and two years later.

Reading rule

Do not call a model a market or a landlord payment tenant stability. Ask who could buy protection and where the loss ended up.
