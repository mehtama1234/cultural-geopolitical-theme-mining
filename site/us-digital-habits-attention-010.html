Finding 010: Consumer AI choice is differentiated by task, but privacy control is limited by knowledge and switching costs

Status: bounded consumer-AI market and privacy-action finding · Checked: 2026-09-21

The bounded finding

A June 2026 study of 1,999 U.S. adult AI-assistant users finds a concentrated market that is not culturally or functionally uniform. ChatGPT and Gemini hold most primary use, but smaller platforms occupy task niches: Claude reaches 33% of coding tasks versus 7% overall, while Copilot is more work-oriented. About 43% of users intentionally route different tasks to more than one platform.

The more interesting conversion is between concern and control. More than 80% reported concern about conversation-data use, but roughly 60% did not know whether their assistant trained on conversations. In the study's model, knowledge of the training policy was more strongly associated with protective tools than concern alone. The choice experiment estimated that users valued avoiding human review at $11.20 per month—more than avoiding model training at $2.97—with the value rising for sensitive tasks.

task and sensitivity
  -> platform selection and disclosure context
  -> trust learned through use, privacy concern, and policy knowledge
  -> protective setting, paid privacy feature, multihoming, or continued default use
  -> switching, disclosure, practical exit, and durable trust [open]

Why it matters

This is a consumer-culture mechanism, not an adoption statistic. People appear to treat AI assistants as different tools for different kinds of work and disclosure. Trust is not simply inherited from market share: the study reports large user-versus-aware-nonuser differences for some challengers. But concern does not automatically become protection. Knowledge, price, habit, task fit, and accumulated context can determine whether a person acts.

It sharpens the atlas's “dependence versus exit” tension:

  • a user may multihome without being able to leave the default platform;
  • a user may value privacy but not know which setting changes data use;
  • a privacy feature may be worth money in a sensitive task but lose to price;
  • task-specific trust can support continued use without proving safe disclosure.

Do not overread

  • The study is a weighted survey and choice experiment, not product telemetry or a longitudinal switching panel.
  • Reported platform choice is not verified time, disclosure, productivity, or retention behavior.
  • Willingness to pay is hypothetical and does not show purchase or provider implementation.
  • The associations between knowledge, concern, and protective tools do not establish causation.
  • The study does not measure harm, dependence, wellbeing, political persuasion, or actual data deletion.

Next decisive test

The next useful evidence is a same-user or authorized account panel linking task type and sensitivity to actual platform choice, disclosure, settings changes, human review/training terms, repeat use, switching attempt, and successful deletion or exit. Keep stated trust, observed use, provider policy, and verified control as separate stages.

Source

Zou, Platform Choice, Trust, and Privacy in the Consumer AI Assistant Market
