National health spending is a system scale, not a household outcome

Checked: 2026-09-15
Sources: CMS historical NHEA tables, CMS NHE fact sheet, and CMS projections
Machine record: CMS national-health-expenditure record

The bounded finding

CMS’s National Health Expenditure Accounts put the US health system at approximately $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person, equal to 18.0% of GDP. CMS reports 2024 spending of $1,644.6 billion through private health insurance, $1,118.0 billion through Medicare, $931.7 billion through Medicaid, and $556.6 billion out of pocket.

This is a system-scale map of money and sponsorship. It is not a household medical-burden survey. The same national total can contain very different experiences: an insured person whose care is covered, an uninsured person who delays care, a caregiver who loses work time, a patient with medical debt, or a household that pays premiums but receives little care.

What the NHEA makes visible

GDP relationship 18.0% of GDP Health care’s macroeconomic weight Whether households experience the share as value, strain, security, or delay
Payment channels Private insurance 31%; Medicare 21%; Medicaid 18%; OOP 11% Payer architecture and funding routes Premiums, deductibles, denied care, debt, time, and unpaid care
Sponsors Federal government 31%; households 28%; private business 18%; state/local government 16% Who is assigned financial sponsorship in the national account Who bears the practical incidence and who can change terms

The categories are related but not interchangeable. “Household sponsor” is not the same as direct cash paid at a point of service. “Out of pocket” is not the same as total household burden. “Per person” is not a typical-person estimate.

The forward signal

CMS projects average NHE growth of 5.4% per year from 2025 through 2034, above projected GDP growth of 4.1%, with health spending reaching 20.6% of GDP in 2034. That projection is useful as a pressure and planning signal: the system is expected to claim a larger share of national output.

It does not answer the more human questions. Will that growth purchase better access? Will it reduce avoidable illness? Will it shift costs into premiums, taxes, employer compensation, public budgets, or household payment? Will it create more care capacity or merely more spending around the same bottlenecks?

The connection to household room

national spending and GDP share
  -> payer and sponsor architecture
  -> coverage and provider terms
  -> patient bill, premium, tax, wage, or time exposure
  -> care received, delayed, substituted, or forgone
  -> health, work, care, debt, trust, and political judgment

CMS measures the first two stages. MEPS, the Federal Reserve SHED, AHRQ access measures, medical-debt sources, and longitudinal household data are needed for the later stages. The atlas should join those layers only when the unit, person, date, geography, and denominator can be preserved.

What this does not prove

  • It does not show that higher national spending produces better health or more equal access.
  • It does not estimate the typical household’s medical burden or the cost of an illness episode.
  • It does not reveal who delayed, skipped, substituted, or received care.
  • It does not turn payer or sponsor shares into a causal account of political trust.
  • It does not establish that the projected 2034 spending share will occur.

The next test

Pair CMS NHEA with MEPS and household financial surveys to follow the route from system spending to the same household’s premium, deductible, out-of-pocket payment, unpaid care, work/time loss, medical debt, care received, and recovery. Keep aggregate national accounts, person-level expenditures, and event-level care episodes as separate evidence layers.

Reading rule

When a health-spending total rises, ask: whose payment changed, whose care changed, whose time changed, and who still could not reach care?
