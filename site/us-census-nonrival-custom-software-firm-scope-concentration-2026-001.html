Finding 001: Reusable software can make larger firms easier to run

Status: bounded technology, firm-scope, and market-structure finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The finding in plain language

Some custom software can be used at many company locations at the same time. That makes it different from equipment that must be physically duplicated.

Using confidential Census capital-expenditure data, a CES working paper reports that firms with more establishments are more likely to invest in custom software, while spending less on it as a share of total capital expenditure. The authors build a model in which firms weigh the management cost of operating many establishments against the benefit of software that can be reused across them. In the calibrated model, cheaper custom software can explain part of the long-run rise in firm scope and concentration.

The route is:

custom software becomes cheaper or more capable
  -> one investment can serve more establishments
  -> the cost of managing firm scope changes
  -> firms may expand or concentrate
  -> prices, jobs, wages, service quality, and consumer choice [not measured here]

What this adds to the atlas

This adds a firm-and-technology mechanism beneath concentration:

Digital infrastructure can change not only what firms produce, but how many places one firm can coordinate and control.

That makes concentration partly a technology-and-management question, not only a merger or pricing question. It also creates a cultural and political question for later work: when one organization becomes the interface for many places, who can contest it, switch away, or understand its decisions?

Limits and counterexamples

  • The source is a Census CES working paper, not an official Census endorsement.
  • The empirical relationship does not by itself prove that software caused firm scope; larger or better-managed firms may be more able to adopt it.
  • The concentration result is a calibrated model implication, not a direct estimate of current market concentration caused by software.
  • The study does not measure wages, employment, prices, privacy, service quality, worker discretion, consumer choice, or political influence.
  • Larger scope can bring efficiencies as well as dependence or bargaining-power concerns; the source does not decide which outcome dominates.

The next decisive artifact is a firm-industry-place panel joining software adoption, establishments, entry and exit, prices, productivity, wages, jobs, worker control, supplier and customer options, and later concentration or switching.
