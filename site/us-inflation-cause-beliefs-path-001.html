Connected US finding · prices, politics and household money

When a household price becomes political blame

A price people feel can become a story about who caused it and what should be done.

In one sentence

A stated cause is evidence of belief, not proof of the economic cause.

Keep price experience, blame, policy, trust, and action separate.

What the sources show

People name different causes and policy answers

A US NBER survey with information and choice experiments finds that people see inflation as broadly negative, name several causes, and differ by party and news source. Many understate the tradeoffs of fighting inflation and support policies that do not always match their stated cause.

County evidence links local buying power and inflation to election results, while Fed-trust research shows that the messenger changes how much weight consumers give an inflation message.

NBER 32497 · NBER 35301 · NBER 33071

The connection

household price or pay loss → cause named and source believed → policy tradeoff judged → trust, blame, vote, or demand for action

Different explanations may reflect different real exposures, not only bad information. The price basket and the story about it must be tracked separately.

The four outside views

Household: A family compares rent, food, gas, wages, and debt with what a speaker says. Its lived bill may matter more than a national average.

Company and sector: Firms, workers, retailers, lenders, and media explain prices from different positions. Their costs and messages may point to different answers.

Money and finance: A policy that lowers inflation may also raise unemployment or borrowing costs. A household may support a cause but reject the tradeoff.

Public power: Taxes, rates, trade, aid, market rules, and public communication turn a price story into possible action. Trust affects whether it is heard.

What would change the finding

  • Household price basket, income change, debt, and local market.
  • Party, news source, messenger, repetition, and personal exposure.
  • Cause belief, policy tradeoff, trust, blame, and risk.
  • Spending, saving, borrowing, turnout, vote, and policy demand.
  • Differences by income, race, age, place, and education.
