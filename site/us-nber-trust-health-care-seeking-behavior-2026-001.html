Finding 001: Trust is a practical health-care choice currency, but not a treatment outcome

Status: bounded U.S. trust, care-seeking, and health-system finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 32028, revised in April 2026, uses a nationally representative U.S. adult survey to distinguish trust in doctors, nurses, hospitals, insurers, public-health authorities, and the health-care system overall. The study models care-seeking through perceived usefulness, expected disutility, out-of-pocket costs, and claim denials.

The authors report that higher trust is strongly associated with more retrospective and anticipated preventive and elective care-seeking and less care avoidance. The reported association is comparable in magnitude to education and income gradients. Structural estimates describe overall trust as relatively substitutable across entities but trust within a provider domain as more complementary; counterfactual losses of trust in doctors have the largest reported simulated effect. Associations vary by age and political affiliation.

The safe interpretation is:

Trust can function as a practical choice currency between a health need and a care-seeking decision, but reported trust and anticipated care are not the same as an exogenous trust shock, completed treatment, or better health.

Evidence chain

health need or care decision
  -> trust in provider, institution, insurer, or public-health authority
  -> beliefs about usefulness, disutility, cost, and denial
  -> seek, delay, or avoid preventive/elective care
  -> [open] appointment receipt, treatment completion, health, debt, later trust,
     complaint, switching, and political action

What it adds to the broader atlas

This gives the trust-and-meaning lane a measured behavioral middle. It explains why institutional presence or formal coverage need not produce usable care: trust changes the perceived value and risk of entering the route. It also prevents an overly simple trust story by separating provider, hospital, insurer, and public-health relationships and by preserving age and political heterogeneity.

What the source does not show

  • The association does not prove that changing trust caused a later care decision.
  • Anticipated or retrospective care-seeking does not establish an appointment, treatment completion, affordability, health outcome, or adequate care.
  • Trust may reflect prior experience, access, provider supply, costs, time, transportation, health status, or information.
  • Structural and counterfactual results depend on the model and do not identify the effect of a named institutional event or communication.
  • The study does not measure durable trust change, remedy, switching, or political action.

Next decisive test

Follow respondents or patients through a dated trust-relevant encounter, appointment or denial, treatment receipt, cost and time burden, health outcome, complaint or remedy, later trust, and switching or non-use. Preserve trust by entity, actual versus anticipated care, and subgroup differences; test whether the same event changes behavior after accounting for access and health need.
