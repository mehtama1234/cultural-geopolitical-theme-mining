When app convenience leaves money on the trip

Two apps can show different prices for the same ride, but the fare is only one part of what a rider buys. Time, reliability, safety, and the effort of comparison shape the real choice.

The argument

Checking takes time, attention, battery, data, and a willingness to risk losing the ride. A rider may trust one app, hold a discount there, or expect a shorter wait.

Someone carrying a child, starting a shift, or traveling somewhere unfamiliar may rationally pay more to reduce uncertainty. Repeated default choices can still give a platform power even when each single choice looks reasonable.

Customer power depends on whether the rider can see and act on the alternative before the trip is gone.

Follow one ride

A worker needs to reach a child-care pickup before closing. Uber shows $28 and Lyft shows $23. Opening the second app takes a minute, but the first warns that the fare may change and the driver may cancel. The worker chooses the first ride. The $5 difference is evidence of a trade between money, time, and uncertainty—not proof of a bad decision.

two platforms show different prices → checking takes effort → rider chooses an app → fare, wait, reliability, and safety are exchanged → repeated defaults shape competition

What the source establishes

EvidenceWhat it means
An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute gap of about $3.50, roughly 14% of the average fare.The same-looking trip can carry different displayed prices.
Device data show only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other.Many riders do not make a live comparison.
Neither app was consistently cheaper.A rider cannot rely on a simple “always choose X” rule.

Source: NBER Working Paper 34441.

The deeper finding

Convenience can be a market advantage because it removes the moment when a customer might compare. The platform may only need to be familiar, trusted, and quick enough that checking feels too costly.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show why each rider chose an app, whether the chosen ride was better, or how repeated fare gaps affect household budgets. It covers one city and a short price window.

Next test

Follow riders and matched trips for six months. Join both app prices, comparison time, wait, cancellations, reliability, trip purpose, income, safety, total cost, and switching. Measure missed work, late care, and time recovered as well as fare saved.

Reading rule

A price gap is not automatically a customer loss. Show what the rider paid for besides the fare.
