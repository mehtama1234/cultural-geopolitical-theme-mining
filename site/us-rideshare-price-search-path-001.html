The fare is only one part of the ride

The argument

Rideshare competition looks healthy when two apps show two prices. But a customer can benefit from that difference only if they can see it, compare it, and wait long enough to act. The real purchase is a bundle of fare, pickup time, chance of cancellation, account history, safety features, and the effort of checking another app.

That changes the meaning of a price gap. A rider going to dinner may compare. A nurse ending a late shift, a parent approaching a child-care closing time, or a person carrying groceries may pay the first acceptable quote because delay has a cost. The platform that becomes the default screen can keep customer attention even when it is not the cheapest option.

The deeper finding is not simply that apps charge different prices. It is that search friction can weaken competition at the point of need. When comparison costs minutes, uncertainty, and a possible missed obligation, choice exists on paper but may be hard to use in the moment. A platform can gain power from being the first place a rider opens, even when another app sometimes has the lower fare.

Follow one ride

A worker finishes a late shift. One app quotes $28 and another quotes $23. The second app is not free to check: the worker must open it, locate the trip, enter the destination, and decide whether the cheaper car will arrive soon enough. The first app may already have the pickup point, payment method, and a driver on the way. The worker accepts $28. That is not proof of exploitation. It is a decision made under a deadline.

Now repeat the decision twice a week. The extra $5 becomes about $520 a year before tips. If the worker has no record of the cheaper quote, the cost disappears into many small decisions. If enough riders behave this way, a platform can benefit from being the place people start, even when its fare is higher.

The opposite case matters too. If the cheaper app has a long wait, poor wheelchair access, weak safety support, or a history of cancellation, the higher quote may buy a real service difference. A rider may also value a familiar account, a stored destination, a clear pickup area, or a support channel that worked before. These are not excuses to ignore price. They are reasons to define the product being compared before calling one fare higher or lower.

The value of the cheaper quote depends on whether it survives the search. A rider may lose the first price while opening the other app, see a different price after returning, or find that the cheaper car is not available for a wheelchair, a child seat, or a difficult pickup point. A comparison that ignores these failures counts a saving the rider could not safely use.

The cost can also spread beyond the ride. Paying $5 more twice a week is a household expense, but avoiding a late arrival may protect a shift, a child-care arrangement, or a medical visit. A rider with transit or a car can reject both quotes; a rider without a backup route may be buying access to work or home. The same fare gap can therefore be a small convenience cost for one person and a forced access cost for another.

The budget effect becomes clearer when the choice repeats. An extra $5 twice a week is about $520 a year before tips. That amount may be small for one rider and important for another. It can become a higher rent choice, a missed meal, or less money for a repair. A rider may also choose the more expensive app because a missed shift or late pickup would cost more than the fare gap. The same observed payment can therefore mean either a price penalty or a rational purchase of reliability. We need the outcome after the ride, not just the quote before it.

  -> each app shows fare, wait, and service signals
  -> rider pays a search cost to compare them
  -> rider trades money against delay and failure risk
  -> trip is completed, cancelled, or missed
  -> repeated choices shape household cost and platform power

What the source actually establishes

Finding What it means What it does not mean
An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute price gap of about $3.50, around 14% of the average fare. The same-looking trip can produce materially different quotes. Every rider could have saved that amount.
Only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other. Live comparison is not common. We do not know whether people lacked time, trusted one app, or had another reason.
Neither app was always cheaper. There is no simple rule that solves search. The study does not measure the better pickup, driver, or safety outcome.

Source: NBER Working Paper 34441.

The deeper finding

The market has two prices: the posted fare and the price of finding a better fare. The second is paid in attention, minutes, uncertainty, and risk. It falls for people with time, multiple accounts, good phone access, and flexible plans. It rises for people who are late, caring for someone, unfamiliar with the area, or unable to tolerate a failed pickup.

That makes the default app more than a convenient screen. It is a gate to the comparison set. A rider who always opens one app may never learn whether the other was cheaper. A rider who checks both may discover a saving, but may also lose the first quote, wait longer, or choose a car with a worse fit. The value of comparison depends on the clock and on what can go wrong.

There is also a connection to transportation access. For a person with a car, transit, or a second app, a higher fare may be avoidable. For a person leaving work in a place with weak transit, rideshare may be the only route to a job, home, food, or care. The same app behavior can therefore have a different effect across households. What looks like a small convenience fee can become an access fee when there is no backup route.

The important market signal is not only the size of the fare gap. It is whether riders can act on it without losing a necessary trip. If they cannot, two prices do not create two usable choices. The competition is real at the app level but weaker at the moment the customer must decide.

This is why convenience can become a form of market power. A platform does not need to win every comparison. It can win by making comparison feel impractical at the exact moment the customer needs the service.

What remains unknown

The study does not show why each rider chose an app, whether the chosen trip was safer or faster, or how fare gaps affect budgets over time. It covers one city and a limited period. It does not show whether riders could switch without losing a quote or whether the cheaper option was actually available to the rider. It also does not test whether a comparison tool would save money without increasing cancellation, wait, privacy, or account costs.

Next test

Track matched trips for six months across late-work, care, airport, medical, and ordinary errands. Record both quotes, quote expiration, pickup time, wait, cancellation, completed-trip time, tip, service quality, trip purpose, income, phone access, transit alternatives, and whether the rider checked both apps. Report three outcomes separately: dollars saved, time lost, and obligations protected, such as arriving at work or collecting a child on time. Then test whether a comparison tool changes the result for people with flexible trips versus people with no backup route.

For each ride, record the outcome after booking: whether the quoted fare held, whether the driver arrived, how long the rider waited, whether the rider was late, and what backup was used. Compare people with flexible trips against people with a hard deadline and no other route. This separates a possible saving on the screen from a saving that a household could actually keep.

Reading rule

A fare gap is evidence of a possible saving, not proof of customer harm. Show what the rider paid for besides the fare.
