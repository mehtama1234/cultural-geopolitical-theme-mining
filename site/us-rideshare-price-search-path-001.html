Connected US finding · rideshare price and choice

When app convenience leaves money on the trip

Two apps can show different prices for the same ride while most riders check only one. The fare gap is only part of the customer’s cost.

The argument

A rider carrying a child, starting a shift, or traveling somewhere unsafe may pay more to reduce uncertainty. Habit, trust, discounts, wait time, and reliability can be worth more than the lowest displayed fare.

Show what the rider paid for besides the fare.

What the source establishes

An NBER audit of 2,238 matched New York City trips found an average absolute price gap of about $3.50, roughly 14% of the average fare. Only about 16% of people who opened one app also opened the other, and neither app was consistently cheaper.

The study covers one city and a short window. It does not show each rider’s income, purpose, quality, final cost, or reason for choosing an app.

NBER Working Paper 34441

Follow the ride choice

different app prices → comparison takes effort → rider chooses an app → fare, wait, reliability, and safety are exchanged → money and time remain

A higher fare may buy a shorter wait, trusted driver, discount, or fewer cancellations. Calling every gap a loss ignores what the rider bought.

Keep these apart

  • Posted fare and final fare.
  • Fare and total trip cost.
  • Comparison effort and preference.
  • Price and service quality.

Next test

Follow riders for six months through both app prices, comparison, wait, cancellations, purpose, income, safety, total cost, and switching.

Reading rule

A price gap is not automatically a customer loss.
