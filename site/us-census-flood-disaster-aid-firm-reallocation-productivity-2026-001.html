Finding 001: Disaster finance can redirect firm survival and productivity

Status: bounded U.S. disaster, firm, and public-finance finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

The Census CES working paper studies large federally declared floods using plant-level Census data, business credit, and county-income measures. It reports that shutdowns increased after floods and were concentrated among the least productive plants. Machinery released by exiting plants was acquired by more productive entrants, while surviving plants upgraded machinery and productivity. The paper also reports expanded bank financing, particularly for young and small firms, after declared disasters.

The recorded estimates are a 3.6-percentage-point increase in shutdown within two years, more than 11% higher survivor productivity by the sixth year, about 14% higher productivity among post-flood entrants, and about a 3.1% increase in bank loans. In the paper's comparison, severe non-declared floods were associated with about a 0.73% aggregate TFP decline and a 0.9% per-capita income decline, while the declared-disaster income estimate was suggestively positive by about 0.7%.

The safe interpretation is:

Public disaster finance can change the selection and financing of firms after a flood, potentially turning destruction into productivity-enhancing reallocation; firm productivity and county income are not the same as worker continuity, household recovery, or universal local benefit.

Evidence chain

large flood
  -> federal declaration and disaster spending
  -> credit access, plant exit, and machinery reallocation
  -> survivor and entrant productivity; county-income recovery
  -> [open] worker continuity, firm ownership, customer/service continuity,
     household recovery, trust, and political action

What it adds to the broader atlas

This adds a firm-and-public-finance realization stage to the disaster lane. It shows why an emergency program should not be judged only by dollars announced or aggregate recovery: finance can affect which organizations remain, which machines move, and whether new capacity enters. It also supplies a counterfactual boundary: severe non-declared floods show weaker productivity selection and worse reported aggregate outcomes in the study setting.

What the source does not show

  • A plant shutdown is not a worker-level job-loss, relocation, or welfare measure.
  • Higher survivor or entrant productivity does not establish better wages, job quality, local ownership, service continuity, or fair distribution.
  • County per-capita income does not show that every resident recovered or that housing, health, insurance, or social ties were restored.
  • The results focus on infrequent inland floods and are not a universal result for repeated coastal disasters or every sector.
  • The modeled fiscal return does not prove realized tax collection or that aid is always self-financing.

Next decisive test

Link disaster declarations and firm finance to worker earnings, layoffs, re-employment, ownership, relocation, housing and insurance continuity, local service availability, and household recovery. Separate incumbent workers, new entrants, displaced residents, surviving firms, and firms that exit; then test whether productivity reallocation translates into durable local capacity, trust, or political response.
