When household choices become a judgment about the country

A family can manage its own bills and still believe the national economy is going badly. “My family is okay” and “the country is failing” answer different questions.

The argument

A household may switch brands, delay a car purchase, save less, and work an extra shift while still saying it is doing okay. The adjustment can feel fragile, and that feeling may become a national judgment before it becomes a clear vote.

Household action, economic judgment, and political response are three separate steps.

What the sources establish

The Federal Reserve says 77% of adults took at least one action after prices rose in 2025. In the same survey, 73% said they were doing okay or living comfortably, while only about one-fourth rated the national economy good or excellent.

Pew reports low federal-government trust with a large party gap. NBER studies connect a Child Tax Credit change to sentiment and county real-wage changes to later voting patterns. These are different units and outcomes, not one causal chain.

Federal Reserve · Pew · NBER sentiment · NBER real wages and voting

Follow the three steps

price, bill, wage, or benefit change → household switches, delays, saves less, borrows, or works more → personal judgment → national judgment filtered through party, place, media, and expectations → demand for relief, blame, or political change

A changed shopping choice is not a changed political belief. A person may be angry and not vote, vote on another issue, or keep a party identity through a bad month.

Keep these apart

  • Household action and household loss.
  • Personal well-being and national opinion.
  • Trust and sentiment.
  • County voting and an individual voter’s reason.

Next test

Use Fed SHED files to cross-tab price actions with personal and national views by income, age, race, disability, and place. Test real buying power against turnout and vote choice only after accounting for party identity and local information.

Reading rule
