Finding 029: Work location is not schedule control

Status: provisional BLS ATUS work-location and time-architecture finding · Checked: 2026-09-14

The bounded finding

The BLS 2025 American Time Use Survey shows that work location is a real distributional surface, but it is not the same thing as schedule autonomy or time returned to households. On an average day that they worked, 35% of employed people did some or all work at home and 70% did some or all work at their workplace. The categories overlap: a person can work in both locations on the same day.

Among employed people age 15 and over, BLS reports 109.667 million who worked on an average day. Of those, 69.8% worked at their workplace for an average of 7.87 hours there, while 34.5% worked at home for an average of 5.10 hours at home. The location pattern differs materially: women worked at home on 38.2% of days they worked versus 31.3% for men; among workers age 25 and over, 51% of those with a bachelor's degree or higher worked at home compared with 19% of high-school graduates with no college.

The defensible interpretation is: home-based work changes where paid work is performed, but the published table does not establish shorter total work, greater control, less care conflict, lower surveillance, or improved wellbeing.

These are published annual averages and subgroup comparisons, not a panel of the same people. The location columns are not additive because the home and workplace indicators allow both locations on one day. The time-use categories are primary activities unless explicitly identified as secondary childcare.

Why this matters for the end-to-end program

employer rule and job fit
  -> location permission and commute requirement
  -> total hours, care coordination, monitoring, and interruption
  -> money, rest, health, family security, and social availability
  -> retention, voice, trust, and political meaning

The ATUS table populates location and selected time-allocation nodes. It does not observe the employer rule, the worker's preferred arrangement, interruptions while working at home, total weekly workload, compensation, health, or later institutional response. It therefore complements the AI work-control records and the worker/workplace event ledger, but cannot substitute for them.

Counterexamples kept visible

  • Working at home can reduce commuting while increasing availability demands, surveillance, care conflict, or unpaid work around the paid job.
  • Working at the workplace can coexist with high schedule control, supportive coworkers, and protected time; location alone does not rank job quality.
  • Higher home-working prevalence among college graduates may reflect occupation, industry, earnings, or job selection rather than education causing autonomy.
  • A lower average of primary paid work between annual samples does not establish that home work reduced hours; the samples are separate and the 2025 release warns that a federal shutdown removed interviews from September 30 through November 11, 2025.
  • Primary childcare and secondary childcare are different measures. Less primary childcare does not prove less care, because care can occur while working, doing household activities, or engaging in leisure.

Next same-unit test

The next acquisition should join a named worker or household to an employer rule or work-location change and preserve, in time order:

  1. location and commute days;
  2. scheduled and actual hours, interruptions, and monitoring;
  3. primary and secondary care, household labor, and rest;
  4. pay, occupation, employer permission, and representation;
  5. health, satisfaction, retention, switching, or exit; and
  6. household security, trust, and later collective or political response.

The decisive countercells are home-based/high-control, home-based/low-control, workplace-based/high-control, and workplace-based/low-control workers. The published ATUS tables establish why those cells should not be collapsed.
