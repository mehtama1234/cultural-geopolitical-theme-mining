Recurring payment can turn inattention into a consumer-exit problem

Status: provisional case finding · Checked: 2026-09-17

The FTC's May 2026 Shutterstock case makes the subscription question concrete. The Commission alleged that the company failed to clearly disclose renewal and cancellation terms, charged consumers without express informed consent, and made cancellation difficult. The proposed settlement provides $35 million for consumer relief and requires clearer material-term disclosure, express consent, and simple cancellation mechanisms.

subscription or content pack
  -> renewal term, charge, or cancellation fee is not understood
  -> attention or support contact is required to stop payment
  -> continued payment, cancellation effort, relief, or switching
  -> household room, trust, and seller/consumer power

The case closes the institutional-response arrow, but only partly closes the consumer arrow. It gives us a named mechanism and proposed controls; it does not give us a representative denominator, an observed unwanted-renewal rate, the time or money burden per customer, or proof that each continuing customer wanted to stop.

What the case contributes

Layer Evidence Boundary
Product design FTC allegations concern annual paid-monthly plans and on-demand packs, including renewal and early-cancellation terms The public announcement does not provide account-level exposure or current-flow telemetry
Exit Before 2024, the FTC says early cancellation could require phone, chat, or email support; the complaint also alleges difficult-to-find terms A burdensome route is not the same as an impossible exit, and the case does not report completion rates
Remedy Proposed $35M consumer relief and requirements for disclosure, consent, and simple cancellation Proposed controls do not establish implementation, payment receipt, or recurrence prevention
Household effect The mechanism is plausibly connected to recurring cost and attention/time The case does not measure budget strain, overdrafts, missed essentials, or trust change

The key distinction

Three states must remain separate:

  1. Continued payment: the account renewed.
  2. Unwanted payment: the customer says the renewal was not desired or understood.
  3. Failed practical exit: the customer attempted to stop, but could not do so without material time, money, access, or support cost.

The FTC case is evidence about the design and governance of the route between these states. It is not a national estimate of any of them. Automatic renewal can also be useful when it preserves software, news, food, care, or other service continuity for a customer who knowingly wants it.

Broad-program connection

This is a small but important bridge between the culture/attention and consumer-power themes. Attention is not only time spent on a platform. It can also be the scarce attention required to monitor recurring charges, remember terms, find a cancellation route, contact support, and verify that billing stopped. A seller can therefore capture value from both the service and the customer's failure to notice or act, while the public remedy attempts to restore informed choice.

The next high-value evidence is implementation depth: a signed final order, refund or relief receipt, current enrollment and cancellation screens, support contact burden, repeat complaints, and account-level outcomes. Those should be paired with the NBER payment-card mechanism, but not merged into a single loss rate. The strongest comparison would preserve plan type, renewal notice, customer intent, attempted exit, realized charge, relief, continued use, and switching.
