Finding 004: Disaster recovery can have money and projects without proving that a community recovered

Status: bounded public-capacity and disaster-recovery finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The simple finding

The federal government can put billions into disaster recovery and coordinate missions, while the public record still cannot show clearly whether local businesses reopened, jobs returned, services worked, or families became secure.

GAO reports that the Economic Development Administration awarded about $2.1 billion for disaster recovery in fiscal years 2014–2024 and coordinated 45 Economic Recovery Support Function missions from 2013–2025. Nearly 45% of award dollars went to utilities, roads, and other public infrastructure; 36% went to business, medical, training, and other facilities. These are real public inputs and projects, but they are not the same thing as a working local economy.

Among 292 construction and revolving-loan grants with available three-year reports, 55% of grantees reported no jobs created or retained and no private investment. GAO warns that these reports were not reliable enough to estimate economic effects. This is not a 55% failure rate for all disaster grants. It is evidence that the measurement system cannot cleanly tell us what happened.

The route under test

disaster
  -> federal appropriation and coordination
  -> award, disbursement, and project
  -> working road, utility, facility, firm, job, and service
  -> household security, return, trust, or political response

The evidence is strongest at the first three steps. The later steps are only partly measured. Hawaii stakeholders also told GAO that recovery planning and implementation suffered when supplemental funds arrived about two years after the disaster. That is a selected account, not a national delay rate, but it shows why an appropriation is not the same as timely help.

What this adds to the broader atlas

This is the community-scale version of a recurring pattern:

A system can show capacity, spending, coordination, and visible activity while leaving the practical result—who can use what, when, and for how long—uncertain.

It links disaster exposure to infrastructure, firms, work, housing, and public trust without pretending that these sources follow the same household or community from the disaster to the later outcome. Some projects may have worked and some benefits may be missing from the measures. The point is that the public record cannot yet separate those possibilities well.

Limits and next test

  • The record is about EDA institutions, grants, missions, and reporting; it is not a resident or firm panel.
  • Awards and appropriations are not the same as disbursement, completion, service use, business survival, or household recovery.
  • The 55% figure covers 292 grants with available reports and must not be read as a portfolio failure rate.
  • The source does not establish causal effects, a complete place comparison, or changes in trust and political action.

The next useful test is a place-keyed ledger that joins disaster date, appropriation, award, disbursement, project completion, service availability, firm and job outcomes, housing, migration, and resident access—while keeping government, firm, worker, household, and resident units separate.
