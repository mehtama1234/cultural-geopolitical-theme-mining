Equal data access can open the market and weaken the data supply

The argument

When a platform controls data that sellers need, equal access may help outside sellers compete. But if data sharing is cheap or compulsory, the platform may have less reason to gather, clean, and update the data. Customers can then face a trade: more sellers and less gatekeeper power, but stale data, longer search, or worse matches.

This is not a choice between sharing data and protecting the platform. The useful questions are who gets access, what it costs, how accurate the data are, who can correct them, what the platform still invests, and what the customer receives at the end.

The deeper finding is that data access is market design. Opening a data service changes both competition around the data and the incentive to produce the data in the first place.

Data is not one thing. A seller may need product search, demand, price, inventory, review, delivery, customer, or complaint data. Giving access to one kind can help entry without giving away private customer information. Giving all of it to every rival can create new privacy and security risks. “Equal access” therefore needs a boundary: equal to which data, in what form, at what time, and for what use?

Follow one product search

A seller needs platform data to price and place a product. An equal-access rule opens the data to rivals. Entry rises, but the platform invests less in collection and updates. The data become stale or costly. Customers see more sellers but spend longer searching, receive less useful offers, or pay more for a poor match.

The reverse can also happen. The platform keeps strong incentives to improve data but uses control to favor its own products or charge rivals more. Customers get accurate results but fewer independent paths. Neither outcome can be assumed from the rule’s name.

The data may be valuable because it is timely, not merely because it is large. A seller can price a product differently when inventory is low, demand spikes, or delivery is delayed. A stale feed can create canceled orders, wasted stock, or higher prices. A customer may see more sellers and still receive a worse result if the information no longer describes what can actually be bought.

Who can correct the data matters as much as who can read it. A seller may be wrongly labeled late, a customer may be assigned to the wrong segment, or a product may be matched to the wrong category. If the platform controls the record and the appeal, equal access to a bad record can spread the error. The customer and seller need a way to see the basis for an important decision and fix it without revealing more personal data than needed.

What the sources actually establish

Evidence Meaning Limit
HBS reports more sharing after privacy rules in one receipt-and-reward app. Data supply begins with customer permission. It does not measure seller competition.
NBER’s model says equal data and equal data prices can open competition. Access can reduce a gatekeeper’s advantage. The result is modeled, not observed in one named market.
The model says equal access may reduce the platform’s reason to produce or improve data. Opening access can weaken investment. The size of the effect depends on industry design.
The model says customer welfare need not rise in every case. More competition is not the only outcome. It does not identify the best rule for every platform.

Sources: NBER Data Neutrality, Data Supply, and Market Competition and HBS More Trust, More Data.

The deeper finding

The customer result requires four measures together: seller entry, data quality, platform investment, and final price or fit. Privacy adds a fifth question: whether customers knowingly supplied the data that made the market work.

This is why a simple “open” versus “closed” debate misses the hard part. An open system can have poor data. A closed system can have excellent data and weak seller power. The public choice is about the full chain, including correction rights, access fees, updates, and who bears the cost when the data are wrong.

The deeper power is the ability to turn activity into a private map of the market. The platform can see what customers search for, which offers lose, where sellers run out of stock, and which complaints repeat. A rival may compete on the shelf without seeing why the shelf behaves that way. If access is denied, the platform can shape the market without challenge. If access is forced without limits, the data can be copied without preserving the work or privacy that produced them.

This also connects to the household. Customers supply clicks, receipts, location, reviews, returns, and support messages. Those traces can improve matching, but they can also reveal income, health, family structure, or urgent need. A better market for sellers is not automatically a better bargain for the people whose behavior produced the data. Consent, purpose, deletion, and correction are part of the exchange.

The political question is who owns the ability to know what is happening. A platform may call data a private asset, while sellers and public regulators may need it to test fair competition. Customers may have a right to privacy but little ability to bargain over collection. A workable rule must decide which data are necessary for competition, which must stay protected, and who pays for a trusted shared record.

What remains unknown

We do not yet have a named US platform with measured access prices, update rates, collection and maintenance spending, seller outcomes, privacy choices, and customer welfare before and after a rule. The model does not predict one automatic result for every industry.

We also do not know whether sellers would build the same data service themselves, whether shared access improves product quality, or whether customers would choose to provide less data if the benefit were made clear. A change in search time or price could come from data quality, ranking, advertising, inventory, or demand. Those causes need to be separated.

Next test

Choose one platform and measure data-access fees, quality, collection and update spending, seller entry, prices, variety, search time, privacy choices, and customer outcomes. Include small sellers and customers with few alternatives.

Create a data ledger for each market: what was collected, who could see it, when it was updated, how errors were corrected, what access cost, and what decision used it. Compare closed access, equal read access, shared data with privacy limits, and independent audit. Measure seller entry, margins, product availability, delivery accuracy, customer search time, price, returns, data supplied, deletion requests, and complaint resolution. The useful result is a market that remains competitive without making the customer pay for stale information or unchosen surveillance.

Reading rule

Do not call equal data access good or bad until you measure seller entry, data quality, platform investment, and the customer’s final result together.
