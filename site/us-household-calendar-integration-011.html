Eldercare often reaches beyond the provider’s household, but the roster does not measure burden

Status: checked · Checked: 2026-09-13

One-sentence finding

In separate 2024 and 2025 ATUS annual samples, roughly four in five eldercare provider respondents had at least one listed non-household recipient, while the roster averaged about 1.3–1.4 listed recipients per provider; this establishes the social reach of care, not its intensity, cost, or displacement.

What we directly know

Annual sample Provider respondents / roster records Mean listed recipients Household recipient Non-household recipient
2024 1,319 / 1,738 1.326 22.7% 79.0%
2025 1,016 / 1,429 1.434 21.3% 80.0%

The indicators are respondent-weighted at the provider level. A provider can have more than one listed recipient, and a provider can have both household and non-household recipients, so the two recipient percentages are not intended to sum to 100%.

How the result may matter

care need beyond one household
  -> coordination across household boundaries
  -> time, travel, money, and scheduling may be distributed across providers
  -> work, health, family routine, or social availability may be affected

The first two arrows are supported as a measurement structure: the roster records recipient relationship categories and multiplicity. The final arrow is an open hypothesis. The roster does not record enough to say that non-household care reduced work, increased travel, changed trust, or produced political action.

Why this is useful to the end-to-end program

This adds a boundary to the care/time chain. Care is not necessarily contained within the provider’s household, so a household-only resource or expenditure frame can miss the coordination network. It also supplies a counterweight to a simple “care equals time in the home” interpretation: the observed social reach could coexist with shared support, low intensity, or substantial strain.

Limits and what would change the interpretation

  • 2024 and 2025 are separate annual ATUS samples, not repeated providers.
  • The roster identifies listed recipients, not care hours, distance, expenses, relationship quality, or recipient condition.
  • Recipient records are not independently weighted; provider-level indicators use the respondent final weight.
  • Household and non-household indicators overlap when a provider reports both.
  • No causal trigger, work counterfactual, employer accommodation, health outcome, recovery window, trust measure, or political-action measure is present.

A stronger end-to-end result would require a repeated provider or household unit with a dated care change, recipient need, paid/unpaid time, travel, schedule control, work/health outcome, and follow-up interpretation. Evidence that non-household providers maintain work and health through shared support would be a meaningful counterexample to a burden interpretation.
