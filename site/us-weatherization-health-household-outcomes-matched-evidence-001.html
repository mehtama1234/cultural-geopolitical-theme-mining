A short argument · US weatherization, bills, and health

Efficiency savings are real but smaller than the promise

Weatherization can lower energy use and improve safety, but projected savings can overstate what households receive.

The argument

A successful upgrade must be judged by actual bills, safe temperature, air quality, health, and household cash—not by projected savings alone.

Follow one upgraded home

A low-income family receives weatherization. The bill falls, but the room is still cold because the family cannot afford enough heat, or a piece of equipment remains broken. Lower use is useful, but it does not tell us whether the home became safer.

weatherization work → actual energy use and bill → temperature, air quality, safety, and comfort → health, work, care, and household cash → future repair and housing choices

The deeper finding

Lower measured energy use does not automatically mean a household is warmer or healthier. The family may be using less because the home improved, or because it still cannot afford enough heat.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not join one home's bills, temperatures, air quality, safety, health, work, care, and later housing choices.

Next test

Join same-home bills, temperatures, air quality, equipment, health, missed work, care, shutoff, and housing records before and after work. Compare predicted and actual savings.

Reading rule

Do not call weatherization successful from a modelled saving. Show the bill, room, air, safety result, and later household choices.
