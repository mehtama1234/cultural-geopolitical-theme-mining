Connected US finding · weatherization and household health

Efficiency savings are real but smaller than the promise

Weatherization can lower energy use and improve safety and comfort. A projected saving is not the same as a safer, warmer home with more money left over.

The argument

The same decline in energy use can mean an improved building, continued heat rationing, changed equipment, or less time at home. Temperature, air, safety, health, and work records give the number meaning.

An upgrade must be judged by what the household actually receives.

Follow one home through winter

A renter lives in a drafty apartment. Weatherization seals leaks and checks the furnace. The bill falls, but the family may still use less heat than it needs. Or the home becomes warmer and the parent misses less work. The energy number alone cannot tell these stories.

The deeper finding

Lower measured use does not automatically mean a healthier home. The real result has at least five parts: bill, safe temperature, air and equipment safety, health, and money left for other needs.

What remains unknown

The evidence does not join same-home bills, temperatures, air quality, equipment, health events, missed work, care, shutoffs, and housing stability before and after work.

Next test

Follow renters, owners, manufactured homes, rural homes, and low-income households through two heating and cooling seasons. Join work, bill, temperature, air quality, safety, health, work, care, shutoff, rent, and debt.

Reading rule

Do not call weatherization successful from a modeled saving. Show the bill, the room, the air, the safety result, and the household’s later choices.
