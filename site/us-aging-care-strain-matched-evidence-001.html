Matched evidence · US aging, care, and work

Unpaid eldercare can become a second job before it becomes a bill.

Families supply a large amount of care without a direct care payment. The cost may appear as lost earnings, less saving, or less time rather than as a care charge.

In one sentence

Unpaid care is real labor, but its full cost is often hidden in the caregiver’s work and household budget.

Care scale is not the same as care burden for every person.

The connection

need for care → unpaid family time or paid care
              → work, earnings, health, savings, or housing change
              → family and employer response
              → public support or political demand

BLS shows the size and timing of unpaid care. NBER connects caregiving to earnings and employment. Neither tells us the complete balance of wages lost, paid care avoided, health, and the older person’s outcome.

The hidden second job

Unpaid care includes meals, transport, grooming, companionship, and being available. Some replaces a paid service. Some adds to a worker’s day. The cost may appear as fewer hours, job exit, less saving, a delayed move, or exhaustion.

Four views

Household

Count care hours, paid care avoided, work lost, savings used, and housing changes.

Customer

Compare paid care for price, reliability, schedule, quality, and access.

Money and finance

Separate lost earnings, unpaid time, tax relief, benefits, and retirement effects.

Public power

Ask whether policy pays the caregiver, funds service, protects leave, or assumes family capacity.

Next test

Join care days with work hours, earnings, leave, health, savings, and housing. Compare unpaid care with paid relief and ask who can buy back the time.

Reading rule

Do not call unpaid care free. State who provides the time, what work or money changes, and what care the older person receives.
