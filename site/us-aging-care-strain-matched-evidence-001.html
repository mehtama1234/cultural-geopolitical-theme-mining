Connected US finding · eldercare and work

Unpaid eldercare can become a second job before it becomes a bill

A worker may provide rides, medicine help, food, forms, and supervision. The care is real even when no care invoice appears.

The argument

Family time can protect an older person while reducing a caregiver’s work hours, savings, health, and future choices. Women are more represented among unpaid providers, and NBER finds earnings and employment declines after caregiving begins for women.

Unpaid care is a transfer from one person’s future to another person’s present.

Follow one caregiver

An employee’s parent needs help several times a week. The employee uses leave, changes shifts, turns down overtime, or leaves a distant job. The family saves paid-care costs, but the worker loses earnings and retirement room. When the parent’s health worsens, the arrangement may fail suddenly.

need for care → unpaid family time or paid care → work, earnings, health, savings, or housing change → family and employer response → public support or greater dependence

The deeper finding

The relevant measure is not only hours given. It is the options lost: a shift, promotion, job change, retirement contribution, rest, or move. A tax credit may offset cash while leaving time and coordination untouched.

What remains unknown

The sources do not give the complete balance of paid care avoided, wages lost, health effects, savings, and the older person’s result for the same family.

Next test

Link caregiver and recipient records around the same event. Measure tasks, hours, distance, schedule, paid replacement cost, leave, earnings, health, savings, housing, and recipient outcomes. Test tax credits, paid leave, respite, and home services.

Reading rule

Do not call unpaid care free. State who provides the time, what work or money changes, and what care the older person receives.
