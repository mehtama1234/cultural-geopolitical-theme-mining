A short argument · US eldercare, work, and family money

Unpaid eldercare can become a second job before it becomes a bill

The US often pays for eldercare with family time before it pays for it with money.

The argument

A worker may drive a parent to appointments, manage medicines, prepare food, handle forms, or stay nearby. The care is real, but its cost may show up as fewer work hours, lower savings, or exhaustion.

Follow one working caregiver

An employee's parent needs help several times a week. The employee uses leave, changes shifts, turns down overtime, or leaves a job with a long commute. The family saves the price of paid care, but the worker loses earnings and future retirement savings.

need for care → unpaid family time or paid care → work, earnings, health, savings, or housing change → family and employer response → public support or dependence on relatives

The deeper finding

Unpaid care is a transfer from one household member's future to another's present. It can protect an older person and avoid a paid bill, but the price may be a missed shift, a smaller pension, worse health, or a delayed housing move.

What remains unproven

BLS and NBER do not give the complete balance of paid care avoided, wages lost, health effects, savings, and the older person's result for the same family.

Next test

Link caregiver and recipient records around the same event. Measure tasks, hours, distance, schedule, paid replacement cost, leave, earnings, health, savings, housing, and recipient outcomes.

Reading rule

Do not call unpaid care free. State who provides the time, what work or money changes, and what care the older person receives.
