GAO 2026 disaster-assistance scams, information, and trust layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: US Government Accountability Office, GAO-26-108280 Reference: disaster-assistance scam education, reporting, and enforcement Status: consumer-protection and information-risk finding; not a scam prevalence or loss estimate

Why this update matters

Disaster recovery has an information problem in addition to a money and housing problem. A survivor must identify the legitimate agency, understand what it is asking for, protect personal information, and decide whether reporting a suspicious interaction is worth the effort. The same urgency that makes assistance valuable can make deception more effective.

This layer adds a consumer/trust mechanism to the FEMA and EDA recovery chain: public aid does not enter an empty information environment. It enters a moment of fear, urgency, disrupted communication, and uneven institutional reach.

Recorded findings

Surface Result Boundary
Exposure Disaster-assistance scams target survivors and people trying to help them, using deception or manipulation for financial gain The report does not estimate the number exposed or victimized
Prevalence Prevalence is unknown; complaint data and surveys suggest many people encounter scams during disaster recovery Encounter is not victimization, financial loss, or successful fraud
Loss measurement Agency data limitations prevent accurate estimates of how often scams occur or how much money is lost Unknown is an evidence boundary, not evidence of low harm
Underreporting Shame, embarrassment, belief that reporting will not help, and lack of awareness about reporting can suppress reports The relative contribution of each reason is not quantified
Prevention channels Agencies use recovery-center information booths, press releases, and emergency-app push notifications to raise awareness Education availability is not message receipt, comprehension, or behavior change
Enforcement friction Time and resource constraints, case prioritization, and sophisticated international operations hinder prosecution Enforcement constraints do not establish that agencies failed to investigate a specific case
Institutional surface FEMA, SBA, HUD, the National Center for Disaster Fraud, and other agencies receive or process relevant complaints A complaint route is not a complete denominator of survivors or interactions
Compounding loss Scams can add financial and nonfinancial losses to disaster damage and may increase as disasters become more frequent The report does not estimate the incremental loss attributable to scams

The central tension

The public system asks people to act quickly while the information environment becomes harder to verify. A survivor may need to share identity or property information, pay for urgent repairs, accept temporary housing, or navigate a new application. A scammer can imitate an agency, contractor, charity, lender, or recovery helper. The practical choice is therefore not simply “apply or do not apply”; it includes whether the channel appears legitimate and whether a person can afford to pause and verify it.

disaster loss and urgency
  -> need for rapid aid, repairs, donations, or information
  -> disrupted channels and uncertainty about legitimate actors
  -> scam exposure, avoidance, payment, or information disclosure
  -> added financial/privacy stress and possible distrust
  -> reporting, investigation, education, or withdrawal from help-seeking

Every arrow needs a different observation. A warning sign at a recovery center shows institutional intent. A received and understood warning shows reach and legibility. A changed decision shows prevention. A complaint shows reporting, not the full exposed population. A prosecution or refund shows an institutional response, not necessarily restored household security.

What this changes in the broader trend map

This finding links disaster assistance to consumer power, digital access, privacy, trust, and practical exit. It also provides a counterexample to a simple “more information solves fraud” story: education can be offered while people still cannot safely verify a message, lack time to investigate, fear embarrassment, or believe reporting will not help.

It creates a feedback possibility. If a survivor encounters a fraudulent or confusing aid channel, the experience may reduce use of legitimate aid, charitable help, contractors, insurers, or digital services. That behavioral response should be measured directly; it cannot be inferred from a complaint count or from general distrust.

What remains unproven

GAO does not provide a representative survivor-level rate of scam exposure, successful victimization, money lost, identity compromise, reporting, or recovery. It does not establish that education reduces losses, that one channel is safer than another, or that scam exposure causes distrust or non-use of legitimate assistance.

The report also does not identify how risk differs by age, disability, rurality, language, internet access, housing status, immigration status, or prior experience with public agencies. Those are plausible segmentation dimensions, not findings from this layer.

Next test

Create a small, privacy-preserving assistance-channel ledger for selected disasters that records message source, channel, language, timing, requested information or payment, verification opportunity, warning exposure and comprehension, suspicious-contact report, agency response, loss or no loss, and later legitimate-aid use. Link only where lawful and consented; do not retain unnecessary identity data.

Compare recovery-center, phone, web, app, contractor, insurer, and nonprofit routes by reach, verification burden, reporting friction, response time, and protected outcome. The key test is whether a person can recognize and use a legitimate remedy without giving up privacy or abandoning assistance—not the number of warnings issued.
