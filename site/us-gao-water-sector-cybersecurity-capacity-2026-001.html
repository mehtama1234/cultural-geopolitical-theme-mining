Finding 001: Water-sector connectivity creates a cyber-to-service capacity gap

Status: bounded critical-infrastructure and public-capacity finding · Checked: 2026-09-23

The bounded finding

GAO's May 2026 testimony describes cybersecurity risks across the roughly 170,000 water and wastewater systems that make up the U.S. sector. Operational technologies controlling valves, pumps, and other physical devices are increasingly connected to internet-enabled devices. That connectivity can make remote operation useful across large and distributed systems, but it also gives online attackers a path toward physical infrastructure.

GAO identifies an uneven capacity problem rather than a single sector-wide failure. Systems vary in cybersecurity capability and face workforce shortages, older technology that is difficult to update, and limited financial resources. Meeting clean-water regulatory requirements can compete with cybersecurity investment. A successful attack could disrupt service and harm public health or the environment, but the testimony does not estimate the probability or size of that outcome.

The federal response is also intermediate. After a prior GAO review, EPA conducted a sector risk assessment and developed a risk-management plan. EPA also identified gaps in cybersecurity risk-assessment requirements for wastewater and certain drinking-water systems, along with limitations in its legal authority. GAO will continue monitoring the response.

The route is:

internet-connected operational technology
  -> remote access and cyber exposure
  -> uneven operator, workforce, legacy-system, and funding capacity
  -> attack or prevention attempt [not measured here]
  -> water/wastewater continuity and public-health consequence
  -> federal, state, local, and operator response

What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a concrete infrastructure realization layer. Existing water records can show access, construction, maintenance, or resilience conditions; this record adds how digital control systems make cybersecurity a prerequisite for physical service continuity and how legal authority can lag technical exposure.

The next decisive artifact is a named water system followed through identified control exposure, mitigation investment, incident or near miss, service continuity, public communication, household burden, and post-incident repair.

Limits and counterevidence

  • The testimony is based partly on an earlier GAO review and follow-up materials; it is not a representative incident-rate study.
  • Sector size is not a count of systems with the same exposure or capability.
  • A vulnerability or legal gap does not establish an attack, outage, health effect, or operator failure.
  • EPA's risk assessment, management plan, CISA partnership, and possible future legal action may reduce risks; implementation and outcome remain open.
  • The record does not show which households or communities bear the greatest exposure, price, waiting, or recovery burden.
