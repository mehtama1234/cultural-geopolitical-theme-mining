Matched evidence · US benefits and work

Earning more does not always mean having more room.

A wage can rise while food, health, child care, or other help falls away. But leaving a program does not prove that a family became worse off.

In one sentence

The useful measure is the family's total resources after wages, taxes, benefits, and necessary work costs are counted.

Program exit is not the same thing as progress or loss.

The connection

earnings rise → aid or coverage changes → necessary costs change
              → real gain or loss in household room
              → work choice and family strain
              → trust in programs and public demand

The evidence supports parts of this path in different settings. It does not support treating a program exit as proof of a better life.

The accounting problem

Cash wages are easy to see. The value of food, Medicaid, child care, transport help, and time spent dealing with a rule is harder to put in the same account. A family may work more and still lose room if support falls too quickly.

Four views

Household

Count wages, taxes, benefits, care, health, transport, debt, and time.

Customer

Record whether the family can use the benefit and what proof it requires.

Money and finance

Calculate net cash and necessary costs—not only the wage increase.

Public power

Ask who set the threshold and whether it produces work, exit, or lost help.

Next test

Compare the same household’s wages, taxes, benefits, care costs, health coverage, transport, and work over time.

Reading rule

Do not call a program exit progress or a wage increase a gain until you count the help lost and the new costs required to earn it.
