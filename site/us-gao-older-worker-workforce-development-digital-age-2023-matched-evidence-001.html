Older workers can reach job programs without getting the same usable next step

The deeper finding

People age 55 and older made up about 22%—769,000 people—of participants in six selected federal workforce-development programs in program year 2023. After exiting selected Department of Labor programs, about 60% of older adults reported having a job in the second quarter, compared with 69% of people ages 40–54 and 73% of people ages 16–39. In the selected vocational rehabilitation program, the comparable figures were about 50%, 54%, and 58%.

Older participants also used training less often. For example, 23% of older WIOA Adult participants used training compared with 45% of younger adults, and 14% versus 25% in WIOA Dislocated Worker. Forty-one older workers interviewed at 10 centers in five states described real benefits from digital-literacy and employment services, but also difficulty with applications, new skills, few openings, and possible age discrimination.

older worker seeks help
  -> job-center access and eligibility
  -> career service, training, or digital support
  -> exit from services
  -> job or no job
  -> [open] job quality, earnings, retention, household security, retirement

What this adds to the broader atlas

This adds an aging-workforce version of the formal-help-versus-usable-help pattern. A program can count older participants and provide a real service while the next step remains harder to use, especially when training competes with care, money, health, disability, or the need for immediate wages.

It also adds a cultural and institutional layer: older workers are not only a retirement group. They are workers navigating digital systems, employer age judgments, family responsibilities, and changing job demands. Local centers had useful practices, but GAO found that these practices were ad hoc and not systematically shared across partners.

What the report does not establish

  • The six programs and 41 interviewed workers were selected, not nationally representative.
  • The employment comparisons are descriptive and do not prove that age caused the difference.
  • Lower training use may reflect care, finances, health, disability, immediate work needs, or retirement plans—not only a program barrier.
  • “Exit” generally means 90 days without services and no planned future services; it is not the same as successful completion.
  • The report does not measure wages, job quality, retention, discrimination, household security, or retirement outcomes.

Next test

Follow one older worker from outreach and application through eligibility, career services, training choice, digital support, employer contact, offer, pay, job quality, retention, care obligations, health, and later retirement or household security. Compare sites that share promising practices with sites that do not, while preserving local labor-market differences.

Evidence status: GAO documents participation, service-use, and post-exit employment differences plus participant perspectives; causal mechanisms and durable work or household outcomes remain open.

Reading rule

Do not call a workforce program successful because people enrolled or exited. Ask whether the person could use the training, reach an employer, get a decent job, and remain secure afterward.
