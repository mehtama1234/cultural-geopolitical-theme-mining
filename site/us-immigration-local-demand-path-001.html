Connected US finding · place, housing and local demand

When new residents change the local customer base

New residents add workers, customers, needs, and neighbors at the same time. That can create local jobs while adding pressure to housing and services.

In one sentence

A local job gain does not settle the rent, wage, service, or belonging question.

Count who gains demand and who carries the added cost.

What the sources show

Immigration changes demand as well as labor supply

Using US Census data from 1980 to 2000, NBER estimates that each immigrant created about 1.2 local jobs for local workers, mostly in local services. Other NBER research finds that immigrants can expand labor supply and competition while also starting firms and expanding labor demand.

Recent NBER work studies the postpandemic surge and estimates roughly offsetting effects on inflation. HBS reports more socially useful ideas from mixed migrant-local teams in the study.

The connection

new residents arrive → more workers, customers, and local needs → service demand, business entry, jobs, wages, and housing respond → residents experience different prices, access, and belonging → local and national political demands change

New demand can support local workers and businesses while adding pressure where systems expand slowly.

The four outside views

Household: New customers may bring useful stores, care, food, and services. Rent, crowding, wait times, and school or health access may also change.

Company and sector: Firms gain workers and customers and may start new services. They may also compete for scarce housing, labor, or public space.

Money and finance: New demand can raise sales and local jobs without raising every worker's pay. Housing costs and business profits may move differently.

Public power: Housing supply, schools, health care, transit, language access, work rules, and local taxes decide who receives the gain and who carries the cost.

What would change the finding

  • Current local demand, service use, business entry, survival, and ownership.
  • Housing supply, rents, crowding, home prices, and public capacity.
  • Wages, hours, job quality, and prices for immigrants and existing residents.
  • Schools, health care, transport, wait times, and tax receipts.
  • Local attitudes, trust, belonging, and political response.

Next test

Follow matched places before and after a change in new-resident inflow for five years. Join migration, customer demand, business, housing, wage, service, tax, and attitude data.

Reading rule

Do not treat immigration as one market effect. Count workers, customers, housing pressure, services, and belonging separately.
