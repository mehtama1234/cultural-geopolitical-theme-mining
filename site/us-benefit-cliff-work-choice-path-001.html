When a raise changes the help a family can keep

A raise can increase pay while reducing food, health, childcare, or cash help. The family may still be better off, but the wage number alone cannot tell us.

The argument

The real question is whether work leaves the family with more room, more stable work, and a safer way to meet basic needs. A sudden loss of support can make a new job too risky when care and transport costs rise too.

A benefit exit is not proof of a benefit cliff.

What the sources establish

NBER studies found SNAP work requirements reduced participation among parents without increasing work in the studied settings. HHS models how SNAP, tax credits, Medicaid, childcare, and TANF interact as earnings rise, showing high effective rates for some households. HBS shows care and transport needs make the same job benefit useful to one worker and less useful to another.

The net gain from work depends on aid, taxes, necessary costs, hours, and stability—not wages alone.

Follow the earnings change

earnings rise → public help tapers, ends, or becomes harder to keep → food, health, childcare, or transport cost changes → real gain from work changes → hours, job choice, or trust changes

A smooth taper can leave a family better off. A sudden cutoff can remove the room needed to keep working.

Keep these apart

  • Wage gain and total resources.
  • Benefit exit and benefit cliff.
  • Work requirement and employment gain.
  • Higher earnings and higher usable room.

Next test

Follow families before and after an earnings change for one year. Join wages, benefits, taxes, necessary costs, hours, job stability, debt, food security, and housing.

Reading rule

Do not call a benefit exit a cliff until total resources and necessary costs are counted.
