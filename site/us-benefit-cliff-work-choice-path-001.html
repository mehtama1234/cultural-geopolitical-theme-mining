Connected US finding · aid, work and household money

When a raise changes the help a family can keep

A raise can increase pay while reducing food, health, child care, or cash help. The family may still be better off, but the gain can be smaller than the wage number suggests.

In one sentence

The right measure is pay plus aid minus taxes and necessary costs.

A program exit is not proof that total household resources fell.

What the sources show

Work and aid do not move in one simple direction

Recent NBER research finds that SNAP work requirements reduced participation among parents but did not increase work. An earlier study also found large SNAP exits after work requirements without a main employment effect. HHS models how several programs interact as earnings rise, while HBS describes how care and transport needs make the same job benefit useful to one worker and less useful to another.

NBER 32441 · NBER 28877 · HHS benefit cliffs · HBS caregiver support

The connection

earnings or hours rise → public help tapers, ends, or becomes harder to keep → food, health, child care, or transport cost changes → the real gain from work changes → work hours, job choice, trust, or political demand changes

Work requirements can remove aid without raising work. A gradual taper can still leave a household better off.

The four outside views

Household: A raise can bring more cash and more costs at the same time. The family may choose fewer hours if care, health, or transport becomes too costly.

Company and sector: Hours or benefits may not fit a worker's care or transport needs. A benefit that looks valuable may not be usable.

Money and finance: Measure pay plus aid minus taxes and necessary costs. Losing one program is not the same as losing total resources.

Public power: Reporting rules, state lines, benefit timing, and program design decide whether work brings room or only paperwork and risk.

What would change the finding

  • Earnings, taxes, each benefit, and necessary food, health, care, and transport costs.
  • Hours offered, hours worked, job stability, benefits, and commute.
  • Family structure, disability, race, place, wealth, and child care access.
  • Whether the loss came from a real income change or an administrative failure.
  • Work, debt, health, food security, and housing after six and twelve months.

Next test

Follow the same families before and after an earnings change for one year. Join wage records, benefit records, taxes, required costs, work hours, job changes, food security, debt, and health.

Reading rule

Do not call a benefit exit a benefit cliff until total resources and necessary costs are counted.
