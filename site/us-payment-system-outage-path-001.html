Connected US finding · payment and access

When the power fails, payment becomes access

A household can have money and still be unable to use it when electricity, communications, or a merchant terminal fails. During an outage, payment helps decide whether essentials can be reached.

The argument

Before a storm, people may stock up and use credit. During the outage, a card reader or network may stop working. Cash can keep one purchase moving only if the household has cash, the store is open, supplies remain, and the person can travel safely.

Payment resilience is part of basic access.

What the source establishes

NBER combines outage, weather, transaction, scanner, and survey data. Spending rises before disasters, often using credit. After outages, digital payments fall and cash use rises. Nearly half of surveyed consumers could not use their preferred electronic payment, while greater cash access reduced the decline in completed purchases.

The evidence does not show which households missed food, medicine, fuel, or work access. A shift toward cash can show a useful backup, a failed preferred payment, or both.

NBER Working Paper 35115

Follow the purchase

storm or infrastructure failure → electronic payment unavailable → cash, another card, offline tool, or no transaction → essential purchase completed or missed → household cost, health, work, and recovery change

Banks, networks, merchants, utilities, and fuel providers may each control one link. A failure at one link can stop a purchase that all the others could have served.

Keep these apart

  • Payment availability and purchase completion.
  • Essential need and the purchase itself.
  • Cash use and hardship.
  • Immediate access and later recovery.

Next test

Follow households through a real outage for three months. Join payment attempts, cash access, merchant acceptance, essential purchases, credit, work, care, health, and recovery.

Reading rule

Having money is not the same as being able to use it.
