When the power fails, payment becomes access

The argument

A household can have money and still be unable to use it when electricity, communications, or a merchant terminal fails. In an outage, payment is not an abstract financial service. It helps decide whether food, medicine, fuel, or a needed trip can be completed. The account balance is stored value; access is the set of places where that value can still buy something.

Before a storm, people may stock up and use credit. During the outage, a card reader or network may stop working. Cash may keep one purchase moving, but only if the household has cash, the store is open, shelves are stocked, and the person can travel safely. A shift toward cash can show a useful backup, a failed preferred payment, or both. It does not tell us by itself whether the household was prepared or forced into a less safe option.

The deeper finding is that payment resilience is part of basic access. The failure is not only “the card did not work.” It may become a missed prescription, a longer trip for fuel, expensive credit, or lost work time. The household account balance can remain unchanged while the household’s usable options shrink. A small business faces the same problem in reverse: it may have inventory and customers but be unable to take payment, pay workers, or restock.

Follow one essential purchase

A family loses power after a storm and needs medicine. The pharmacy can open but its terminal cannot connect. The family has some cash, but not enough for the full prescription, and the nearest working pharmacy is 20 miles away. A second card may work, or the family may delay treatment. Each backup has a different cost.

Now follow the same event for a small store. The store can open and has food, but cannot authorize cards. It may accept cash, write a tab, or close. If it accepts tabs, it carries the risk that customers cannot repay. If it closes, households travel farther and the store loses sales exactly when it needs cash. An outage therefore changes both sides of the transaction: who can buy and who can keep selling.

The order of failure matters. If communications fail but power remains, a terminal may be unusable while shelves are full. If power fails, refrigeration, fuel pumps, elevators, and pharmacy systems may fail too. If roads are blocked, a working payment method cannot reach an open store. Payment is one link in access, not the whole chain.

The payment channel, merchant, supply, transport, and household buffer all have to work together. A backup that works for a restaurant may not work for a pharmacy or fuel station.

The outage can also create a race for the backups. People with early warning may withdraw cash, fill the car, or buy supplies before the network fails. That can leave less cash, fuel, or stock for people who had no warning, no transport, or no room on a credit card. Preparation spending is therefore not only a private choice; at scale it can change what is available to the next household. The same transaction data can hide that distributional split.

Recovery has two clocks. The first is the time until a purchase can be made. The second is the time until the account, merchant, and household records agree about what happened. A card may be declined, then charged twice when the system returns. A merchant may accept an offline payment but wait days for approval. A customer may see a balance hold and delay another purchase. Restoring the signal is not the same as restoring trust in the balance.

→ cash, another card, offline tool, store credit, or no transaction
→ essential purchase completed or missed

What the source actually establishes

Step Evidence Meaning Limit
Preparation NBER combines outage, weather, transaction, scanner, and survey data and finds spending rises before disasters, often using credit. Households try to prepare before access becomes harder. Preparation spending does not show whether the needed item was later available.
Channel failure After outages, digital payments fall and cash use rises. Payment methods change when networks fail. A cash shift may show choice, constraint, or a change in what stores accept.
Choice loss Nearly half of surveyed consumers could not use their preferred electronic payment during an outage. A preferred method may not be a usable method. The survey does not identify every essential purchase that was missed.
Backup Greater cash access reduced the decline in completed purchases. Cash can help. Cash does not solve supply, safety, transport, theft, or prescription limits.

The deeper finding

Payment is part of infrastructure because it connects stored money to a real purchase. Each link has a different owner: banks, networks, merchants, utilities, fuel providers, and households. A failure at one link can stop a purchase that the others could have served.

The wider finding is about uneven recovery. A household with cash, a second card, a working car, and a nearby open store can turn a payment failure into an inconvenience. A household with one account, little cash, no car, and a prescription due that day can turn the same failure into a health or debt event. The outage exposes the value of backups that are usually invisible.

It also changes the meaning of “financial inclusion.” Having an account is not enough if the account cannot be used when the network is down. A resilient system needs a safe fallback, clear rules for offline transactions, protection against duplicate charges, and a way for merchants to reconcile later. But every fallback shifts risk: offline credit can expose a merchant to loss, and cash can expose a customer to theft or loss.

This makes small merchants a crucial middle point. A large chain may have several processors, insurance, and a central cash system. A corner store may have one terminal, one bank account, and little ability to absorb a bad offline payment. If the system asks that store to provide the fallback, it is asking a local business to finance public resilience. If it refuses, nearby households pay in travel, time, or missed care. The right question is who is equipped and paid to carry the backup risk.

The political lesson is easy to miss because the failure looks technical. A household may blame its bank, the utility, the network, the merchant, or emergency management depending on where the transaction stopped. A later refund may repair the account but not the missed shift or delayed medicine. Public trust will follow the lived interruption, not merely the final reconciliation entry.

What remains unproven

The study does not show which households missed food, medicine, fuel, or work access, or which backup design works best in every outage. It does not prove that every cash user was in hardship. Cash can be lost or stolen, and a long outage can empty shelves and stop fuel pumps. It does not show whether a merchant’s offline approval created later fraud, duplicate billing, or unrecovered sales.

Next test

Follow households and small merchants through a real outage and the three months after it. Join payment attempts, cash access, merchant acceptance, offline tools, essential purchases, credit, work, care, health, transport, duplicate charges, lost sales, and reconciliation. Compare households with different backups but similar needs. Report four outcomes separately: whether the transaction happened, whether the essential need was met, what extra cost was paid, and how long recovery took.

Test the system before the next emergency with a controlled outage exercise across banks, processors, utilities, pharmacies, fuel stations, and small stores. Track a simulated purchase from decline through fallback and final settlement. Measure who bears loss when an offline payment is rejected, duplicated, or disputed. The exercise should include households with no car, limited cash, disability, and time-sensitive medicine; a fallback that works only for a well-resourced customer is not a general resilience measure.

Reading rule

Keep payment availability, purchase completion, essential need, extra cost, and later loss separate. Having money is not the same as being able to use it.
