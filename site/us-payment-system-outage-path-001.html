Connected US finding · payment and access

When the power fails, payment becomes access

A household can have money and still be unable to use it when electricity, communications, or a merchant terminal fails.

The argument

During an outage, payment helps decide whether food, medicine, fuel, or a needed trip can be completed. Cash may help only if the household has it, the store is open, supplies remain, and travel is safe.

The account balance is stored value. Access is the set of places where that value can still buy something.

The household balance can remain unchanged while usable options shrink.

Follow one essential purchase

A family loses power after a storm and needs medicine. The pharmacy is open but its terminal cannot connect. The family has some cash, not enough for the full prescription, and the nearest working pharmacy is 20 miles away. A second card may work, or treatment may be delayed. Each backup has a different cost.

Now follow the same event for a small store. The store has food but cannot authorize cards. It may accept cash, write a tab, or close. If it accepts tabs, it carries repayment risk. If it closes, households travel farther and the store loses sales when it needs cash.

storm or infrastructure failure → electronic payment unavailable → cash, another card, offline tool, store credit, or no transaction → essential purchase completed or missed → household, merchant, health, work, and recovery costs change

If communications fail but power remains, a terminal may be unusable while shelves are full. If power fails, refrigeration and fuel pumps may fail too. If roads are blocked, a working payment method cannot reach an open store.

The hidden value of a backup

Household

A second card, cash, or nearby store can turn a failed payment into an inconvenience rather than a missed need.

Merchant

Offline sales keep a store open but shift the risk of fraud, duplicate charges, or unpaid tabs onto the business.

System

Having an account is not enough if it cannot be used when the network is down. Recovery and reconciliation matter too.

The same outage has different effects because backups are uneven. A household with cash, a second card, a working car, and an open store can recover faster than one with one account, little cash, no car, and a prescription due that day.

What remains unknown

The study does not show which households missed food, medicine, fuel, or work access, or which backup works best in every outage. It does not prove that every cash user was in hardship. Cash can be lost or stolen, and long outages can empty shelves and stop fuel pumps.

Next test

Follow households and small merchants through a real outage and the three months after it. Join payment attempts, cash access, merchant acceptance, offline tools, essential purchases, credit, work, care, health, transport, duplicate charges, lost sales, and reconciliation. Report whether the transaction happened, whether the essential need was met, what extra cost was paid, and how long recovery took.

Reading rule: keep payment availability, purchase completion, essential need, extra cost, and later loss separate. Having money is not the same as being able to use it.
