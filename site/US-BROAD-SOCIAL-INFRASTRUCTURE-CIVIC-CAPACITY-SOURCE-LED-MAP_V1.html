Social infrastructure and civic capacity: source-led map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: a depth pass across the social-connection, cultural-participation, and public-life records. It separates access to social settings, stated support, cultural participation, and civic engagement; none alone is belonging or power.

1. Neighborhood amenities, contact, trust, and mutual aid are related but not interchangeable

The American Neighbor Survey record finds 40% talked with neighbors at least a few times weekly and 40% worked with neighbors in the previous year. People reporting six or more amenities within a 20-minute walk reported weekly neighbor conversation at 43%, versus 27% among those reporting none. The cross-sectional design cannot show that third places caused contact, trust, or actual aid.

1a. A new third place can be followed by local firm formation where an equivalent gathering venue was absent

The third-place/startup record compares U.S. census tracts that received a first Starbucks with tracts planned to receive one but that did not, using 1990–2022 business-registration data. Among places without an existing coffee shop, the study reports 9.1%–22.5% more annual startups over seven years after entry (about 2.9–6.4 additional firms per year). Effects were reported as larger in the Starbucks–Magic Johnson under-resourced-neighborhood partnership, while the study found no significant entrepreneurship effect where cafés already existed. The contrast matters: a branded entrant is not automatically a social-capacity gain when a comparable venue is already present.

Supported subtheme: a locally accessible gathering venue can be part of a place-to-network-to-firm-formation route, with an absence-of-equivalent-venue condition and a meaningful null comparison.
Not established: that social connection rather than expected growth, foot traffic, or other neighborhood change produced the result; firm survival, jobs, wages, owner income, customer benefit, displacement, housing effects, or individual belonging. The public abstract does not provide the full analytic sample or all selection and quality measures.

2. Reported emotional support does not capture the ability to participate

The CDC community-connection record shows 82% of adults reported needed social/emotional support in its 2021 indicator, while 12% reported health-related difficulty participating in social activities in 2024. The different years and survey systems cannot be pooled.

3. Cultural participation is a socially patterned resource, not a proven connection intervention

The NEA arts-participation record reports 25.2% attended an arts event in the prior month, with 16.2% attendance under $25,000 household income versus 55.2% at $200,000+. Attendees more often reported needed social/emotional support. Resources, time, health, transport, and prior social ties can produce both; causality is not observed.

4. Civic engagement has different styles and does not form a simple trust ladder

The Pew public-life record distinguishes Mobilizers, Connectors, Spectators, and Outsiders. Very/extreme political interest ranged from 58% among Mobilizers to 9% among Outsiders, yet federal-government trust was low across all groups (17–22%). Engagement can coexist with skepticism; clusters are descriptive, not fixed identities.

5. Institutions and kin can be social infrastructure before they become a formal service

The Pew religious-institutions record finds 80% said religious organizations help strengthen community bonds and 78% said they play an important role helping people in need, while only 37% personally belonged to a congregation. Perceived community legitimacy is not evidence of delivered or adequate aid.

The grandparent-connection record separates grandparent status, contact, shared residence, care, and closeness: 21% reported 100+ hours of grandchild care across two years, but care is not automatically burden, necessity, or material support.

5a. Family can be a chosen and extended support structure without becoming a measured material safety net

The Black family-networks record uses a June 2025 Pew survey that includes 4,271 Black adults and defines family through birth, marriage, law, and chosen/non-relative ties. Among Black adults with the relevant relationship, 73% reported feeling very or extremely close to a nonrelative considered family; the corresponding shares were 77% for a spouse or partner, 66% for a parent, and 63% for a sibling. Reported closeness to grandparents, cousins, and aunts/uncles was higher for Black than non-Black adults in the cited relationship-specific comparisons.

Supported subtheme: a social-support network can extend beyond household rosters and legal kin, making family definition and closeness a relevant social infrastructure surface. Kin category, chosen-family recognition, closeness, delivered care, financial transfer, co-residence, and practical help are not the same variable.

Not established: who provided or received care, money, housing, food, transportation, or emotional support; whether any relationship was protective or burdensome; causal differences by race; household security; conflict; political action; or later durability. The relationship measures have distinct conditional denominators and are cross-sectional self-reports.

6. Civic belonging can include practical support and local agency, but selection remains central

The CIRCLE political-homes record reports youth with access to political homes had civic action and self-efficacy about 20 points higher, with settings providing information, food, transportation, compensation, and safe spaces. It is not randomized access.

The rural local-agency record reports 70% average trust in local organizations versus 25% national trust, and 90% individual local agency among network-recruited respondents. It is not a probability estimate for rural America.

6a. A school can operate as a bundled social-support connector, with later outcomes that do not reveal which support mattered

The Communities In Schools record reports a staggered-rollout study in high-poverty U.S. schools. Coordinators connected struggling students to tailored academic, behavioral, mental-health, and basic-needs resources, including housing stabilization. The study reports higher test scores, high-school completion, and adult earnings; it attributes about half of the graduation effect to test-score improvement and the remainder to attendance and behavior progress. Its reported program reach of 2 million students annually is not the study's estimation denominator or a measure of identical exposure.

Supported subtheme: a school-based connector can link multiple social and material supports to educational and later labor-market outcomes, and the reported pathway is broader than academic instruction alone.
Not established: the separate effect of housing, mental-health, academic, or behavioral support; adequate service receipt; wellbeing, safety, household trust, or local civic capacity; transfer to every school; or an unqualified claim about each support component. The cited source is a working paper and its abstract does not provide the analytic denominator or treatment intensity; the staggered-rollout result should not be generalized to every local service mix, school, or student.

6b. Personalized school contact can change enrollment before digital engagement becomes learning

The Chicago remote-learning engagement record keeps three randomized field experiments in Chicago Public Schools separate. Personalized telephone calls increased families' likelihood of registering for a virtual summer-school program. Weekly text messages did not change the primary summer-absence or Khan Academy-use outcomes, although a secondary math-course outcome improved. Adding an instructional aide did not change its primary attendance or platform-use outcomes, while the paper reports beneficial effects in following-year math grades and passing rates.

Supported subtheme: an institution's route to a family matters. A personalized contact can change program take-up, yet registration, attendance, platform use, course performance, and later academic results are distinct stages. Generic digital contact cannot be treated as interchangeable with direct relationship-building or as a single learning intervention.

Not established: durable attainment, effects beyond the Chicago remote-learning and summer-school settings, family time or material burden, which feature of a call or aide produced any effect, an overall engagement effect across interventions, social connection, or a general digital-learning benefit.

7. Material strain tracks perceived support loss more strongly than contact loss

The expense-and-connection record uses a corrected March 2026 Census Pulse cross-section. Among respondents whose household expenses were not difficult, frequent loneliness was 1.8% and low social/emotional support 14.0%; among those finding expenses very difficult, the figures were 30.2% and 40.9%. Frequent phone/video contact was 28.3% in the first group and 21.8% in the second, showing that contact frequency and felt support do not move as one measure. The comparisons are same-round, descriptive conditional shares.

Supported subtheme: material difficulty can coexist with contact while perceived support and connection are weaker; network presence is not evidence of usable help.
Not established: that expense difficulty caused loneliness, a dated bill or relationship rupture, practical aid received, recovery, or later civic action.

7a. Social isolation and support shortfall have unequal distribution before they become a care or civic outcome

The CDC loneliness and support record uses a 26-state 2022 BRFSS analytic sample of 218,915 adults. It reports 32.1% feeling socially isolated always, usually, or sometimes and 24.1% sometimes, rarely, or never receiving needed social/emotional support. Reported isolation was 43.3% among adults ages 18–34, 47.9% among households below $25,000, 56.7% among bisexual adults, and 56.4%–63.9% across reported transgender groups. Adjusted cross-sectional associations with stress, frequent mental distress, and depression history are not evidence that isolation caused those outcomes.

Supported subtheme: social infrastructure has an unequal exposure layer: who reports isolation and inadequate support differs across age, income, and sexual/gender-identity groups before any formal care, community intervention, or political consequence is observed. Network presence, reported support, and mental-health burden remain separate measures.

Not established: a causal effect of isolation, aid actually received, care use, durable belonging, civic action, recovery, or a national estimate beyond the participating states and the stated survey conditions.

8. Civic participation can broaden while the time intensity of service thins

The civic reach-and-time record reports formal volunteering rose from 23.2% in 2021 to 28.3% in 2023, while informal neighbor help rose from 51.7% in 2019 to 54.2%. But among formal volunteers, mean annual hours fell from 96.5 in 2017 to 70.0 in 2023 and the median from 40 to 24; 18% volunteered online or hybrid, averaging 95 hours versus 64 for in-person-only volunteers. These are repeated cross-sections and activity-specific denominators.

Supported subtheme: the number of people touching civic work and the amount of sustained time supplied are distinct dimensions of local capacity.
Not established: who reduced hours, whether digital activity replaced travel or increased capacity, or whether the shift changed service quality, belonging, or political influence.

9. A communal institution may carry high trust and service with unequal internal access

The Latter-day Saint communal-ties record reports 72% volunteer activity among current Latter-day Saints versus 41% of all U.S. adults, and 48% saying most people can be trusted versus 34% of all adults. Within the affiliation, reported volunteering was 91% for adults with a bachelor's degree or more versus 61% with less education, and 80% in the Mountain region versus 62% elsewhere. The survey cannot attribute those gaps to congregation participation.

Supported subtheme: dense communal infrastructure can be associated with service and generalized trust while resources, geography, and education divide participation within the same identity group.
Not established: that membership caused trust or volunteering, that service was equally available or non-burdensome, or that the pattern creates durable belonging or public power.

10. AI companionship is a new support surface, not yet a demonstrated social substitute

The AI companion record reports that 27% of internet-using adults had social AI interaction; among selected companion users, 59% said AI gave needed support, 53% used it for difficult interpersonal advice, 39% sometimes disclosed things they would not tell others, and 36% felt emotionally connected to at least one AI tool. These are conditional, self-reported survey measures rather than a longitudinal measure of human relationships.

Supported subtheme: social support and meaning now have a platform-mediated surface that may supplement human ties, mediate disclosure, or change where people seek advice.
Not established: substitution for people, loneliness reduction, dependence, privacy loss, wellbeing, durable use, or effects after exit.

10a. Social-infrastructure work can carry a sector-specific compensation cost without settling its social value

The nonprofit-pay record uses linked administrative tax, employer, ACS, LEHD, and Form 990 data. It places nonprofit work at roughly 10% of the U.S. workforce and reports average nonprofit earnings 12% below for-profit earnings. In the paper's worker-firm job-ladder model, most of the gap is attributed to firm rather than worker selection, though the result depends on its exogenous-mobility assumption. Industry differences are large: nonprofit health and education pay can be comparable to or higher than for-profit counterparts, while publishing and arts show larger penalties; nonprofit and for-profit earnings have converged over time.

Supported subtheme: the institutions supplying care, culture, advocacy, and civic service have their own labor-resource condition. Service capacity, mission, non-wage benefits, job quality, earnings, and worker household room are separate stages rather than one social-value score. Not established: service quality, worker wellbeing, hours, job security, household outcome, consumer benefit, or that lower pay reflects lower commitment or weaker social value. The Census working paper's causal interpretation and sector coverage retain model and data-component boundaries.

Cross-subtheme finding and next test

place, access, time, health, money, cultural setting, network, or platform
  -> contact, attendance, support request, disclosure, mutual aid, or civic participation
  -> usable assistance, belonging, collective capacity, or withdrawal
  -> institutional judgment and later public action

The missing evidence follows the same person or neighborhood through access, actual support received, adequacy, recovery, and later civic action. Survey attitudes and attendance cannot supply that missing sequence.
