Formal access is broad, but the financial route still determines room to act

Checked: 2026-09-15
Source: FDIC 2023 National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households
Reproducibility: FDIC data downloads and resources
Machine record: FDIC access and credit routes record

The bounded finding

The FDIC survey adds a household-level US financial-access layer that is more discriminating than a single “banked” rate. In 2023, 4.2% of households—about 5.6 million—were unbanked. Another 14.2%, about 19.0 million households, were underbanked under the FDIC definition: they had an account but used at least one specified nonbank transaction or credit service in the previous year. At the same time, 15.7% of households had no mainstream credit product in the survey’s credit frame, including 78.4% of unbanked households and 13.0% of banked households.

The financial route therefore has several distinct gates: account ownership, the need to use nonbank services, visibility in mainstream credit, and the digital or physical channel used to transact. These measures do not establish hardship or distrust by themselves. They show that formal access and usable room to act are not the same thing.

The FDIC survey is conducted biennially with the Census Bureau as a supplement to the Current Population Survey and is designed to represent households in the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The household unit matters: these are not adult-level account measures and should not be pooled casually with the Federal Reserve SHED or World Bank Findex.

The important split

formal account
  -> transaction route
  -> credit visibility and available terms
  -> ability to pay, borrow, save, recover, or switch
  -> consumer confidence, complaint, exit, or political judgment

The first three layers are where FDIC contributes direct evidence. The final layer remains open. A household can be banked but have little liquid money. It can be underbanked because a nonbank service is convenient, or because the bank route is costly or unavailable. It can have a credit record but still be denied or offered terms it cannot carry. “Financial inclusion” is therefore not one endpoint; it is a route with different points of friction and control.

What this adds to the existing financial-capacity program

The broader atlas already contains provider-side IMF FAS measures, adult-level World Bank Findex, Federal Reserve household financial conditions, OFR institutional and market-capacity records, CFPB complaint routes, and New York Fed debt/credit evidence. FDIC supplies a missing US household bridge between account ownership and the use of alternative transaction and credit channels.

The safe cross-source interpretation is staged:

Stage Source family What can be said
Provider reach and institutional capacity IMF FAS, OFR, FDIC institutional data Financial-service supply and system capacity exist at different levels
Household account route FDIC Some households lack a bank/credit-union account; others use nonbank services despite having one
Adult financial resilience Federal Reserve SHED, World Bank Findex Separate adult/household measures describe buffers, payments, and formal access
Credit exposure and debt New York Fed, SHED, CFPB Credit records, balances, complaint routes, and hardship are separate outcomes
Correction, trust, and exit CFPB, survey meaning measures, future event data Same-case remedy, repeat effort, switching, trust change, and political action remain open

What this does not prove

  • It does not prove that being unbanked caused a household’s financial hardship.
  • It does not show whether nonbank providers improved or worsened the household’s terms.
  • It does not turn “no mainstream credit” into a credit-score, denial, or poverty estimate.
  • It does not establish that “don’t trust banks” among unbanked respondents equals generalized institutional distrust or political behavior.
  • It does not show a dated event, the alternatives considered, the response chosen, or the later recovery of the same household.

The next test

Use the public FDIC files and multi-year weights to construct a repeated 2009–2023 household comparison with explicit survey-vintage controls. Then compare, where the data support it:

  1. account status and nonbank route by income, race/ethnicity, education, disability, family type, age, and geography;
  2. mainstream-credit visibility with income volatility, payment difficulty, and reported financial adaptations;
  3. bank-channel and mobile/nonbank route differences with physical branch access, transaction needs, and consumer effort;
  4. 2021-to-2023 changes without treating separate cross-sections as a household panel;
  5. FDIC classifications beside SHED, Findex, and CFPB records without inventing a respondent-level join.

The stronger event-level test would need a dated account or payment failure, the household’s alternatives, provider response, repeat effort, correction, cost, and later switching or exit. FDIC identifies the route categories; it does not close the event.

What would change the finding

The working picture would weaken if account ownership, nonbank use, and credit visibility were nearly interchangeable across households and did not vary with resources, place, or subgroup. It would strengthen if households with formal accounts still faced distinct transaction and credit routes, and if those routes predicted different costs, effort, recovery, or ability to leave after a measured financial event.

Reading rule

When a source says a household is “banked,” ask: can it use the account for this transaction, at this price, with this credit record, through a channel it can reach, and with a remedy if the route fails?
