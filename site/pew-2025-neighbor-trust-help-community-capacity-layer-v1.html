Pew 2025 neighbor trust, mutual aid, and community capacity layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: Pew Research Center, How connected do Americans feel to their neighbors? Survey field dates: March 10–16, 2025; 9,482 U.S. adults in the American Trends Panel Status: community-trust and informal-capacity layer; not a measure of actual assistance delivered, generalized social cohesion, or causal neighborhood effects

Why this update matters

The atlas has formal institutions, public programs, religious affiliation, and political participation. This layer adds the neighborhood as an operating social institution: who is known, trusted, similar, expected to help, and available in an emergency. It is especially useful for the broad goal because it distinguishes generalized trust from local trust and stated willingness from reciprocal capacity.

Pew finds that older, White, more affluent, more educated, and rural or suburban adults are generally more likely to know and trust neighbors and to report helping behavior. People who attend in-person religious services at least monthly are also more likely to know and trust neighbors. Pew notes that party differences in neighbor trust are related to race, education, and other factors, so the raw partisan contrast should not be read as a party mechanism.

What this adds to the broad goal

  1. Local trust is not generalized trust. Forty-four percent trust most or all neighbors, compared with 34% saying most people can be trusted. A person can be locally embedded while remaining skeptical of society in general.
  2. Willingness and available support diverge. People are more likely to say they would help than that neighbors would help them. This creates a practical-capacity question: social resilience may depend on reciprocity, not goodwill alone.
  3. Informal infrastructure can substitute for formal capacity—but is not free. Keys, meals, errands, money, and emergency conservation can protect households or public systems, yet they transfer time, risk, and obligation to friends and neighbors. The survey does not measure the cost or adequacy of that substitution.
  4. Similarity and trust are related but not identical. Half report racial similarity among neighbors, only 23% political similarity, and perceived similarity correlates with trust and mutual-help expectations. This does not establish that homogeneity causes trust or that diversity prevents help.
  5. Place and resources shape community capacity. Rural/suburban residence, income, education, age, and religion condition reported connection. These are possible structural pathways, not proof that one neighborhood form or demographic group causes stronger reciprocity.

End-to-end position

The source supports this bounded chain:

place and social proximity
  -> familiarity and local trust
  -> willingness and expectation of mutual aid
  -> potential informal protection or public-request compliance

It does not observe a dated household shock, who actually provided help, duration, adequacy, burden, failed reciprocity, formal-service interaction, material recovery, later trust, or political action. It cannot establish that neighbor trust prevents hardship, replaces public programs, increases civic participation, or causes social cohesion.

Next test

Use a compact place-linked or repeated household design to follow a dated crisis, care need, heat/energy emergency, migration, or service disruption through neighbor contact, help requested, help delivered, time/money burden, formal assistance, recovery, and later trust or movement. Preserve distinct measures for knowing, trusting, willingness, expectation, actual help, reciprocity, adequacy, and cost.

Boundaries and acquisition note

This is a weighted cross-sectional ATP survey with self-reported expectations. The 2015/2018 comparisons are aggregate trend comparisons, not recontact-panel estimates. Neighbor, friend, local-community, and public-official request items refer to different social relationships and should not be collapsed. This pass retained official HTML only and downloaded no topline PDF, microdata, or large archive.
