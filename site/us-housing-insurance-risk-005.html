Finding 005: Wildfire insurance can move into partial payment while repairability and safe return remain open

Status: bounded California wildfire insurance implementation finding · Checked: 2026-09-21

The bounded finding

California’s Department of Insurance wildfire claims tracker reported, as of July 6, 2026, 41,727 claims filed for the Eaton and Palisades fires, 40,482 claims partially paid, and $25.2 billion paid to date. The tracker combines home, business, living-expense, auto, debris-removal, and other disaster claims, so these are claims-processing and payment stages rather than a household repair or recovery rate.

The Department’s separate May 2026 State Farm investigation reviewed 220 claims and found 398 violations in 114 claims, including delayed investigation, underpayment, repeated adjuster reassignment, and smoke-damage claim delays or denials. On September 15, 2026, California announced that AB 1795 had become law, establishing enforceable standards for investigating, testing, and restoring smoke-damaged homes. The law creates a wildfire-zone presumption for smoke damage, requires insurers to pay necessary testing and sampling costs, keeps qualifying additional-living-expense benefits in place until a home is restored and safe to occupy, requires inspection within 30 days after claim notice or access, requires repair estimates meeting pre-loss health and safety standards, and preserves the policyholder's contractor choice.

The route is therefore:

wildfire / smoke damage
  -> claim and living-expense route
  -> partial payment and regulator intervention
  -> claim-handling reform and smoke-restoration standards
  -> completed repair, safe return, adequate coverage, and stable housing [open]

Why it matters to the broad atlas

This is a stronger realization stage than a FAIR Plan policy count or a complaint-closure total. It shows claims entering payment and a regulator converting observed handling failures into legal standards. But “partially paid” is not “repaired,” and a state reform is not yet a safe home. The customer’s remaining problem can move from proving damage to financing the repair, finding temporary housing, managing health risk, and deciding whether to stay.

The case also shows two clocks operating together: money can flow while claim handling remains contested, and a legal standard can be enacted while its implementation and household effects are still future stages.

The new law is a meaningful shift in who must carry uncertainty. It moves some proof, testing, timing, and temporary-housing obligations toward insurers, but it does not itself show that a particular household received testing, an adequate estimate, continued living-expense payments, a completed repair, or a safe and affordable place to return to.

Limits and next test

  • The claims tracker combines multiple claim types and does not identify unique households or properties.
  • “Partially paid” does not show final settlement, repair completion, or safe habitability.
  • The State Farm examination is a sampled insurer-specific investigation, not a statewide violation rate.
  • The legislation establishes standards and obligations; it does not yet demonstrate compliance or household recovery.
  • The public records do not show who moved, returned, borrowed, delayed repair, or experienced lasting health/work effects.

The next decisive artifact is a lawful property-level ledger joining policy and loss date, claim decision, payment, repair/testing, temporary housing, additional living expenses, final habitability, renewal or replacement coverage, and stay/move outcome.
