When the news selects the economic mood

A headline about a sharp increase may shape what people expect next more than a report about a decrease. But the story and the household's own basket can move in different directions.

The argument

News can frame a real cost, change a plan, and assign blame. It cannot tell us by itself what a household bought or who it held responsible.

Inflation is lived first as a price and then as a public story about that price.

Follow one price story through a household

A family hears repeated reports of a higher Consumer Price Index. It searches for deals, delays a large purchase, or borrows sooner because it expects prices to rise again. Ten days later the headline effect may fade, but rent, food, and fuel prices remain. Another family hears the same report through a trusted source and blames a different institution.

price movement → selected news and repetition → inflation expectation and cause belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

What the sources establish

EvidenceWhat it means
An NBER household-panel study finds higher-price news raises inflation expectations by 0.4 percentage point, while lower-price news has no significant effect.Bad price news can move expectations more than good price news in the study.
Another NBER study finds daily US television coverage raises expectations; effects fade after about ten days.Repetition and timing can shape a short-term economic frame.
NBER research finds party and news source shape perceived causes and policy preferences.People can disagree about both the price path and who caused it.
Rent, food, gas, wages, debt, and local prices determine what a family actually faces.A national headline may fit one basket and miss another.
The studies do not show each household's full media diet, price basket, spending, or political response in one record.Expectation evidence does not prove changed behavior or voting.

Sources: NBER Working Paper 33837, NBER Working Paper 34088, and NBER Working Paper 32497.

The deeper finding

A calmer headline does not undo a higher price level. Political judgment may track accumulated loss even when the rate of increase has slowed. The public story matters because it can change when people buy, how much cash they hold, and which institution they blame.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether higher expectations caused more spending, borrowing, or political action for the same families. It does not fully separate media exposure from party identity, trust, local prices, or financial stress.

Next test

Follow households from a measured price story through three months. Join complete media exposure, personal prices, wages, debt, expectations, trust, spending, saving, borrowing, and political action. Compare the national story with the household's own basket.

Reading rule

A headline can change what people expect without changing what they do.
