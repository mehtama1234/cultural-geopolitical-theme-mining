When the news selects the economic mood

The prices people hear about can shape what they expect next, even when the news does not show the full price picture.

In one sentence

A headline can change expectations without changing what a household buys or how it votes.

Keep exposure, belief, action, and political action separate.

What the sources show

High-price news moved expectations more than low-price news

A household-panel NBER study finds that exposure to higher-price news raises inflation expectations by 0.4 percentage point, while lower-price news has no significant effect. Another study finds that daily television coverage raises expectations, with stronger responses to positive CPI surprises and effects that fade after about ten days.

These studies do not show each household's full media diet, price basket, spending, or political response.

The connection

price movement → selected news and repetition → inflation expectation and cause belief → spending, saving, borrowing, or political judgment

Bad price news can report real harm. Lower-price news may be less common or less relevant to a household still facing a high price level.

The four outside views

Household: A family weighs a headline against rent, food, gas, wages, and debt. Repeated news may make a future bill feel urgent.

Company and sector: Media, retailers, lenders, employers, and firms choose what price change to show and explain. Their messages can change plans.

Money and finance: Expectations can affect saving, borrowing, and purchases, but actual results depend on income, rates, prices, and credit.

Public power: Data release, central-bank communication, media rules, and public information shape which price story reaches people.

What would change the finding

  • Household price basket, income, debt, and local prices.
  • Media exposure, source, trust, repetition, and timing.
  • Price news, expectation, cause belief, and policy preference.
  • Spending, saving, borrowing, turnout, voting, and public demand.
  • Differences by party, income, age, race, place, and education.
