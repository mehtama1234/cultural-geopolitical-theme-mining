Finding 003: Care is organized through multigenerational living, unpaid substitution, and gendered work tradeoffs

Status: provisional 2025 SHED care/work finding · Checked: 2026-09-17

The bounded finding

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey shows care as a social and economic institution rather than a single household burden. Thirty-three percent of adults lived with at least two adult generations; 49% of adults under 30 lived with a parent, up 12 percentage points from 2019. Among parents of children under 13, paid childcare was used by 25%, while 44% used some unpaid childcare. Median monthly childcare payments among families paying for both childcare and housing were $1,083, compared with $1,900 in housing payments.

Care is also distributed unevenly through gender and work. Among partnered parents of young children, 58% of mothers versus 12% of fathers said they were usually the primary caretaker; among dual-earner parents the comparison was 49% versus 8%. Thirty-seven percent of mothers versus 18% of fathers living with children under 13 were not working for pay. For adult care, 18% of adults regularly provided unpaid care to an adult relative or friend; 32% of prime-age caregivers had no paid job, rising to 44% among daily caregivers.

The safe societal trend is therefore narrower than “families are collapsing” or “care causes women to leave work”:

Households are adapting to care needs through co-residence, paid and unpaid substitution, and unequal caretaker roles; the same adaptation can provide support and belonging while also narrowing money, time, and paid-work options.

Mechanism map

child / adult care need
  -> paid care, unpaid network, co-residence, or direct time
  -> money, work, schedule, housing, and mobility tradeoff
  -> family meaning, dignity, support, strain, or conflict
  -> employer, provider, and public-system response
  -> later security, trust, political demand, or exit

The source directly measures living arrangements, care use, selected costs, caretaker roles, and employment associations. It does not follow a family through a dated care shock, alternative search, schedule change, health or income consequence, employer response, or later political meaning.

Boundaries and counterexamples

  • Multigenerational living can be chosen and protective, can pool housing and care, or can reflect constrained affordability; the cross-section cannot distinguish these pathways.
  • Paid childcare use is not care quality or access. Higher-income use can reflect employment and affordability, while lower use can reflect unpaid support, nonemployment, unavailable providers, or preference.
  • Unpaid care is not automatically sacrificed time. It can be reciprocal, meaningful, and flexible, but the survey does not measure hours or what it displaced.
  • Mothers' and fathers' caretaker reports differ partly in how “shared” roles are reported; the role gap is descriptive, not a causal estimate of gender norms, wages, or employment.
  • Adult-care questions focus on regular care and may omit irregular or very low-frequency caregivers. Caregiver nonemployment may reflect prior health, labor-market selection, household resources, or preference as well as care.

Next test

The strongest next design is a same-family or person panel that records the dated care need; paid, unpaid, and co-resident alternatives; price, travel, waiting, and schedule control; work and health response; employer or public support; and later return, persistence, recovery, trust, or political action. The current SHED result should be paired with ATUS diary evidence and a lawful longitudinal file, not treated as a causal time-displacement estimate.
