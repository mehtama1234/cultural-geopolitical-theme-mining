Connected US finding · housing, risk and credit

When rent protection becomes a financial product

Rent insurance can spread the cost of an income or health shock before a family loses its home. It can also add a cost before the crisis begins.

In one sentence

The people most likely to need rent protection may be the hardest to insure cheaply.

Keep the premium, claim, avoided eviction, and later security separate.

What the source shows

A model finds a risk-sharing case with a targeting problem

An NBER model covers a limited number of rent payments after an income or health shock. It finds higher welfare, smaller security deposits, and less homelessness. Unrestricted access is not financially viable in the model: private insurers target better-off renters while public coverage focuses on people most at risk.

The model does not measure premiums, take-up, claims, landlord response, or later credit.

NBER Working Paper 32582

The connection

income or health shock → rent cannot be paid → insurance claim, public aid, legal help, or no protection → landlord receives payment or starts a case → housing and credit path changes

Insurance changes who carries the risk and when. It does not remove the shock or make every rent affordable.

The four outside views

Household: Protection may prevent a move, deposit, or damaged credit record. A premium may be hard to pay before any claim.

Company and sector: Insurers, landlords, lenders, and aid groups decide how claims affect leases and access. The product may exclude renters with the highest risk.

Money and finance: Premium, deductible, coverage limit, exclusion, claim delay, and renewal price determine the value. A saved deposit is not a saved home.

Public power: Insurance rules, rent aid, eviction records, housing supply, and public coverage decide whether protection is broad or mainly sold to safer renters.

What would change the finding

  • Actual products, premiums, claims, exclusions, deductibles, and take-up.
  • Income, health, job, rent, deposit, landlord response, and claim timing.
  • Denials, coverage gaps, eviction, credit, housing quality, and later rent.
  • Who can afford coverage and who is screened out.
  • Public cost compared with direct rental aid and legal help.
