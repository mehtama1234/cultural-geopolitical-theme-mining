Stimulus can be politically effective without being economically well targeted

Status: full-paper-audited international comparison · Checked: 2026-09-14

The bounded finding

NBER Working Paper 33973 studies Italy's 2014 “80 Euro Bonus,” a salient monthly transfer of €80 to payroll employees in an income window that largely targeted middle-income workers. The paper combines administrative eligibility and recipient data with the universe of Italian municipal election returns and uses quasi-random geographic variation in recipient shares with difference-in- differences. It also uses placebo/pre-trend checks, an eligibility-rule instrument, a threshold design, and voter-panel evidence around receipt.

The reported pattern is a direct end-to-end political-economy mechanism:

salient transfer receipt
  -> immediate household resources and consumption response
  -> visible attribution to the incumbent party
  -> vote reward, persistence, or punishment when the transfer is withdrawn
  -> political incentives to preserve or target the program

The paper reports a 0.18 percentage-point increase in incumbent-party vote share for each one-percentage-point increase in recipient share in 2014. At the average municipality's 20.15% recipient share, the estimated short-run vote-share effect is about four percentage points. The electoral gain remains about 0.09 percentage points higher in high-recipient places in 2019, five years later.

Political response versus consumption response

The paper reports that beneficiaries spent about 20% of the bonus on non-durables and 30% on durables, with the rest saved, citing related consumption evidence. Its heterogeneity and counterfactual analysis says that shifting eligibility toward poorer potential recipients would increase the overall consumption response by roughly 30% but reduce projected electoral returns by at least 15%.

This is the important program-level distinction: the people most likely to spend a transfer are not necessarily the people whose receipt produces the largest electoral return. In the study, higher-income excluded individuals show stronger electoral backlash than poorer excluded individuals, while electoral returns are stronger where recipients are relatively closer to the middle of the income distribution.

What this adds to the US-centered atlas

This is not a US estimate. It is a comparison mechanism that makes the US research questions more precise. A US stimulus or public-benefit analysis should not assume that receipt, consumption, trust, attribution, and vote are the same outcome. It should separately measure:

Stage Required US evidence
Exposure Eligibility, actual receipt, timing, salience, and amount
Material response Spending, debt, saving, food, health, work, and care outcomes
Meaning Who the recipient credits or blames; fairness, dignity, and legitimacy
Political response Vote, turnout, contact, organization, or withdrawal
Persistence Later receipt, repeal/revocation, recovery, and party/institutional response

The Italy evidence also supplies a strong counterexample to a simple “more help means more consumption and more political support” model. Targeting can be economically efficient while politically weak, or politically efficient while economically poorly targeted.

Limits and counterexamples

  • Italy's transfer rules, party system, proportional elections, payroll administration, and income distribution differ from the United States.
  • Municipality-level recipient shares do not identify every recipient's vote; the voter panel helps with individual switching but does not turn all aggregate results into a person-level estimate.
  • The €80 payment was salient in paychecks and arrived just before a national election; a less visible benefit or delayed tax credit may produce a different attribution path.
  • Consumption response and electoral reward can diverge; neither is a complete measure of household welfare or trust.
  • The counterfactual targeting comparison is model-based and should not be imported as a US forecast.

Next US test

Use the design as a template for US public-system and stimulus records: link benefit eligibility and actual receipt to timing, household resources, spending/food/work outcomes, prior trust, attribution, and later action. Keep the existing SIPP, SHED, USDA, ANES, CCES, and administrative layers separate unless a valid same-person or same-case join exists.
