When card rewards become part of the price

A card reward looks like a private benefit. The fee that helps fund it can enter the price paid by many shoppers, including people who never receive the reward.

The argument

A merchant pays different costs for cash, debit, and credit. With a rewards card, part of the merchant’s payment fee can help fund points or cash back. The merchant may recover that cost through a common price, a surcharge, a cash discount, a smaller margin, or a decision about which cards to accept.

That creates different deals beneath one shelf price. A customer who pays a card balance in full may gain. A cash or debit customer may help fund the reward without receiving it. A customer short on cash may receive the reward and then lose more through interest.

The useful measure is not “cash back.” It is the household’s net cost after the price, reward, interest, access, and alternatives are counted.

Follow one grocery purchase

A store posts one price for a basket of groceries. A shopper uses a rewards card and earns points. The store does not receive the full posted amount: money is taken by the payment network, issuer, or processor. The store must decide whether to absorb that cost, spread it across prices, charge a permitted fee, offer a cash discount, or stop accepting the card.

payment method → merchant fee → price, discount, surcharge, or margin response → reward for one customer and no reward for another → interest for a customer who carries the balance → different household cost and cash room

Now compare two households. Household A pays the card balance every month and uses the points. Household B pays cash or debit and receives no points. If the store uses one common price, B can help pay for the reward given to A. Household A may still lose overall if it carries a balance and pays interest. A third household may want the reward but lack the card, credit limit, or cash flow to use it safely.

This is why “the card gives 2% back” is incomplete. The real questions are: 2% back from what price, after what merchant cost, for which customer, and after any interest?

The three transfers

1. The store fee

The merchant pays for accepting the payment. That cost may be spread across customers or taken from the merchant’s margin.

2. The reward

Value returns mainly to customers with the right card, enough spending, and the ability to pay in full.

3. The interest bill

A customer who carries the balance can send money back to the issuer through interest and late fees.

The same purchase can contain all three. Looking only at the visible reward makes a system-wide transfer look like a free benefit.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not yet show the complete net cost for the same US households over time. It does not tell us how payment fees change grocery prices in each place, how often low-income households pay interest after using rewards cards, or whether cash discounts reach the people most affected. It does not prove that one payment rule caused later debt or financial stress.

Next test

Follow households and merchants for one year, ideally around a rule change or matched stores. Record payment method, posted price, surcharge or discount, reward, interest, credit limit, balance payment, merchant acceptance, processing cost, location, and the purchase that would have been made if the first option were unavailable. The key output is the net cost of the purchase and the cash left after it.

Reading rule: keep the merchant fee, common price, reward, interest bill, and household net cost separate.
