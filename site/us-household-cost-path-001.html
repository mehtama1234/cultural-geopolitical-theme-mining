When household protection becomes a public feeling

Short answer

Three separate studies point to three different ways a household can feel a cost: a higher bill to protect a home, a payment system that rewards some shoppers more than others, and the loss of a child benefit. Together they suggest a useful line of inquiry: household pressure may be felt through both money paid now and money no longer arriving.

The common unit is not “inflation” or “financial stress.” It is room to absorb the next problem. A household can lose room through a higher required bill, a price it cannot avoid, or support it expected to receive. Those paths can meet in the same budget even when the firms, agencies, and rules behind them are different.

This is a connected reading, not a proven chain. The sources do not follow the same households from insurance payments to card use to economic views. The purpose is to join compatible mechanisms without pretending that correlation across studies is a causal result.

What the sources show

1. Protection can raise the monthly cost of keeping a home

Keys and Mulder study more than 74 million insurance premiums inferred from mortgage escrow payments from 2014 through 2024. They estimate that rising premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes.

This means the cost can appear twice: as a larger bill and as slower growth in the value of the home. An owner may pay more to keep protection while the asset grows more slowly. The estimate is for the most exposed ZIP codes; it is not a $40,000 loss for every homeowner.

NBER Working Paper 32579

2. The checkout price can hide who funds rewards

Egan, Matvos, Seru, Wang and Yao use merchant payment data to estimate that interchange fees move about $30 billion each year from cash and debit users to credit-card users. Different shoppers and different merchant fees reduce the estimated transfer by about 25%, but do not remove it.

The result is an estimate about the payment system, not a direct bill sent to each cash or debit user. It depends on how merchants pass fees into prices and on where different shoppers buy things. It does not tell us whether a particular household could have avoided the price or earned the reward.

NBER Working Paper 35067

3. Losing a benefit can change how people judge the economy

Bastian and Harvey estimate that every $1,000 lost when the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit expired lowered consumer sentiment by 1.7 points. The effect lasted nearly two years and was largest among lower-income families with several children.

This gives us evidence that a change in money arriving at home can affect economic views. The study does not show that the change altered votes or that every family used the lost money in the same way.

NBER Working Paper 35059

The careful connection

The order of the shocks can change the result. A family with a reserve may absorb an insurance increase and later receive the tax credit. The same family may be forced into debt if the benefit ends first and the premium rises later. Three households can face the same annual total and have different outcomes because one has time to adjust, one has to borrow, and one loses a key backup before the next bill arrives.

The household also decides which risk to keep. It may drop coverage, carry a card balance, reduce food quality, delay a repair, or ask a relative for help. Those are not equivalent forms of adjustment. A combined index can show that pressure rose, but only a sequence of decisions can show what the household protected and what it made fragile.

The three findings fit one question: what does a household experience when the money available for protection, purchases or children changes? A home-insurance bill reaches the monthly budget and the value of an asset. Payment rules shape the price paid by people using different methods. A lost benefit changes the income a family expected to receive.

Follow one household through a year. In spring, its insurance renewal adds a required monthly payment. During ordinary shopping, a common price includes payment costs while rewards go to a customer with a premium card. In summer, a child benefit the family had planned around expires. The household may cut savings, use credit, delay a repair, change food brands, or work more hours. By winter, it may describe the economy as worse, but no single record will show the whole path.

The three mechanisms also have different clocks:

Pressure Immediate effect Later exposure
Insurance More cash required to keep the home protected Less coverage, delayed repair, lower asset value, or sale risk
Payment design Different effective price or reward by payment method Debt, reduced savings, or a change in where the household shops
Lost support Less income available for children and bills Lower buffer, changed spending, and a changed view of the economy

This table is a way to organize the next test, not evidence that the same household experienced all three effects.

The sources support those separate statements. They do not prove that insurance costs caused a person to change payment method, or that either caused a political response. The useful finding at this stage is that “cost of living” has more than one clock: bills can rise, assets can grow more slowly, and expected support can disappear.

The larger insight is about the loss of slack. A household may continue paying every bill and still become less secure because its savings, time, coverage, or ability to switch has fallen. That is why a snapshot of income or consumption can miss the change. The same paycheck can support a stable life in one year and a fragile one in the next if the backups have disappeared.

The order of the cuts matters. A household may first stop adding to savings, then use a card, then delay care or repairs, and only later miss a required bill. By the time a missed payment appears, the real change began months earlier when the buffer was used up. Two families with the same current spending can therefore have different futures: one is spending from income, the other is spending from borrowed money or a shrinking reserve.

The pressures can reinforce one another without having the same cause. A higher insurance bill leaves less cash for a card balance. Card debt makes the lost tax credit more painful. A thinner buffer makes an unexpected repair or premium increase harder to absorb. The household's later response may look like a reaction to one price, while the combined loss of room came from several smaller demands. This is why a single “cost of living” index can be useful for scale but weak for explaining the household decision.

There is also a question of who receives protection. Insurance keeps an asset covered but may not protect a renter, a homeowner with a high deductible, or a family that drops coverage. Card rewards return money to people who can qualify for and safely use credit, while the price of the goods may be shared across shoppers. A tax credit arrives through the public system but only changes a family's room if it is expected, received, and large enough to meet the next bill. The same policy can therefore look generous in aggregate and thin at the kitchen table.

What would change the finding

This is where public trust can diverge from the average. A family may hear that the economy is strong while its reserve, coverage, and ability to switch have all narrowed. It may not identify one cause, but it knows that ordinary bad luck now produces a larger bill. That experience can affect blame or support for policy, but this synthesis does not prove a political effect.

The central question is the point of no longer having a backup. Before that point, a household can absorb a shock. After it, the next shock is passed into debt, care, housing, work, or a family member. Finding that threshold may be more useful than ranking which single bill rose the most.

  • Same-household data linking insurance costs, payment method, income and economic views.
  • A comparison of households that lost different amounts of protection or public support.
  • Evidence on whether people cut care, borrowing, saving or housing quality first.
  • State and local records showing whether these pressures became complaints, policy demands or votes.
  • The household’s remaining savings, credit room, coverage, time, and ability to switch after each shock.

Next test

Use an event ledger with dates for each renewal, payment, benefit change, debt draw, missed care, repair, and switch. Give each household a simple monthly statement showing what changed and what remained. Follow whether the family regained its reserve or entered the next shock with less room. This would turn “cost of living” from a single feeling into a visible sequence of lost and restored backups.

Pair the Federal Reserve’s household survey measures with insurance, payment, benefit, and credit records by state and income group. Follow households before and after each pressure, recording the remaining savings, coverage, credit room, time, and alternatives. Treat this as a comparison of related pressures until a dataset follows the same people over time.

Build a monthly “room” measure rather than relying on income alone: required bills, liquid savings, available credit, insurance coverage, unpaid care time, and the cost of changing providers. Ask households which backup they used first and which one disappeared next. Compare families facing one pressure with families facing two or three at once, and repeat the sentiment measure after each bill or benefit change. The test should identify the first point at which a household stops absorbing a shock and starts passing it into debt, care, housing, work, or political judgment.
