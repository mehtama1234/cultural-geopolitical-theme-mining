Connected US memo · household cost and public judgment

When household protection becomes a public feeling

A household can lose room through a higher bill, a price it cannot avoid, or support it expected to receive. Those paths can meet in one budget even when the firms, agencies, and rules behind them are different.

Read this carefully

This is a connected reading, not a proven chain. The sources do not follow the same households from insurance payments to card use to economic views.

The common question is not “financial stress.” It is: how much room remains when the next problem arrives?

Three separate findings

1. Protection can raise the cost of keeping a home

Keys and Mulder study more than 74 million insurance premiums inferred from mortgage escrow payments from 2014 through 2024. They estimate that rising premiums reduced home-price growth by more than $40,000 in the most exposed ZIP codes. The cost can appear as a larger bill and slower growth in the home’s value.

NBER Working Paper 32579

2. The checkout price can hide who funds rewards

Egan, Matvos, Seru, Wang, and Yao estimate that interchange fees move about $30 billion each year from cash and debit users to credit-card users. Different shoppers and merchant fees reduce the estimated transfer by about 25%, but do not remove it. This is a system estimate, not a direct bill sent to each cash user.

NBER Working Paper 35067

3. Losing a benefit can change economic views

Bastian and Harvey estimate that every $1,000 lost when the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit expired lowered consumer sentiment by 1.7 points. The effect lasted nearly two years and was largest among lower-income families with several children. This does not show that the change altered votes.

NBER Working Paper 35059

Follow one household for a year

In spring, an insurance renewal adds a required monthly payment. During ordinary shopping, a common price includes payment costs while rewards go to a customer with a premium card. In summer, a child benefit the family planned around expires. The household may cut savings, use credit, delay a repair, change food brands, or work more hours. By winter, it may describe the economy as worse, but no single record shows the whole path.

The careful connection

The sources support three separate statements. They do not prove that insurance costs caused a person to change payment method, or that either caused a political response. The useful connection is that “cost of living” has more than one clock: bills can rise, assets can grow more slowly, and expected support can disappear.

The larger insight is the loss of slack. A household may continue paying every bill and still become less secure because its savings, time, coverage, or ability to switch has fallen. A snapshot of income or consumption can miss that change.

What would change the finding

  • Same-household data linking insurance costs, payment method, income, benefits, and economic views.
  • The household’s remaining savings, credit room, coverage, time, and ability to switch after each shock.
  • Evidence on whether people cut care, borrowing, saving, or housing quality first.
  • State and local records showing whether these pressures became complaints, policy demands, or votes.

Next test

Pair Federal Reserve household measures with insurance, payment, benefit, and credit records by state and income group. Follow households before and after each pressure. Record whether the remaining room changed and what the household gave up to preserve it.
