Connected US finding · prices and public judgment

When the price people see becomes the economy they judge

A slower rise in prices can still feel like a continuing loss when wages have not caught up and cheaper choices are no longer cheap.

The argument

People judge the economy through repeated purchases. A family may buy less, switch stores, save less, use credit, or stock up. A changed purchase is not automatically a vote.

The household basket is where public judgment begins.

Follow one basket through a year

A family buys a budget brand, pays rent, fills the car, and saves a little. The budget brand rises faster than the premium brand. Trading down no longer gives the same relief. The family buys less, delays a repair, or uses credit. A later headline says inflation is cooling, but the family still starts from the higher price level.

price or wage change → repeated checkout experience and perceived buying power → trading down, buying less, saving less, or stockpiling → view of firms, government, and the future → political demand

What the sources establish

EvidenceWhat it means
Two representative US surveys in an NBER study find people mainly dislike inflation because they believe wages are not keeping up with prices.The felt problem is the pay–price gap.
Those beliefs are linked to reported changes in shopping and saving choices.Economic belief can sit near household action, but is not proof of it.
HBS reports the cheapest US food products in its study rose 30% from January 2020 to May 2024, versus 22% for the most expensive foods.Trading down may not protect the budget as before.
HBS research links price increases to fairness, value, and demand.A higher price changes what a customer thinks is worth buying.
The sources do not join one household's prices, wages, purchases, media, trust, and political action over time.The public-judgment link remains an inference.

Sources: NBER inflation, HBS Cheapflation, and HBS pricing.

The deeper finding

Inflation is lived as both a price and a story about the price. A lower rate may calm the headline while the accumulated loss remains in the budget.

What remains unproven

The sources do not show whether one family's basket caused a change in trust or a vote, or whether a cheaper substitute remained equally useful or safe.

Next test

Follow households for one year with actual prices, wages, product choices, savings, debt, and local news. Ask when they felt the loss, what they blamed, and whether the response changed a purchase, trust, or political action.

Reading rule

Do not use the national inflation rate as a household story. Do not treat a slower price rise as a price cut.
