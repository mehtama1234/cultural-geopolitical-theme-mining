Finding 001: A formal broker relationship can exist without verified consumer control

Status: bounded health-insurance, consumer-recourse, and institutional-trust finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

GAO reports that agents and brokers helping consumers buy or change plans on the federal health-insurance Marketplace must be licensed and registered, and CMS restricts system access to registered agents and brokers. Those controls do not reliably prove that the consumer authorized each action.

GAO found that CMS's consent processes are weak, an agent or broker's access is not limited to the consumer already associated with that enrollment, and consumers are not informed of all agent or broker actions. Procedures added in 2024 were not always used and included limited identity confirmation.

The number of complaints tied to confirmed unauthorized enrollments and plan switches grew more than fourfold from 2023 through 2025. GAO also reported that at least 160,000 federal Marketplace applications in plan year 2024 had likely unauthorized changes. An unauthorized change could leave a consumer unaware that coverage changed or could result in loss of coverage.

The route is:

consumer seeks coverage
  -> broker assistance and registration
  -> consent and identity verification
  -> enrollment or plan change
  -> consumer notice and ability to correct
  -> retained coverage and trust [not fully protected]

What this adds to the broad atlas

This adds a health-insurance version of a recurring control problem:

A visible formal route and a licensed intermediary do not guarantee that the person affected can see, authorize, or reverse the transaction.

The harm is not only financial. An unauthorized plan switch can change access to doctors, prescriptions, premiums, tax credits, and continuity of care.

Limits and counterevidence

  • The complaint count is a confirmed-complaint measure, not the prevalence of all unauthorized activity or all Marketplace consumers.
  • “Likely unauthorized” applications are an investigative estimate, not a final finding about every application or broker.
  • The review concerns federal Marketplace controls and three selected state Marketplaces; it is not a national estimate for all insurance transactions.
  • California, Georgia, and New Mexico had controls beyond CMS's, including one-time passcodes for some consent actions. The problem is therefore not an inevitable feature of broker assistance.
  • A stronger consent control does not by itself establish that consumers kept coverage, received adequate plans, or trusted the Marketplace afterward.

The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected plan-change ledger joining consumer authorization, identity check, broker identity, plan and subsidy change, notice, complaint, correction, coverage continuity, and later care use.
