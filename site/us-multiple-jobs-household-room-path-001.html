Connected US finding · multiple jobs, work and household room

When equal income buys a different life

Two people can bring home similar pay and still have very different lives.

In one sentence

Several jobs can buy flexibility while taking more travel, time, benefits, and saving room from the worker.

Count net pay and time, not gross income alone.

What the sources show

Similarly paid people with multiple jobs spent and saved differently

HBS research using surveys, General Social Survey data, and transactions from more than 90,000 bank customers reports that similarly paid people with multiple jobs spent 17 percentage points less of take-home pay, including 16 points less on necessities and almost 2 points less on extras. They had higher transport spending and saved less.

A separate HBS study of DoorDash drivers in California estimates that schedule flexibility has meaningful money value for some groups.

HBS gig-work study · HBS flexibility study

The connection

one job is not available, suitable, or flexible enough → several jobs or gig work → split schedule, travel, and uncertain income → cautious spending, less saving, or less care → short-run flexibility versus long-run security → firm staffing choices and public debate

Lower spending may mean pressure, deliberate saving, or different goals. The key account is net pay and time.

The four outside views

Household: A worker weighs schedule control against travel, fuel, care, sleep, health, and the risk that one job disappears. Flexibility may be worth money.

Company and sector: Firms offer flexibility, stable hours, benefits, training, or piece-rate work. A company may shift waiting time, vehicle cost, and income risk onto the worker.

Money and finance: Count take-home pay after travel and work costs, then savings, debt, insurance, paid care, and unpaid time.

Public power: Labor rules, benefits, taxes, transportation, and care support shape whether several jobs are a choice or a gap-filler.

What would change the finding

  • Hours, schedules, travel, vehicle cost, waiting time, and work expenses.
  • Gross and net income, benefits, taxes, debt, savings, and income swings.
  • Care, health, education, household size, and reasons for another job.
  • Job quality, control, safety, training, promotion, and later work.
  • Differences by race, gender, age, disability, place, and immigration status.
  • Whether one stable flexible job produces the same pattern.

Next test

Follow workers for two years before and after they add or leave a job. Join pay records to hours, travel, care, benefits, spending, savings, debt, health, and job quality.

Reading rule

Do not call several jobs flexible without counting the travel and risk.
