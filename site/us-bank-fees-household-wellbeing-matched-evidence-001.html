A short argument · US bank fees, overdraft, and cash

A bank fee can be small on paper and large in a thin budget

A bank rule can look equal while its value depends on how much cash a household has.

The argument

Removing an NSF fee is direct relief. Overdraft access is different: it can keep a payment moving, but only helps if the household can recover before the next charge.

Follow one low-balance day

A paycheck is two days away. A utility payment arrives today. The bank can reject it, charge an NSF fee, or cover it with overdraft. The payment result shapes the next move: pay a fee, use a payday lender, delay food, borrow, or repay after payday.

low account cash → payment rule → fee, overdraft, or failure → payment succeeds or fails → later fee, interest, or alternative credit → household room and trust change

NBER bank-fee study

The deeper finding

Liquidity is the hidden divider. A household with little cash may need payment access most and gain the least from a rule that assumes quick repayment.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not link one account event to the bill, food, housing, health, work, later borrowing, and trust of the same household.

Next test

Join account records to the payment event that caused the shortage. Measure fees, overdraft, repayment, payday borrowing, payment success, and later essential outcomes by liquidity.

Reading rule

Do not call a fee cut broad household relief until you show what it removed, saved, or changed.
