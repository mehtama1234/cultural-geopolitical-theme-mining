The same income can buy a different life by place

The argument

A national income number can hide a local customer reality. The same paycheck buys less housing, food, transport, or care in one place than another. The difference matters most when a household cannot easily move, switch providers, work remotely, or trade down to a cheaper product.

A place is not simply cheap or expensive. It also offers jobs, services, safety, family ties, and ways to get around. The careful finding is narrower: local prices change real buying power, and the burden is not evenly spread. A low-income family has fewer ways to escape a high price than a high-income family.

The price on the shelf is only one part of place. A cheaper store may be farther away, require a car, or have less choice. A cheaper home may add a long commute, childcare cost, or weaker access to care. A high-rent area may also put a job, school, clinic, and family network within reach. Comparing places requires the full cost of getting the needed goods and services, not only the rent or grocery receipt.

Follow two similar paychecks

Two workers earn the same annual wage. One pays high rent but has a short commute. The other pays less rent but needs a car and has fewer nearby services. A state price index may show a gap, but each household's real life depends on the basket it must buy and the options it can change.

If the first worker cannot move without losing a job or family support, the price gap becomes a constraint rather than a preference.

The adjustment options are unequal. A higher-income household may buy near home, order online, move, or wait for a sale. A lower-income household may need to buy the closest package today, take a costly bus, or accept a smaller home in a less connected area. The local price is therefore multiplied by time, distance, and the money required to reach an alternative.

Local wages do not solve the problem automatically. A job may pay more because the place is expensive, but the extra pay can be consumed by rent, transport, childcare, or health costs. Another job may pay less but offer a shorter commute or a stable schedule. Annual wage comparisons miss the work and expense needed to turn income into a usable life.

Place also changes the ability to absorb a shock. A family near several stores may switch after a price increase; a family in a food desert may have no close substitute. A tenant in a tight market may pay more rather than move. A rural household may face a lower rent but a large vehicle claim for every essential trip. The same national policy can therefore produce different effects because the local alternatives differ.

What the sources actually establish

Step Evidence Meaning
Local prices BEA's 2024 state RPPs range from 110.7 in California to 86.9 in Arkansas; housing-rent RPPs range from 154.3 in California to 54.2 in West Virginia. Place changes the price of the same broad basket.
Unequal exposure NBER builds price indexes for more than 140 products and finds nearly twice the spatial spread for low-income as high-income households. Low-income households face a wider place-based price range.
Real consumption NBER finds high-school graduates and dropouts in expensive places have lower real consumption. High prices can show up as less actual consumption.
Higher-income response NBER finds little relationship between local prices and real consumption for college graduates with similar observable traits. More resources can provide ways to absorb or avoid the gap.
Missing household result The records do not show one family's full basket, service quality, job access, and moving cost. A place index is not a household budget.

Sources: BEA Regional Price Parities, BEA real consumer spending, and NBER local prices and consumption.

The deeper finding

Affordability is a relation between a place and a household, not a label attached to a state. The same price can be manageable for one family and displacing for another. Public policy should ask whether wages, housing supply, transit, health care, schools, and local services offset the price gap for people with the least room.

This is why buying power should be measured after required costs. A household may have a higher nominal wage and less money left after rent, transport, insurance, and care. It may have lower measured consumption because it is choosing less, not because it needs less. A regional price index identifies the pressure; it does not tell us which option disappeared.

There is a hidden market structure behind the average. When a place has one grocery store, one hospital, one broadband provider, or few landlords, a household cannot easily shop around. When many providers compete, the same national price increase may be softened by switching. Local concentration and local mobility interact. A place can be expensive because demand is high, but it can also be expensive for people who have no practical exit.

The political meaning is close to home. Residents may blame wages, landlords, stores, taxes, newcomers, or public rules for the same squeeze. Those explanations can point to different parts of the chain. A useful finding does not start with the blame. It shows the local bill, the income, the available alternatives, and the cost of changing them. That is how a broad price measure becomes a grounded account of public anger or support.

The deepest connection is between place and permission. A household is “free” to move, switch stores, change jobs, or use transit only if it can pay the transition cost and keep the needed service. Local affordability is therefore partly a question of how many workable alternatives a family has.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show whether a family moved, changed products, lost well-being, or received better services because of the local price difference. It also does not capture family help, debt, quality, safety, or the cost of leaving.

It also does not establish that higher local prices are a net loss when they buy shorter travel, safer conditions, better services, or higher wages. Nor does it show how quickly families can adjust or who bears the cost when they do. A price index and real consumption measure are important evidence, but they do not identify the household's counterfactual place.

Next test

Compare similar families across two high-price and two low-price commuting zones. Follow income, rent, food, transport, health, care, job access, family support, quality, savings, debt, and moves before calling a place more or less affordable.

Add travel time, store distance, provider concentration, childcare, insurance, utility, and one-time moving costs. Record what the family could not change, what it substituted, and how long adjustment took. Compare households with similar income and needs but different access to stores, jobs, transit, and family support. Report money left after required costs, dependable time, service quality, and the cost of leaving—not only the regional price index.

Reading rule

Do not call a place cheap or expensive without naming the household group and the things it must buy.
