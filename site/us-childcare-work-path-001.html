When family care becomes paid-work time

Childcare is paid for in more than one currency. Some families pay a large cash bill. Some rely on relatives. Some lose work time when care is too expensive, unavailable or badly timed.

In one sentence

Family time and paid work are linked through an everyday service that households arrange privately.

Paid care can enable work, and unpaid care can be valuable support.

What the sources show

1. Paid care is a large monthly payment

The Fed found that 24% of parents with children under 13 used paid care in the prior week. The median payment was $240 per week, or about $960 per month; for 20 or more hours, the median was $1,400.

Federal Reserve care and living arrangements

2. Families also pay with relatives' time

Forty-six percent of parents with children under 13 used some unpaid care from someone other than the parent. Relatives, especially grandparents, were common providers.

Federal Reserve childcare tables

3. Care problems can show up as lost work days

Census estimates that households with care problems lost an average of 65.8 work days in 2024. This is an average among affected households, not a national average or a causal estimate.

Census child-care challenges paper

4. The work effect is not shared equally

Among parents living with children under 13, 36% of women were not working for pay compared with 17% of men. The Fed also reports that mothers more often identified childcare as part of that choice.

Federal Reserve care and work findings

The connection

child needs care → paid-care price, supply problem, or unpaid family arrangement → money cost, lost work time, or a parent changing work → food, housing, debt, health, status, or public-help choice

The sources support the cost, care-arrangement and reported work-time pieces. They do not prove which option a household would choose under a different care price or supply.

What is still unknown

  • Would the family pay more, rely on a relative, change shifts, reduce hours or leave work?
  • How do wages, schedules, commute and local care supply change the choice?
  • Does lost work time change food security, credit use, health care or benefit eligibility?

Next test

Use Census SIPP or CPS care questions to compare households with similar children and wages but different care disruptions. Track paid care, unpaid care, work hours and income in the same period.

Reading rule

The finding concerns unequal choices and tradeoffs, not a judgment about paid or unpaid care.
