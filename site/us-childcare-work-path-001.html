Connected US finding · childcare and work

When family care becomes paid-work time

Childcare is paid for in more than one currency: cash, family hours, lost shifts, and work choices.

The argument

Care can make work possible and still consume much of the pay it enables. Unpaid care can be loving support and still depend on a relative’s health, distance, and schedule.

Ask who supplies the hours, what care costs, and what the family gives up.

What the sources establish

The Federal Reserve found that 24% of parents with children under 13 used paid care in the prior week; the median payment was $240 a week, about $960 a month. Forty-six percent used some unpaid care from someone other than the child’s parent.

A Census working paper estimates that 23.9% of households with children under 13 paid for care in 2024, averaging $10,520 a year. Among households reporting inadequate care, average lost work time was 65.8 days. That is not a national average or causal estimate.

Follow the care choice

child needs care → price, supply, schedule, or unpaid-family constraint → cash cost, lost work time, or parent work change → food, housing, debt, health, and benefit choices

A parent may pay for care and still lose a shift because the provider closes early. Another may avoid the bill by asking a relative, who gives up work or rest.

Keep these apart

  • Paid care and unpaid care.
  • Care price and care availability.
  • Parent not working and care as the cause.
  • Work enabled and work made less valuable.

Next test

Compare households with similar children and wages but different care disruptions. Track paid care, unpaid care, work hours, income, commute, subsidies, food, health, debt, and housing.

Reading rule

The finding concerns unequal choices and tradeoffs, not a judgment about either form of care.
