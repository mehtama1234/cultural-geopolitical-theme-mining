Insurance can turn a place risk into a staying problem

The argument

Owning a home does not mean the cost of staying is fixed. In a high-risk place, insurance can become a second housing payment. The household must keep coverage to protect the home and often to satisfy a mortgage, but the premium, deductible, or loss of coverage can make the home harder to keep.

There is a second price in the record. A lower credit score can raise the insurance price even when two homes receive identical coverage. The household may therefore pay more because of where the home sits and because of what its financial history says.

The result is a pressure point between property and household cash. A higher premium may lead to a repair, a larger deductible, borrowing, a state-backed plan, a sale, or no coverage. The evidence does not tell us which choice every family makes. It shows why “Can I afford the mortgage?” is no longer enough.

The premium is only the entry price for protection. A deductible is the amount the family must produce after the loss. An exclusion can remove the event the family thought it had insured. A policy limit can leave rebuilding short. A claim can take months while the household pays for temporary housing, transport, storage, or repairs. The real question is whether the policy turns a disaster into a manageable bill or leaves the family with a second crisis.

Follow the cost of staying

Imagine a homeowner whose premium rises after a series of storms. The mortgage payment has not changed, but the total monthly cost has. The homeowner can absorb the increase, reduce another expense, raise the deductible, postpone a repair, or look for another insurer.

If coverage becomes unavailable, the problem gets larger. A mortgage lender may still require insurance. A household may face a choice between an expensive last-resort plan and an unprotected home. If the home sells, the insurance price may also affect its value and the next buyer's ability to finance it.

The home is still standing. The staying decision has changed.

The household may respond by accepting more risk rather than leaving. It can raise the deductible, drop optional coverage, delay roof or drainage work, or use a state-backed plan with different terms. These choices can reduce the monthly bill while making the next storm more damaging. A home that remains insured may be less protected than before. Coverage status alone cannot show the quality of protection.

Insurance also affects the buyer who has not moved in. A buyer may qualify for a mortgage but discover that the premium, deductible, or required repairs break the budget. A seller may lower the price to compensate, but the lower price can be read as a sign of risk and make financing harder. The insurance problem can therefore reduce both the number of willing buyers and the household's ability to sell without a loss.

What the sources actually establish

Step Evidence Meaning
Place risk GAO reports inflation-adjusted average premiums rose from 2019 to 2024, with much larger increases in some southern coastal areas. Risk can become a location-specific housing cost.
Availability GAO reports higher disaster risk can reduce private-insurance availability. A price increase can become a coverage problem.
Home value NBER estimates more than $40,000 less home-price growth in the most exposed ZIP codes. Risk can affect the asset as well as the bill.

Sources: GAO homeowners insurance review, NBER Working Paper 32579, and NBER Working Paper 34848.

The important split

Four things are often collapsed into “insurance is expensive”: the premium, the deductible, whether coverage is available, and whether the home still holds value. They affect different decisions.

A family can pay a higher premium and stay. Another can accept a high deductible and take more risk. A third can use a state plan. A fourth can sell. A fifth can remain uninsured because the alternatives are worse. The premium data alone cannot tell us which path occurred.

Credit-based pricing adds a fairness question, but the evidence needs care. A score may capture payment history, debt, or other risk signals; it does not by itself prove why the insurer charged more. The Washington policy change is useful because it shows that changing the use of credit information can weaken the price link. It does not settle every reason for the difference.

There is a fifth part: claim performance. Two policies with the same limit can produce different household outcomes if one pays quickly, measures damage differently, or offers a workable temporary-home benefit. A nonrenewal after a claim can also turn protection into a future access problem. The policy must be evaluated at the moment the household needs it, not only when the premium is quoted.

Risk can be priced into the place itself. If coverage becomes expensive or unavailable, homeowners may invest less, sell earlier, or avoid buying in the area. A lender may tighten its rules, a local government may face pressure for a public plan, and a household may remain because moving costs exceed the premium. The same storm risk can become a household cash problem, an asset problem, and a local tax or public-insurance problem.

The political question is who should carry disaster risk. Private insurance spreads some risk across policyholders but can withdraw from places it sees as too costly. Public plans preserve access but can expose taxpayers or policyholders to losses. Building rules and mitigation can lower future damage but require money and control today. The choice is not simply private versus public insurance; it is where the risk sits before and after the storm.

What remains unproven

The sources establish rising premiums, weaker availability in some places, changed home-price growth, and a credit-linked price difference. They do not prove that a particular family moved, defaulted, lost coverage, or became uninsured because of insurance.

They also do not show whether a policy paid enough to rebuild, how often households accept unsafe underinsurance, or whether credit-based pricing reflects risk that could be measured without the score. Premium and home-price data do not establish claim quality, temporary housing, repair delay, or the household's ability to remain.

The next test should follow the same property through premium, deductible, coverage, mortgage, repairs, claims, credit, sale, and move. It should separate renters, mortgaged owners, and owners without mortgages.

Build a property-year ledger with premium, deductible, exclusions, limits, insurer, renewal or nonrenewal, claim, payment timing, repair, temporary housing, mortgage, credit, mitigation, sale, and next-home insurance. Compare similar homes across risk zones and credit profiles, including public and private coverage. Measure total annual cost, uncovered loss, days displaced, repair completion, mortgage stress, sale price, buyer financing, and the household's decision to stay or leave. The useful result is not a lower premium alone; it is durable protection at a cost the household can carry.

Reading rule

A higher premium is a housing warning, not proof that a family moved. Keep risk, price, coverage, value, and household action separate.
