Finding 002: The first payoff may be movement, not pay

Status: provisional
Last checked: 2026-09-11
Project: AI, work, and control

Finding

The strongest early labor-market signal is not a general pay increase. It is movement: some workers use AI to handle tasks or enter roles that once required scarce experience, while the average pay and hours of adopters remain close to the path of comparable non-adopters.

That movement could be an opening for workers, a way for firms to redraw jobs, or both. The evidence does not yet tell us which.

Evidence read more closely

The Denmark study links large worker and workplace surveys to administrative records. It finds no detectable average effect larger than 2% on earnings or recorded hours two years after ChatGPT's launch. It does find that adopters worked about 4% more of a full-time equivalent in their most recent occupation by December 2024. The paper says this movement is strongest toward IT support and office-support roles, where workers have more control over their tool choices, and that the association is stronger for individual use than for employer initiatives. NBER Working Paper 33777

The customer-support study gives a different kind of signal. Its AI tool was designed to assist, not replace, agents. It raised measured productivity and appears to have helped workers continue performing better during some periods when the tool was unavailable. The authors also warn that outages are rare and may not be random, so the learning result is suggestive rather than final. NBER Working Paper 31161

Causal chain under test

AI assistance -> faster task learning or lower entry barrier
-> worker can move into a different task or occupation
-> job structure changes before pay changes
-> power depends on whether movement is chosen, rewarded, or required

The first two arrows have evidence. The last arrow is open.

Why this matters socially

If AI lowers the experience needed to enter a role, it may widen access for some workers. It may also weaken the value of experience for workers already inside the role. The same tool can therefore help newcomers and unsettle incumbents.

This is a more precise question than “will AI replace jobs?” We need to ask who can move, who must move, who has time to learn, and who owns the records used to judge competence.

Four-map reading

Map What the evidence suggests What we need next
Material movement without an average wage gain pay paths, job quality, hours, and firm margins
Social entry and status may change unevenly worker accounts, age, gender, disability, class, and migration
Institution employer policy is not the same as individual use workplace rules, training, unions, and regulation
Power tool access may change who can cross a skill barrier data ownership, review rights, liability, and bargaining

Strongest challenge

Workers who are already more mobile may both adopt AI and change occupations. The paper's event-study and controls reduce this concern, but association is not the same as proof that AI caused every move. The project needs evidence from workplaces where adoption timing or access rules provide a cleaner comparison.

What would change our mind

  • Evidence that occupational movement is entirely explained by pre-existing worker mobility.
  • Evidence that movers do not gain access to better tasks, pay paths, or choice.
  • Evidence that AI assistance improves measured output but does not change learning or task access.

Next tests

  1. Compare the Denmark mobility result with U.S. worker and firm evidence.
  2. Add direct measures of promotion, task assignment, monitoring, and job quality.
  3. Search for evidence on workers who do not adopt, cannot adopt, or are required to adopt.
  4. Compare workers who can use public tools on their own with workers bound to employer systems.
  5. Add a company-level bridge only after identifying the changed task, input, or decision right.
