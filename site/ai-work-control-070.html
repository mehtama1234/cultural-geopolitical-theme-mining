AI use does not automatically produce confidence, control, or a better future

Status: bounded UNDP 2025 AI-and-human-development survey comparison · Checked: 2026-09-15

The bounded finding

The UNDP 2025 Global Survey on AI and Human Development makes four questions visible in the same survey: how often people use AI tools, whether they feel able to control their interactions with AI, whether they trust their government to use AI to improve services, and whether they believe AI systems are designed in people’s interests.

The United States shows a cautious pattern. In the weighted descriptive tabulation, 32.6% of US respondents reported using AI tools at least weekly. 33.9% agreed or strongly agreed that they could control their interactions with AI systems. 29.1% were very or somewhat confident that their government could use AI to improve services, and 37.5% were very or somewhat confident that AI systems are designed in people’s interests.

These numbers should not be collapsed into an “AI readiness” score. Use, personal control, government confidence, and belief in design are different social surfaces. A person can use a tool frequently while feeling little control; a country can report high government confidence without that survey measuring service quality; and a person can expect AI to help without having a way to contest an automated decision.

The comparison makes the separation visible

Country Weekly-or-more AI use Confidence controlling interactions Confidence government can use AI Confidence AI is designed for people
United States 32.6% 33.9% 29.1% 37.5%
China 37.4% 39.9% 88.6% 83.3%
Germany 38.3% 27.8% 44.9% 42.5%
South Africa 59.4% 42.8% 57.2% 67.6%

The comparison is illustrative rather than a ranking of countries. South Africa’s reported use is higher than the US figure in this survey, but that does not establish greater benefit or productivity. Germany’s reported use is also higher while confidence in personal control is lower. China’s high public confidence is not evidence that its government’s AI services perform better or that individuals have more practical control. The point is precisely that the four measures do not move as one.

Why this matters for the US-centered program

The program is studying how technology enters work, household life, consumer choice, public institutions, and political meaning. This survey helps locate a missing middle:

AI availability and exposure
  -> actual use and purpose
  -> perceived control and confidence
  -> ability to correct, refuse, or benefit
  -> work, household, institutional, and political consequence

The US figures reach the use and perception stages. They do not show whether a respondent used AI for work, education, health, finance, or entertainment in a way that changed time, pay, care, quality, or risk. They do not show whether a government service improved, whether an employer imposed the tool, or whether a person could appeal a decision.

The US average hides a real age and gender split

The same tabulation shows that the US average is not a single experience. Among 18–35-year-olds, 46.8% reported weekly-or-more use, compared with 16.6% among respondents aged 60 or older. Confidence in controlling AI interactions was 48.0% in the 18–35 group and 31.8% in the 60+ group. Government confidence was 34.3% and 23.8%, respectively. The 15–17 cell was small (55 respondents), so its higher reported use should be treated as a signal rather than a stable estimate.

Gender differences also appear in the descriptive layer: 37.3% of male respondents reported weekly-or-more use versus 27.7% of female respondents; 46.6% of men versus 33.5% of women expressed confidence controlling their AI interactions. The “other” category contains only 13 respondents and is not a reliable estimate.

Education is not a simple ladder. University-or-more respondents reported 46.3% confidence in their personal control, compared with 36.8% among those who completed secondary school, but their design confidence (34.3%) was below the “some secondary” group (40.9%). Weekly use also varied non-monotonically. That is a useful counterexample to treating education or credentials as a complete proxy for AI agency.

These differences do not show that age, gender, or education causes AI use or control. They identify where the next US study must look: access, purpose, workplace exposure, training, income, time, and whether people can refuse or correct the systems they encounter.

Within the US sample, use and confidence rise together—but the direction is open

Among US respondents who reported weekly-or-more use, 51.8% also agreed or strongly agreed that they could control their AI interactions. The corresponding share among monthly-or-less users was 35.8%, a descriptive gap of 16.0 percentage points. Weekly-or-more users were also more likely to express confidence that government could use AI to improve services (36.0% versus 25.5%) and that AI systems are designed in people’s interests (51.1% versus 30.7%).

This is a useful same-survey bridge, but it is not a causal result. More frequent users may have more training, better access, different jobs, or more familiarity with the systems. Conversely, people who already feel in control or trust the institutions involved may be more willing to use AI. The survey does not reveal which came first, what tool was used, or whether a person could actually correct an error. The result strengthens the need to measure use, perceived control, institutional confidence, and verified outcomes as separate fields in a dated episode.

The first social meaning of AI may be the domain where it appears

In the US sample, respondents most often reported AI use in entertainment, streaming, or gaming (26.0%). The corresponding reported shares were 17.0% for financial services or banking, 16.4% for work-related tools, 14.7% for healthcare applications, 13.1% for education or learning apps, and 6.5% for government services. These domain items can overlap and do not measure how often or how consequential the use was.

When asked about likely future or potential use, 66.7% were somewhat or very likely to use AI for entertainment, 58.9% for education and training, 49.5% for work tasks, 43.3% for medical advice, and 34.7% for financial advice. This gap between reported domain use and intended or expected use is itself informative: adoption may be moving through imagined usefulness before it becomes a stable practice. It is not evidence that these intentions will be realized.

The domain ordering also changes the social question. AI in entertainment may change attention and identity; AI in work may change evaluation and bargaining; AI in health or finance may change risk and reliance; and AI in government may change access, explanation, and the ability to appeal. “AI adoption” hides those different stakes.

Work expectations contain both promise and threat

In the US sample, 32.0% said it was likely or very likely that AI would increase their productivity at work. 27.1% expected AI to help create new job roles, while 21.7% expected their current job to be significantly changed or replaced. The measures can coexist: a person may expect a tool to make work more productive and still expect the job around that work to change.

The work-use comparison sharpens the pattern. Among respondents who said they were somewhat or very likely to use AI for work tasks, 50.5% also expected a productivity increase. Among those who said they were not at all or not very likely to use AI for work tasks, the corresponding share was 14.5%. This 36.0 percentage-point difference is an association between two expectations, not a productivity effect. It may reflect occupation, education, access, optimism, or prior exposure. It does not tell us who captures the gain, who bears job risk, or whether workers gain voice.

That boundary fits the existing work-control evidence. NBER task-adoption and firm-expectation records describe employer and task exposure. JRC and ILO evidence address autonomy, monitoring, voice, and negotiated rules. BLS and SIPP describe labor movement and household resources. The UNDP survey adds people’s own perception of control and institutional confidence, but it does not join those perceptions to a US workplace or household episode.

What the evidence does not establish

  • The survey does not estimate a causal effect of AI use on trust, work, income, health, or wellbeing.
  • Country differences may reflect sampling, language, survey mode, political context, internet access, age structure, education, and different meanings of “control” or “confidence.”
  • The reported percentages are not all based on the same valid item universe; missing and special responses were excluded separately.
  • Confidence in government or design is not verified performance, fairness, or actual human oversight.
  • Weekly use is not dependence, productivity, job displacement, or household benefit.

Next test

For the US, the next pass should stratify these measures by age, education, employment, income, gender, and AI purpose. It should then connect respondents, where lawful and possible, to a dated work or public-service episode:

  1. what system was encountered and why it was used;
  2. who set the rule and what information was collected;
  3. whether the person could understand, correct, refuse, or appeal;
  4. time, pay, health, care, or financial effects;
  5. whether a human decision-maker or representative intervened; and
  6. whether confidence changed after a concrete outcome or remedy.

The key countercells are frequent users with low control, infrequent users with high exposure anxiety, workers whose productivity rises without bargaining power, and people who trust a system until a failed remedy makes its limits visible.

Bottom line: AI adoption, personal control, public confidence, and belief that systems serve people are separate questions. The end-to-end program must follow them into actual work, household, institutional, and political episodes before calling technology a social gain or loss.
