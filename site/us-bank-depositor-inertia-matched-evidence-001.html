A short argument · US deposits, switching, and bank choice

Staying with a bank can save effort while giving up interest

An inactive account may be convenient for the customer and valuable funding for the bank.

The argument

The real question is whether the interest left behind is larger than the effort, error risk, service value, and safety of moving. The answer differs by balance, age, bill complexity, and branch access.

Follow one account

A worker keeps an old account after seeing a better rate elsewhere. Direct deposit and automatic bills are attached to the old bank. Moving everything could cause a missed payment. The worker waits. The bank gains stable deposits; the customer buys convenience with foregone interest.

account opens → income and bills attach → switching effort and error risk → lower rate accepted or better offer pursued → customer return and bank funding → competition and stability change

The deeper finding

Inattention is part of how a bank relationship is built. A bank benefits from deposits that do not move, while the customer may receive less interest. Easier switching could improve bargaining power but also change local banking.

What remains unproven

The studies do not show one customer's full rate gap after fees, taxes, time, service, safety, and switching errors, or what greater switching would do to local lending.

Next test

Join account rates, balances, fees, direct-deposit links, automatic bills, switching attempts, service use, and customer outcomes.

Reading rule

Do not call a depositor asleep or a bank exploitative until you compare the rate gap with switching cost and service value.
