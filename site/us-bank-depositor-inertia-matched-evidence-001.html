When staying with a bank saves effort but gives up interest

A depositor may leave money in the same bank because moving pay, bills, and trust takes work. The bank gains stable funding while the customer may give up interest.

The argument

The real comparison is the interest left behind against effort, error risk, service value, safety, and the ability to move. A low switching rate is not automatically irrational.

Convenience can have a measurable price.

Follow one account through a year

A worker sees a better savings rate elsewhere. The old account receives pay and sends rent, utilities, and insurance payments. Moving means changing several instructions and watching for an error. The worker waits another month. The bank keeps stable deposits; the customer buys continuity with foregone interest.

account opens → income and bills attach → switching effort and error risk → lower rate accepted or offer pursued → customer return and bank funding → competition, local choice, and stability change

The deeper finding

The same setup that makes bills run quietly also makes a rate change easy to miss. A bank gains funding that stays; a customer gains continuity but may lose bargaining power. Easier switching could improve competition while moving deposits away from local branches.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show what greater switching would do to local lending, branches, bank stability, or service for people who need in-person help. It also does not show whether staying reflects inattention, trust, fear of errors, low balance, or a deliberate choice.

Next test

Join account rates, balances, fees, direct-deposit links, automatic bills, switching attempts, service use, errors, and outcomes. Show the annual dollar gain from moving and an assisted transfer path. Compare interest gained with time, errors, branch loss, and later bank choice.

Reading rule

Compare the rate gap with switching cost and actual service and safety value.
