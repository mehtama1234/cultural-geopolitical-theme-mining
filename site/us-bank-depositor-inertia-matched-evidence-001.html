When staying with a bank saves effort but gives up interest

The argument

Many depositors leave money in the same bank even when another account pays more. That can be sensible. Moving direct deposit, automatic bills, savings, branch access, and trusted service takes time and carries error risk. It can also cost the customer interest while giving the bank steady, low-cost funding.

The important question is not whether an inactive customer is foolish or a bank is exploitative. It is whether the interest left behind is larger than the effort, risk, service value, and safety of moving. The answer changes with the account balance, bill complexity, digital access, age, disability, and need for a local branch.

The account is not just a place where money sits. It is a set of links: pay arrives, rent leaves, subscriptions renew, a card is connected, and an emergency transfer may be needed. A higher rate on one account may not compensate for breaking those links or for losing a branch that can resolve a problem. But the links can also give the bank a quiet advantage because the customer must do the work of moving them one by one.

Follow one account through a year

A worker sees a better savings rate at another bank. The old account receives pay and sends rent, utilities, and insurance payments. Moving would mean changing several instructions and watching for an error. The worker waits one more month, then another. The bank keeps stable deposits; the customer buys convenience with foregone interest.

For a small balance, the interest gap may not justify the work. For a large balance, the same effort may be worth hundreds of dollars. A bank relationship can therefore be valuable to both sides while still leaving room for better comparison and easier switching.

The risk is not always visible during the move. A forgotten autopay can cause a late fee. A changed account number can interrupt rent or payroll. A customer may move savings first but leave the checking account because the error risk is too high. Partial switching can reduce the interest gain while preserving most of the old bank's funding benefit. The customer needs a map of every connection, not only a new account opening form.

Trust matters in both directions. A local bank may know how to handle a disputed charge or a small-business cash problem, while a digital bank may offer a higher rate and a lower fee. A customer may stay because the bank has behaved well during a crisis, not because the customer failed to compare. Another may stay because an account restriction or fraud review would be difficult to resolve without a branch. Service quality and fear of failure can look like the same inertia from the outside.

account opens → income and bills attach → switching effort and error risk → lower rate accepted or offer pursued
→ customer return and bank funding → competition, local choice, and stability change

What the sources actually establish

Step Evidence Meaning
Staying NBER finds only 5–15% of depositors open a new account per year in its study. Existing relationships change slowly.
Customer trade A higher rate may pay more interest, but fees, taxes, safety, service, and switching time also matter. Rate alone is not the complete choice.
Bank value NBER models “sleepy” deposits as 57% of average deposit franchise value. Inactive accounts can be valuable funding.
Switching work Direct deposit, automatic bills, branch access, and setup make moving harder. Convenience and error risk have a real price.
Limits The studies do not show one customer's complete rate gap after fees, taxes, time, service, safety, and switching errors. Inertia is not proof of exploitation or irrationality.

Sources: NBER account study, HBS on sleepy customers, and FDIC switching guidance.

The deeper finding

Inattention is not merely a customer weakness. It is part of how a bank relationship is built. The same setup that makes bills run quietly also makes a rate change easy to miss. A bank gains funding that stays; a customer gains continuity but may lose bargaining power.

Easier switching could improve competition. It could also move deposits toward large digital banks, weaken local branches, or change how smaller banks lend. The customer-level gain and the local-system result need to be measured together.

The deeper finding is that switching cost is a shared infrastructure problem. Each bank can make its own account easy to open, but the customer has to move a whole household system across institutions. A common transfer process could reduce the work, yet it could also make rapid deposit movement more common during stress. That may help customers get a better rate and make banks more exposed to fast outflows. Consumer convenience and financial stability can pull in different directions.

The public question is whether customers can see and negotiate the value of their relationship. A bank can pay less interest because it provides a useful branch and safe service, or because it expects customers not to leave. The data needed to tell those stories apart are the rate paid, balance, fees, service use, error history, and the customer's actual alternatives. A label such as “sleepy” cannot answer the question.

There is a household cash-flow link. Interest that is missed may be small each month but meaningful over years, especially when the balance is used for emergencies or retirement. A customer who does not switch may be paying for convenience with a slower debt payoff or a smaller reserve. The cost compounds quietly, which makes it easy to ignore and difficult to explain after the fact.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show what greater switching would do to local lending, branches, bank stability, or service for people who need in-person help. It also does not show whether customers miss the rate because of inattention, trust, fear of errors, low balance, or a deliberate choice to keep the relationship.

It also does not show whether a higher rate is stable, whether the alternative bank has hidden fees or weaker fraud response, or how long the customer would keep the new account. A rate snapshot can overstate the gain. The missing comparison is the full relationship over time.

Next test

Join account rates, balances, fees, direct-deposit links, automatic bills, switching attempts, service use, errors, and customer outcomes. Show customers the annual dollar gain from moving and an assisted transfer path. Compare the interest gained with time, errors, branch loss, and later bank choice.

Give matched customers a clear annual rate-gap statement and an assisted account-transfer tool, then follow both movers and stayers for two years. Record every linked payment, error, fee, fraud case, branch or digital service use, rate change, balance, and later account choice. Measure interest gained, missed payments, resolution time, local branch use, bank funding changes, and customer confidence. The test should reveal whether easier switching creates genuine bargaining power or only faster movement toward the largest institutions.

Reading rule

Do not call a depositor asleep until you compare the rate gap with switching cost and the account's actual service and safety value.
