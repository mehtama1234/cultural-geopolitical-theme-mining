Finding 005: Expense pressure expands search across income groups, but not the same way

Status: bounded same-respondent income-stratified cross-lag finding · Checked: 2026-09-19

The finding

The April-to-June 2025 HTOPS link retains 6,564 respondents with the same SCRAMID. April self-reported expense difficulty is carried to June search, AI-use, and Congress-confidence outcomes. Within every household-income band, people reporting expense difficulty searched more than people reporting no difficulty, but the route differed by resource position:

April household income band Job search, difficulty vs not Benefit search, difficulty vs not Credit search, difficulty vs not Any AI use, difficulty vs not
Under $50,000 39.8% vs 16.3% 20.3% vs 10.1% 16.2% vs 10.1% 41.6% vs 38.3%
$50,000–$99,999 46.2% vs 21.4% 22.5% vs 9.0% 25.1% vs 11.5% 60.5% vs 52.9%
$100,000 or more 37.0% vs 25.1% 15.4% vs 13.0% 23.6% vs 12.1% 71.5% vs 71.7%

The result is not a simple “poor people search, rich people use AI” story. Search rises across the distribution, but lower-income respondents show a particularly large job-search gap and a clear benefit-search gap. Higher-income respondents have much higher AI use overall, yet expense difficulty barely changes AI use in that group.

The six search fields also show a multi-front navigation burden. Among people reporting expense difficulty, the weighted mean number of search domains was 1.34 below $50,000, 1.59 at $50,000–$99,999, and 1.39 at $100,000 or more. The share searching in at least two domains was 40.3%, 45.7%, and 39.4%; among people without expense difficulty it was 21.8%, 27.1%, and 26.5%. This makes the burden more concrete: expense pressure is associated with simultaneous option-building across multiple systems, not merely one isolated search.

The overlap screen also argues against treating AI as a substitute for this navigation. Among expense-difficulty respondents, June AI users searched at least two domains at 56.0% below $50,000, 50.6% at $50,000–$99,999, and 40.7% at $100,000 or more. The corresponding shares among non-AI users were 29.1%, 38.3%, and 36.1%. AI use is therefore associated with more simultaneous search in this descriptive screen, not with the disappearance of search. This may reflect greater need, greater digital access, or greater general engagement; the design cannot distinguish those explanations or establish substitution.

What the chain establishes

April household resource position and expense difficulty
  -> June search across jobs, food, benefits, credit, housing, and medical help
  -> different navigation routes by income band
  -> completion, usable alternative, remedy, recovery, trust, and exit [open]

This strengthens the constrained-agency interpretation. Material pressure is associated with more option-building effort, but the type of option pursued depends partly on household resources. Search remains an effort measure, not evidence that a usable alternative was found or that the household recovered.

Limits

  • Income is the April RFAM_INCOME household-income band, not liquid cash, wealth, debt capacity, or available time.
  • The estimates use April PWEIGHT and April successive-difference replicate weights; the weights are not documented here as attrition-adjusted longitudinal weights.
  • Expense difficulty is self-reported and broad; it is not a dated bill or named institutional episode.
  • The groups are descriptive. No causal effect, mediation, completion, receipt, remedy, recovery, trust change, or exit is identified.
