When local buying power reaches the ballot

People may respond to what their pay can buy, not simply to the inflation rate in a national report.

The argument

If local prices rise faster than local pay, a household can lose room while another in the same county gains. But a county result is not a voter interview.

The evidence is strongest for a place-level economic pattern and weaker for one household’s path to one ballot.

Follow one election cycle

A family’s wages rise 3%, but rent, food, and fuel rise 7%. It buys less, saves less, borrows, or delays care. One candidate blames inflation; another blames policy or firms. The family may reward one explanation, reject both, or vote on safety or identity.

local prices and wages → real buying power and household room → buying less, saving less, borrowing, or delaying a need → blame, trust, and economic judgment → turnout, vote choice, or support for change

The deeper finding

Economic blame is built from a household’s felt gap between pay and prices, then filtered through identity, trust, news, and the proposed fix.

What remains unproven

The evidence does not show that one household’s price loss caused one vote, or that changing a belief changes turnout. County averages hide large household differences.

Next test

Join local prices and wages to a household panel for two election cycles. Follow spending, saving, borrowing, media, policy knowledge, trust, turnout, and vote choice.

Reading rule

Do not say voters punish inflation when the evidence may show lost real pay.
