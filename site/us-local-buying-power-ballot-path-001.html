Connected US finding · buying power, prices and voting

When local buying power reaches the ballot

People may respond to what their pay can buy, not simply to the inflation rate in a national report.

In one sentence

A county-level loss of real pay was linked to election results, but the evidence does not show how each person voted or why.

Keep local prices, wages, household choices, blame, and vote separate.

What the sources show

Falling real wages predicted a political shift in county data

An NBER study uses US county-level family-budget costs, nominal income, and presidential and congressional results from 2021 to 2024. It reports that a decline in real wages, not higher inflation alone, predicted Republican gains. Inflation still had an association with presidential vote share beyond a simple economic-voting measure.

Separate survey research links buying-power beliefs to reported shopping and saving choices. The two sources do not follow the same household from prices to a vote.

NBER Working Paper 35301 · NBER Working Paper 32300

The connection

local prices and wages → real buying power and household room → buying less, saving less, borrowing, or delaying a need → blame, trust, and view of the economy → turnout, vote choice, or support for change

People vote on more than money. Party identity, immigration, abortion, safety, age, media, and local candidates can outweigh economic conditions.

The four outside views

Household: A family compares rent, food, fuel, care, debt, and wages. A national number may matter less than the bill that rose this month and the paycheck that did not.

Company and sector: Employers, stores, landlords, and local services shape prices, pay, hours, and product choice. A local wage can rise while basic costs rise faster.

Money and finance: Measure real income after the actual basket, debt, saving, and credit cost. Borrowing can hide a loss for a time and create a later bill.

Public power: Government may be rewarded or punished for local conditions it did not fully control. Policy timing and alternative causes matter before assigning blame.

What would change the finding

  • Individual prices, wages, debt, savings, and household budget over time.
  • Local housing, food, fuel, care, and transport costs.
  • The policy, employer, industry, or supply change behind the movement.
  • What voters knew, which source they trusted, and who they blamed.
  • Turnout, vote choice, party identity, age, race, income, and place.
  • Trust, spending, turnout, and the next vote after real pay recovers.

Next test

Join local prices and wages to a household panel for two election cycles. Follow spending, saving, borrowing, media, policy knowledge, trust, turnout, and vote choice.

Reading rule

Do not say voters punish inflation when the evidence may show a loss of real pay.
