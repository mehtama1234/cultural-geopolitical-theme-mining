Care, health, and social reproduction: source-led subtheme map v1

Checked: 2026-09-24
Scope: 234 current registry records carry the care_health_reproduction program-theme label. This map groups recurring evidence structures inside that material. It does not turn distinct surveys, administrative records, and research designs into one household story.

What the source material supports

The care material does not support a simple “more coverage means more care” or “family care is only private choice” narrative. It supports a more specific pattern:

Care is delivered through a distributed system of insurance, household money, unpaid time, work rules, family networks, public programs, and competing sources of authority. A formal service or entitlement can therefore coexist with foregone care, debt, lost work, or unresolved responsibility.

1. Coverage, affordability, and usable care are separate stages

The SHED coverage and care-foregoing record reports that 23.8% of insured respondents and 44.7% of uninsured respondents reported at least one type of cost-related care foregoing in 2025. Among the insured people reporting such foregoing, 42.6% also reported medical debt, versus 10.0% among insured people reporting no listed foregoing. The comparisons are cross-sectional, so they do not establish an insurance effect, bill timing, or a clinical result.

The MEPS care-delay and adaptation record adds a same-respondent descriptive layer: cost-related care delay is observed alongside payment strategies, spending/savings sacrifice, work/leave constraints, and family-care context. It does not identify one dated bill, causal mediation, treatment completion, or institutional repair.

The later SHED care-choice record adds a population-level choice surface that should not be collapsed into either coverage or debt. In 2025, 26% of adults reported skipping some medical treatment because of cost: 18% dental care, 15% a doctor or specialist, 10% follow-up care, 10% mental-health care, and 9% prescription medicine. The categories overlap. The reported any-treatment figure was 38% for adults with family income under $25,000 versus 13% with at least $100,000, and 45% for uninsured adults versus 24% for insured adults; 18% of all adults reported debt from their own or a family member's medical care. Separate annual samples reported 28% any-treatment skipping in 2024 and 26% in 2025, while the uninsured figure was 45% in both years. Those repeated cross-sections do not identify a person who recovered, a changed bill, or improved access.

Supported subtheme: insurance status, cost-related delay, debt, and adaptation must be read as connected but distinct care stages; type of care foregone and the choice-to-outcome interval are additional separations.
Not established: whether a particular plan, bill, provider, or denial caused the care decision, whether skipped care was clinically consequential, or whether later health improved.

1a. Randomized coverage can reduce financial exposure and increase care use without moving every later health or work outcome

The Oregon Medicaid lottery record uses lottery assignment among low-income uninsured adults as an instrument for coverage. In the first year, the lottery increased insurance coverage by 24.1 percentage points and outpatient visits by 21.0 points. It reduced unpaid medical bills sent to collections by 6.4 points, any out-of-pocket expenditure by 20 points, and borrowing or skipping other bills by more than half in the reported treatment comparison. These are stronger coverage-to-financial-route and care-use results than a cross-sectional coverage association.

The later outcomes remain deliberately non-uniform. At approximately two years, the study reports a 9.15-point reduction in positive depression screens and better self-reported health, but no statistically significant improvement detected across its reported objective physical-health measures. It likewise did not detect an employment or earnings effect over the labor-market follow-up.

Supported subtheme: insurance access can causally change coverage, care use, collections exposure, out-of-pocket burden, and selected mental-health measures while leaving objective clinical and work outcomes on separate clocks.

Not established: a current national Medicaid effect, the specific bill or care episode avoided, care quality or continuity, plan/provider access, household time substitution, long-run physical health, job quality, trust, or political action. The historical Oregon lottery population is not every coverage expansion.

1b. Leaving a reported care-foregoing state does not establish financial recovery

The SHED care-foregoing panel record links the same respondents from 2024 to 2025 across four reported cost-related-care-foregoing paths. The 2025 persistence path (foregoing in both years; 672 respondents) had 41.78% medical debt, 65.33% reduced savings, 36.39% increased borrowing, and 76.44% delayed a major purchase. The foregoing-exit path (444 respondents) still had 19.12% medical debt, 44.50% reduced savings, 20.25% increased borrowing, and 51.99% delayed a major purchase. The no-to-yes entry path (327 respondents) likewise showed a different 2025 adaptation surface: 30.37% medical debt, 59.67% reduced savings, and 62.95% delayed a major purchase.

All four path groups are annual self-report transitions; the source does not identify a dated bill, treatment, care quality, coverage change, or direction of causation. The relevant comparison is structural: an exit label can coexist with substantial debt and adaptation, while persistence, entry, and no-to-no groups carry different 2025 financial contexts.

Supported subtheme: care foregoing has entry, persistence, and exit stages that should be separated from medical debt, savings reduction, borrowing, and delayed consumption. A lower future foregoing report is not, by itself, a household recovery outcome.

Not established: whether a person obtained needed care, why foregoing changed, financial adequacy, care continuity, health improvement, debt resolution, remedy, trust, or political action. The smaller entry and exit cells have no reported replicate-weight standard errors.

1c. A Medicaid long-term-care delivery change can move Medicare health outcomes across a payer boundary

The managed-LTSS spillover record uses staggered county rollouts and Medicare claims for dual-eligible adults age 65 and older. In Florida, the managed-LTSS transition increased quarterly hospitalization risk 4.2% (0.9 percentage point) and reduced influenza vaccination likelihood 10% (3.2 points). New York showed no significant hospitalization change. The paper therefore observes a state-specific change in Medicare-financed hospital use after a Medicaid delivery redesign, not a single national managed-care effect. The public abstract does not disclose the individual claims denominator or complete rollout and robustness details.

Supported subtheme: coverage, delivery design, preventive care, and acute care can cross program and payer boundaries. A nominally Medicaid-side long-term-services change can be followed by a Medicare-side utilization outcome, and state implementation context is part of the result rather than noise to average away. Not established: whether hospitalization represents poorer health, improved detection or access, coding change, care quality, functional status, mortality, household cost or caregiving time, the full reason Florida and New York differ, or an effect for all managed-LTSS programs. The vaccination decline is a possible mechanism, not a complete explanation.

1d. External oversight can improve an institutionally captive care route and a mortality endpoint without adding measured inputs

The jail-healthcare accreditation record randomized immediate versus delayed offers of National Commission on Correctional Health Care accreditation across 46 midsized U.S. jails. The offer improved compliance with prespecified health-care quality standards, especially training and patient care, and substantially reduced 12-month jail mortality. The study did not detect an increase in capital or labor inputs; staff, record, and medical audits point to coordination, screening, and oversight as operating mechanisms. The public abstract does not disclose every effect size, confidence interval, implementation cost, or long-run outcome.

Supported subtheme: where people cannot freely select a provider, care standards, oversight, coordination, and screening can be a direct health route separate from staffing or capital counts. An externally assessed institutional practice can therefore have a measured mortality endpoint within the facility. Not established: post-release health, trust, fairness or legitimacy of detention, reduced detention exposure, recidivism, family outcomes, contractor- wide performance, every dimension of care quality, or results for prisons and other correctional settings. The experiment identifies the accreditation-offer path in participating midsized jails, not all accreditation systems.

1e. Persistence of reported care foregoing aligns with a worse health surface while work status follows a different path

The care-foregoing health/work panel record follows recontacted SHED respondents across the same 2024–25 reported care-foregoing paths, using a different outcome surface from the financial context in section 1b. In 2025, fair/poor health was reported by 12.06% of the no-to-no group (2,976 respondents), 22.28% of the no-to-foregoing entry group (327), 19.53% of the foregoing-exit group (444), and 30.62% of the foregoing-persistence group (672). Not-working status did not form the same gradient: the four path groups reported 36.32%, 30.04%, 32.56%, and 35.04%, respectively.

Supported subtheme: reported care-foregoing entry, exit, and persistence can sit alongside different later health and work-status surfaces. A lower future foregoing report is neither a demonstrated health recovery nor a uniform employment transition.

Not established: the bill, care need, amount, treatment continuity, coverage route, causal direction, paid-work quality or hours, care quality, institutional remedy, trust, or political action. These are descriptive annual transition comparisons without replicate-weight standard errors.

1f. Prescription delay shares a health and work context, not a demonstrated downstream effect

The MEPS prescription-delay context record uses 2024 person-level survey records with BRR uncertainty. Among respondents reporting a prescription delay, 31.84% reported fair or poor health and 55.06% were employed in the displayed round, compared with 10.02% and 49.32% among respondents not reporting delay. The delay, perceived-health, and employment fields are measured in the same survey round.

Supported subtheme: prescription delay, health status, and employment occupy different care-and-work surfaces and can coexist in a population layer. The contrast identifies a bounded access context, not the sequence by which a medication problem becomes a health or employment outcome.
Not established: that delay caused health or employment status; the date, medication, cost, coverage, pharmacy or prescriber route; treatment continuity; remedy; recovery; household burden; trust; or later action. Health need, age, insurance, access, and reporting composition can generate the same-round difference.

2. Family care is labor-market infrastructure with gendered paths

The family-caregiving record links survey-reported caregiving to administrative earnings across a 25-year window. Relative to matched comparisons, women showed falling earnings and employment after reported caregiving onset; men entered caregiving after significant prior labor-supply disruption. The study does not observe care hours, recipient outcome, paid-care alternatives, leave, or household support.

The first-time-parent leave record shows why “parental leave” is not one option. For 2014–2023 first births in its employed-before-and-after sample, 51.6% of mothers and 49.3% of fathers reported paid leave. Paid leave varied by race/ethnicity and sex; among mothers it ranged from 42.1% for Hispanic mothers to 62.3% for Asian mothers. These are descriptive estimates within the restricted working-parent population, not causal explanations for group differences.

Supported subtheme: care work is structured by job and family position, not only by a household's nominal income or formal leave eligibility.
Not established: the causal role of employer policy, discrimination, partner support, child outcomes, or later household security.

2a. Caregiving can alter the available adaptation menu across employment positions

The SHED unpaid-care/work-adaptation record crosses regular unpaid adult care with employment position in 2024. Among full-time workers, caregivers versus non-caregivers reported using less or stopping products at 70.7% versus 62.2%, increased borrowing at 24.4% versus 16.4%, reduced savings at 57.4% versus 42.2%, and working more or getting a job at 29.7% versus 20.6%. Among people not working, caregivers also reported more borrowing (19.8% versus 11.3%) and savings reduction (50.3% versus 37.6%), while the stated work-more/get-a-job route was low in both groups (10.4% versus 7.5%).

Supported subtheme: unpaid care, existing work position, consumption cuts, borrowing, saving, and attempted work expansion form an adaptation menu rather than a single caregiver effect. The menu differs for people already in full-time work, part-time work, and nonwork.
Not established: care hours, care onset, recipient need, pay or schedule control, causal care effects, replacement-care access, the ordering of work and care, repayment, material adequacy, or later recovery. These are weighted descriptive cross-tabs with outcome-specific denominators and no retained design-based standard errors.

3. Childcare constraints can sit next to later material hardship

The SIPP childcare-work-hardship record links an annual fall report that childcare prevented work or more work to following-month hardship within stable-SNAP-status person pairs. Among the small stable-SNAP, work-prevented cell, reported December rent/mortgage hardship was 43.7% versus 12.0% for the no-work-prevention comparison; utility hardship was 32.4% versus 19.4%. The cell has only 23 records with wide intervals, so those contrasts are descriptive signals, not a causal estimate.

The same record makes a key institutional point: stable benefit receipt is not the same as absence of care-related work constraints or material pressure.

Supported subtheme: childcare belongs in the household-room and public-help maps as well as in a family-services map.
Not established: that childcare arrangements caused the hardship, that SNAP changed the relationship, or that a particular childcare policy would resolve it.

3a. Provider incentives, staffed capacity, and family access are separate childcare stages

The military childcare workforce record documents a large employer-sponsored care system serving nearly 172,000 children with about 19,000 nonappropriated-fund workers in FY2024. GAO reports recruitment and retention difficulty contributing to lengthy waitlists and wait times. Across the Air Force, Army, and Marine Corps, 4,955 recruitment and retention incentives were reported in 2024; the count is neither a worker retention measure nor an added-slot measure, and Navy data were not comparable in that table.

Supported subtheme: care-system workforce policy is a distinct upstream route: incentive issuance, recruitment/retention, staffed classroom capacity, waitlist movement, fee burden, and parents' work/care outcome must not be collapsed into one access indicator. A fee discount can improve affordability without creating a slot.

Not established: any incentive's effect on turnover, staffed capacity, wait times, quality, family receipt, parent work attendance, readiness, or household security; the DOD system is not a civilian childcare estimate.

3b. A childcare program can have career and fiscal persistence beyond the preschool window, in a bounded comparative setting

The comparative Quebec universal-childcare record follows a subsidized universal-care policy into later maternal outcomes rather than ending the evidence at preschool enrollment. The working paper reports an enduring positive maternal-employment effect after children age out of preschool, a positive earnings effect growing to roughly twice the participation effect, and occupational and within-occupation career changes. Its fiscal calculation estimates 75% to 117% of up-front costs could be recaptured in net-present-value fiscal benefits; that final value is modeled.

Supported subtheme: a care-system intervention can have a longer route through paid work, career development, tax remittances, and benefit receipt than an immediate child-care slot or parent work-status measure reveals. Observed maternal labor outcomes and a modeled fiscal balance are distinct evidence stages.

Not established: a U.S. policy effect, equal slot access, care quality, child wellbeing, maternal wellbeing or schedule control, other unpaid-care changes, applicability across U.S. leave, tax, provider, and labor-market institutions, or whether a fiscal model represents a distributionally fair outcome. This is Quebec comparative evidence, not a U.S. treatment estimate.

4. Intergenerational care preserves continuity while redistributing capacity

The kinship-family record reports about 2.4 million children raised by relatives or close family friends because parents could not care for them—roughly 18 children outside foster care in kinship families for each child in foster care. Formal foster-care status can change access to support; it does not settle the quality or sustainability of daily care. GAO's selected community evidence also reports unmet childcare, housing, cost-of-living, and mental-health challenges, but it is not nationally representative.

The dementia coverage record finds that, four years before probable dementia onset, Medicare-enrolled adults with probable dementia were 2.97 percentage points more likely than matched controls to lack supplemental coverage. Medicaid eligibility was 4.95 points higher two years before onset, yet reported take-up was comparable. This historical panel analysis does not observe care quality, spending, caregivers, or financial harm.

The EITC parent-care record tests a different intergenerational substitution route. Its simulated-benefit design estimates that a $500 increase in EITC generosity reduces care from eligible adult daughters to older parents by 13--24 hours per month, driven by less intensive (40+-hour) care. The study finds no measured replacement through financial transfers, other children, spouses, or paid providers. That is a bounded replacement result for its functional-limitation and eligibility population, not evidence that every reduction in family care is harmful or unreplaced.

Supported subtheme: families and public programs can preserve care continuity while leaving capacity, eligibility, enrollment, cost burdens, and the replacement of unpaid care unevenly distributed.
Not established: that kinship care or coverage gaps caused the observed outcomes; care quality, safety, unmet need, health, long-run work/income, and household security after the estimated care reduction; or which support would produce stable care for recipients and caregivers.

5. Health authority is becoming a mixed cultural and institutional route

The health-information trust record shows that 85% of U.S. adults get health information from providers at least sometimes, alongside 66% from people with similar health issues, 36% from social media, and 22% from AI chatbots. Half found judging accuracy at least somewhat difficult and 54% found it difficult to know what to trust when information conflicts. These are reported information practices, not verified exposure, medical decisions, or outcomes.

The health and wellness influencer record finds 40% of adults ever use influencers or podcasts for health information. Its profile analysis shows authority presented through a mixture of self-described health professions (41%), coaching (31%), and entrepreneurship (28%). The analysis does not verify credentials, accuracy, sponsorship, care choices, or health effects.

The health-care trust and care-seeking record provides a separate mechanism layer: higher system trust is associated with greater preventive and elective care seeking and less avoidance, at a reported magnitude comparable with education and income gradients. The paper treats trust in doctors, nurses, hospitals, insurers, and public-health entities as partly substitutable across entities but more complementary within a domain; its counterfactual simulations assign the largest predicted behavioral role to trust in doctors. Because this is survey and structural-model evidence, neither the association nor the simulation shows that a trust shock caused later treatment.

Supported subtheme: health authority is being assembled across clinicians, peers, platforms, commercial identities, and institutions; trust and access are related but not interchangeable.
Not established: whether information exposure or a trust change alters realized treatment, affordability, adherence, health, or durable institutional legitimacy.

6. Care authority is distributed across information, insurance, and family routes

The Pew health-information record finds provider use (85%), peers with similar conditions (66%), social media (36%), and AI chatbots (22%) coexist; half found accuracy at least somewhat difficult to judge. The wellness-influencer record shows online authority mixes self-described professional, coach, entrepreneur, and lived-experience identities. Neither record observes a specific claim, clinical decision, treatment, spending, or health outcome.

The family-caregiving record shows family care coordinating appointments, medication, finance, household tasks, and personal care; lower-income adults with aging relatives report caregiving more often. It does not measure the same caregiver's hours, alternative service, earnings, or recovery.

The SNF prior-authorization record records 12% initial denials, 18% appealed, and 95% of appealed denials overturned in the reviewed Medicare Advantage route. An appeal outcome is not the same as timely admission, patient recovery, caregiver relief, or a corrected future decision process.

Supported subtheme: a care decision is increasingly assembled across many authority and gatekeeping layers; information access, insurance review, and unpaid coordination determine whether formal clinical capacity becomes usable care.
Not established: accuracy, medical necessity, actual care receipt, health outcome, financial adequacy, or durable trust in any single person's route.

6a. The labor market that selects future physicians can shape later spending without a detected health gain

The medical-school selection record uses downturns in other prestigious fields as an outside-option shock at medical-school application, then links physician cohorts to later practice and Medicare outcomes. Low-outside-option cohorts shifted toward students from non-pre-medical backgrounds and, decades later, billed more Medicare spending per beneficiary conditional on experience, specialty, and outcome year. They sorted more into settings with higher-powered financial incentives and used more discretionary, revenue-generating services despite seeing healthier patients; the additional spending had no detectable improvement in the study's patient-health outcome.

Supported subtheme: the provider labor market, professional selection, practice-setting incentives, service mix, spending, and patient health are connected but distinct layers of the care system. A downstream billing pattern can originate partly in upstream outside options rather than only in a current patient's condition or clinician's isolated decision. Not established: fraud, a universal overtreatment claim, individual physician motivation, total social cost, household medical debt, patient trust, care access or quality, the complete patient-selection mechanism, or results for all specialties and populations. The working-paper abstract does not disclose the full linked denominator or every health-outcome definition.

7. Clearing a medical debt can improve credit access without repairing the care or health route

The medical-debt-relief record reports two randomized experiments covering 83,401 people and $169 million in relieved downstream medical debt. In the credit-reporting experiment, relief raised scores by 3.4 points and credit limits by $340 on average; in the hospital-debt experiment it produced no average effect on care use, mental or physical health, or financial wellness. A later unpaid bill sent to collections increased modestly in that arm.

The companion medical-debt response record keeps the payment response distinct from a moral-hazard story. In the point-of-sale hospital-debt arm, another unpaid bill sent to collections rose 1.1 percentage points (6.6% of the 16.2% control mean); the study interprets this as lower repayment of existing medical bills. The design cannot determine whether that reflected expectations of relief, confusion about which debt was relieved, debt targeting, or an intentional nonpayment response.

Supported subtheme: debt remediation can change the financial record without undoing the original care need, restoring clinical use, or creating broad recovery. Credit access, care access, health, and financial wellbeing are separate post-remedy outcomes. Not established: the original bill's cause, household tradeoffs, trust, unpaid care, heterogeneous gains/losses, or durable security; nor why the later collections response occurred.

8. Climate disruption can become a care shock when school is the care infrastructure

The wildfire-smoke caregiving record uses plausibly exogenous smoke exposure linked to school closures and absenteeism. It finds substantial contemporaneous maternal labor-market costs and declining employment when these caregiving shocks accumulate, even where formal childcare was available. The employment loss was fully offset for mothers outside male-dominated industries, consistent with an industry-level workplace-tolerance difference.

Supported subtheme: environmental risk reaches family livelihood through care infrastructure and workplace tolerance, not only through property damage or respiratory exposure. Not established: household income, paid/unpaid care hours, schedule control, alternate care use, health, later employment recovery, or an employer-policy mechanism.

8a. A peer self-harm death can be followed by divergent care-seeking and behavioral responses among surviving youth

The peer self-harm death record uses linked South Carolina administrative data and a matched difference-in-differences design. The study reports that exposure to a peer's self-harm death increased the probability of a self-harm diagnosis by nearly 50%, with both mental-health visit incidence and visit frequency rising after exposure. It reports heterogeneous responses: care-use and criminal-behavior effects were concentrated among white boys, while students without a prior diagnosis increased felony offending rather than care-seeking. Assault and transportation deaths did not produce a comparable increase in self-harm.

Supported subtheme: a social-network shock can enter health, care, and behavioral systems through more than one route; observed service use cannot be read as the same response for every exposed young person. The comparison-death result is an important constraint on a generic bereavement explanation.
Not established: an individual contagion mechanism, completed suicide, the roles of school, family, friendship, media, or community response, treatment completion, safety, later school/health outcomes, recovery, or a national rate. Administrative diagnoses and visits do not capture all distress or informal support, and the public abstract does not provide the full sample or follow-up horizon.

8b. Crisis-line operating capacity can improve without closing a population-health outcome

The 988 capacity and mortality record uses a difference-in-differences design around the July 2022 rollout. States with larger pre-988 crisis-line capacity gaps answered more calls in state and increased their in-state answer rates after implementation. The study reports no detectable short-run reduction in suicide mortality—its estimates rule out even modest reductions—and no statistically significant improvement in broader population mental-health outcomes.

Supported subtheme: hotline routing and answer-rate capacity are real care system outcomes, but they are not a complete crisis-care pathway. The route from a caller's answer through counseling, emergency response, treatment, housing, social support, and later safety remains distinct from a population mortality endpoint.
Not established: that no caller benefited; caller-level connection to care; repeat contact; treatment completion; subgroup effects; longer-run mortality; or the causal contribution of downstream care, means safety, housing, and support systems. The source is a working paper and its public abstract omits the full state, subgroup, and follow-up ledger.

9. Inspection timing can shape measurable care effort while leaving lived quality partly open

The nursing-home inspection record finds 74% of inspections occurred 40–60 weeks after the prior one and that staffing/care-input effort peaked around inspection, then declined before the next cycle. A one-standard-deviation effort increase was associated with a 0.2-point (5.4%) lower patient-mortality rate. The estimates that unpredictable timing could save about 92 additional lives yearly are model outputs, not an implemented reform result.

Supported subtheme: public oversight is part of the care-production environment; predictable audit windows can shape when a facility provides measured effort. Not established: complete care quality, resident/family experience, staffing stability, causal survival effect in every facility, or an adopted reform's outcome.

10. Administrative help can preserve coverage while contact reach remains a distributional gate

The Medicaid navigator record is a Wisconsin field experiment involving 92,957 fee-for-service beneficiaries facing renewal. Assignment to a prerecorded navigator-assistance call raised renewal one point from a 67.8% baseline and lowered procedural denials 1.1 points; gains were larger for Tribal members, children, chronic-condition participants, lower-income people, and adults 50–64. At most about 61% of calls were estimated to reach the correct person.

Supported subtheme: renewal outreach can change a concrete administrative outcome, but address/phone quality determines who can receive the intervention in the first place. Assignment, successful contact, renewal, and continued care are distinct. Not established: plan continuity, care use, bills, health, work, trust, or later recovery, and the result is not a national retention estimate.

11. Insurance reform can remove a hidden application gate without guaranteeing usable care

The ACA underwriting record uses proprietary pre-ACA applications and underwriting decisions to observe a previously hidden rejection margin. The study finds the ACA's ban on health underwriting and pre-existing-condition rejection, together with subsidies, had benefits concentrated among low-income, high-risk, and over-50 applicants. Its public abstract does not disclose the full application count, effect sizes, or later enrollment, payment, network, treatment, work, or household outcomes.

Supported subtheme: a formal coverage reform can alter the prior application-and-rejection route, and its value is not evenly distributed across health risk, income, and age.
Not established: continuous coverage, provider access, treatment continuity, health improvement, job mobility, or household recovery.

11a. Premium stabilization can preserve a visible enrollment route while shifting risk elsewhere

The Medicare Part D premium-stabilization record keeps premium, enrollment, financing, and care access as separate stages. GAO reports that, absent the demonstration, the average standalone-plan premium would have nearly doubled for beneficiaries remaining in their 2024 plan and 37% would have faced increases above $40. Under the demonstration, average monthly premiums moved from $42 to $43 and enrollment rose 2%; CMS reduced beneficiary premiums by up to $15 and limited plan increases to $35. Medicare also subsidized premiums and plan-sponsor risk, at an estimated $9.8 billion for 2025–26.

Supported subtheme: a health-insurance intervention can stabilize a consumer-visible price and preserve enrollment by reallocating risk through public finance and plan sponsorship. Premium level, enrollment, taxpayer cost, plan quality, medication continuity, and household net relief are distinct outcomes.

Not established: the demonstration's isolated causal effect, medication adherence, care use, health outcome, plan switching, household affordability, or whether the public cost is offset by avoided medical spending.

12. Billing protection can alter network patterns without measuring the patient bill

The No Surprises network record finds the share of in-network private claims increased after implementation in three of four selected specialties. For emergency medicine, in-network facility and professional claim shares had declined before the law and increased afterward. Facility payments rose in 2022–23 while professional payments fell, but the changes largely continued prior trends. In-network claims are an indicator of participation, not a causal or patient-level outcome.

Supported subtheme: a balance-billing protection can reshape provider, insurer, and payment arrangements while patient liability and actual care use remain a separate evidence stage.
Not established: lower out-of-pocket bills, access, quality, health, or an isolated causal effect of the law.

12a. A patient-liability protection can shift provider bargaining, network breadth, and premiums on separate clocks

The No Surprises arbitration/network record links federal arbitration, provider-insurer network, and ACA marketplace-plan data around the No Surprises Act. The study reports high-volume, provider-favorable arbitration awards above the statutory benchmark; provider network participation declined in specialties and states most exposed to the reform and after groups' first favorable arbitration outcomes. It also reports suggestive evidence that more arbitration-exposed insurers raised premiums.

Supported subtheme: limiting a patient's surprise-bill liability can alter the provider/insurer bargaining route and thereby affect the network and price environment surrounding care. Patient protection, provider outside options, network membership, and premium response are not interchangeable outcomes. Not established: patient-specific network adequacy, appointment wait, substitute-provider availability, realized out-of-pocket cost, care continuity, health, or household financial recovery. The premium evidence is suggestive, the source is a working paper, and provider participation is not itself a measure of usable patient access.

13. Health-record modernization can outpace operational readiness

The VA EHR modernization record reports that, as of August 2026, four of 18 GAO recommendations were implemented and 14 not fully addressed. VA paused after user feedback, planned a restart at Michigan facilities and nine additional 2026 sites, and targets about 170 sites by 2031. GAO reports no updated cost estimate or sufficiently detailed schedule for leading-practice assessment, alongside unresolved user needs and operational validation. Recommendations and rollout plans do not measure clinical safety or patient outcomes.

Supported subtheme: digital health capacity has a realization sequence: deployment, user readiness, data and workflow reliability, operational proof, and only then potential care benefit.
Not established: patient harm, clinical effectiveness, veteran-satisfaction prevalence, or the eventual success of the planned rollout.

13a. Exposure-record infrastructure can expand staff use before people can see or correct the record governing their route

The toxic-exposure record-access record tracks DOD and VA's Individual Longitudinal Exposure Record (ILER), which joins military exposure information for clinical decisions, research, and disability claims. VA estimated that about 3.2 million of 6.8 million veterans screened since 2022 had at least one potential toxic exposure; GAO found ILER use rose after the PACT Act, primarily among Veterans Benefits Administration staff. Service members gained access and a self-reported-update function in March 2026, while correction requests for service members and veteran access/correction were planned for later 2026.

Supported subtheme: a health-and-benefit record has separate institutional use, individual visibility, self-report, correction, and decision stages. Expanding staff access or screening can improve administrative capacity while the individual contestability route remains incomplete.
Not established: verified exposure, claim approval, clinician use in an individual decision, treatment receipt, correction success, health improvement, or trust. The screened-veteran estimate is not prevalence for all veterans and selected stakeholder views do not establish user experience.

14. Aggregate hospital capacity can increase while local physical access contracts

The hospital consolidation and access record tracks establishments with at least 50 employees from 1987–2019. Hospital employment rose over 40% and average establishment size about 75%, while the number of hospitals fell more than 20% by 2010. The share of counties without a study-defined hospital rose 3.6 points; in the decade beginning 2009, 39.2% of rural Southern counties had none, compared with 30.1% of rural counties elsewhere. County counts do not measure travel time, quality, affordability, or actual patient access.

Supported subtheme: system-level consolidation and employment growth can coexist with a geographically thinner local-care network, especially across rural Southern places.
Not established: a patient-level access or health effect, why closures occurred, or whether transport, neighboring facilities, outpatient care, or telehealth offset local loss.

Cross-subtheme finding and next test

Across these records, care's recurrent hidden trade is:

formal coverage, service, family support, debt remedy, oversight rule, or digital system
  -> usable money, time, information, contact, and access
  -> delayed, substituted, unpaid, foregone, or administratively continued care
  -> work, debt, housing, caregiver, recipient, and clinical consequence
  -> institutional correction and trust

The corpus measures several stages well, but seldom observes them as one dated episode. The next decisive records should preserve the care need, coverage and network terms, bill or prior-authorization event, work and family-time substitution, care received or delayed, remedy, later health and household outcome, and trust or action—without treating separate populations as one person.
