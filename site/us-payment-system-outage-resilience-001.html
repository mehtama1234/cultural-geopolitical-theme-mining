Finding 001: A digital payment system can be widespread while cash remains a practical backup during outages

Status: bounded payment-infrastructure and household-option finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The bounded finding

An NBER study combines electricity-outage, weather, transaction, household scanner, and survey data from the United States, Spain, and Sweden. During disaster and outage episodes, spending fell, digital payments became less available, and cash use rose. Spending also rose before disasters as households stocked up, often using credit.

Nearly half of surveyed consumers said they could not use their preferred electronic payment during an outage. The study finds that greater access to cash was associated with more completed transactions and smaller expenditure declines. A separate panel experiment gave some households preparedness guidance and followed their purchases through the next hurricane season.

The route is:

disaster or power/information outage
  -> electronic payment may fail
  -> cash, credit, offline tool, or no purchase
  -> transaction completed or spending falls
  -> [open] food, medicine, fuel, work, care, debt, and recovery

Cash is therefore not just an old payment preference. In some emergencies it acts as a backup form of practical agency. But a completed transaction does not tell us whether the household got what it needed or could afford it.

What this adds to the broader atlas

This is a digital-infrastructure version of the program's central pattern: formal capacity can be widespread while usable access depends on a less visible backup. It also joins infrastructure and household culture without collapsing them. A payment outage is a technical event, but the consequence depends on cash access, preparation, merchant acceptance, supply, credit, and the type of need waiting at the end of the transaction.

The cross-country surveys are comparison evidence, not extra U.S. household observations. The event-study, survey, scanner, and experimental components are also kept as different evidence layers.

Limits and next test

  • The study does not identify every missed food, medicine, fuel, work, or care need.
  • Spending declines can reflect supply shortages, closures, mobility limits, or income loss as well as payment failure.
  • Cash access may also capture banking access, preparation, income, and place resilience.
  • The public abstract does not provide the full treatment-effect table for the preparedness experiment used here.
  • The next decisive artifact is a privacy-protected outage ledger linking outage timing, payment availability, cash or offline access, merchant acceptance, essential purchase completion, credit repayment, and later household recovery.
