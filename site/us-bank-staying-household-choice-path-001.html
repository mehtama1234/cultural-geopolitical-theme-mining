When staying with a bank becomes a household choice

Leaving money in the same bank can be sensible, but the quiet habit can also leave some households with less interest.

In one sentence

Customer inaction can give a bank steady funding while costing the customer a rate that may matter over time.

Measure convenience, safety, switching effort, rate gap, and household use separately.

What the sources show

Depositors move infrequently and banks value the staying

An NBER study using account openings and closures at more than 900 banks reports that only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. Its model estimates that depositor sleepiness accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value, softens competition, and reduced default probabilities during the 2023 banking turmoil in the model.

An HBS summary reports that 94% of depositors leave funds at the same bank each year and says online account holders may be less likely to move because automatic payments take time to change. The two sources use related but not identical measures.

NBER Working Paper 34267 · HBS sleepy customers

The connection

bank account opens → bills, direct deposit, and habit settle around it → switching takes time and attention → customer accepts a lower rate or weak terms → bank gains cheap, steady funding → customer buffer and bank stability move together, but not equally

Staying can protect convenience and reduce the chance of a bad move. A bank benefit is not proof that the customer received a fair return.

The four outside views

Household: A person weighs interest against automatic bills, direct deposit, access, fraud protection, service, taxes, and account-error risk.

Company and sector: Banks value deposits that remain and use them as steady funding. Competition may improve rates, while rapid shifts can make funding less stable during stress.

Money and finance: Count the rate gap after fees and taxes, the balance, switching time, and what extra money would do for debt, saving, or bills.

Public power: Deposit insurance, disclosure, account portability, open banking, and competition rules shape the cost of staying or moving.

What would change the finding

  • Account balance, rate, fees, taxes, and actual interest difference.
  • Automatic payments, direct deposit, switching time, errors, and service.
  • Account type, bank size, safety, access, and alternative products.
  • Deposits, lending, rates, and bank risk when switching rises.
  • Differences by age, income, race, place, disability, and digital access.
  • Whether the customer knew about the alternative and valued convenience.

Next test

Follow depositors through a full rate cycle. Match balances and terms to bill links, switching attempts, service use, fees, interest, and later saving or debt.

Reading rule

Do not call staying lazy. Do not call cheap bank funding a customer benefit.
