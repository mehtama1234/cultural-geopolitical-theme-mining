When staying with a bank becomes a household choice

Leaving a bank may save interest or fees, but moving an account is work. Direct deposit, bills, safety, and service all sit around the old account.

The argument

Staying can be sensible. It can also leave a customer with a lower rate because the cost of switching is larger than the visible gain. The trade is money, time, service, safety, and error risk.

Customer inaction is not automatically laziness or consent.

What the sources establish

NBER data from more than 900 banks show only 5–15% of depositors open a new account each year. Its model estimates depositor “sleepiness” accounts for 57% of average deposit franchise value and can soften competition.

HBS reports that 94% of depositors leave funds at the same bank each year, with lower switching among older and checking or savings customers. These measures use related but different data and should not be treated as one exact rate.

Follow the account

account opens → bills, direct deposit, and habit settle around it → switching takes time → customer accepts rate or terms → bank gains steady funding → household and bank benefits diverge

Staying may prevent an error or preserve useful service. A bank’s cheap funding is not automatically a customer benefit.

Keep these apart

  • Staying by choice and staying because switching is hard.
  • Convenience and financial return.
  • Bank funding benefit and customer benefit.
  • Headline rate and interest actually received.

Next test

Follow depositors through a full rate cycle. Match balances and terms to notices, bill links, switching attempts, fees, interest, service use, and later saving or debt.

Reading rule

Measure convenience, rate gap, switching cost, and household use of the money together.
