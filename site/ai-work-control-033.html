Data-center growth turns digital capability into a local fiscal and governance test

Status: provisional cross-source infrastructure/state-power finding · Checked: 2026-09-14

The bounded finding

The current evidence connects four stages of the data-center chain without collapsing them into one outcome. LBNL estimates a large national electricity requirement; Prince William County records a concentrated local project pipeline and rapidly rising industry tax revenue; Virginia, Texas, and Georgia records show regulators moving toward minimum obligations, cost allocation, collateral, disclosure, and public contestation. Together, these sources show that digital capability is becoming a fiscal and governance problem as well as an energy problem.

They do not yet establish that data centers lowered or raised a particular household's bill, produced net local benefit, created durable local capability, or gave a firm or state geopolitical leverage. Those are the next empirical arrows.

The chain now measured

national digital demand estimate
  -> local project and building pipeline
  -> assessed equipment/property and public revenue
  -> reliability, tariff, minimum-payment, disclosure, and participation rules

Each arrow has evidence, but the evidence comes from different units and places. The chain should therefore be read as a linked research architecture, not as a causal estimate. In particular:

  • LBNL's national TWh cannot be assigned to Prince William County or converted into a residential surcharge.
  • County revenue cannot be called net public benefit without service costs, infrastructure spending, environmental effects, distribution, and local alternatives.
  • A planned building or campus cannot be treated as operating load.
  • A tariff rule or commission model cannot be treated as an observed bill or proof that all costs were recovered from large-load customers.

What the cross-source combination changes

1. The relevant social question is allocation, not only attraction

The local fiscal series shows why “investment” is incomplete as a description. The county reports a 59% increase from TY2024 to TY2025, but the computer- equipment rate also rose from $3.70 to $4.15 per $100 assessed value. The increase therefore mixes physical expansion, assessed value, equipment cycles, classification, and policy. The right next question is not whether the county received revenue; it is how revenue compares with service and infrastructure costs, who receives the public benefit, and whether the fiscal dependence changes local bargaining room.

2. Place turns a national scenario into an incidence design

The GIS snapshot does not measure electricity, but it supplies a disciplined sampling frame. Brentsville and Gainesville contain most of the planned campus GFA, while most recorded campus GFA remains planned or pending. Utility connections, substations, transmission cases, water records, traffic, tax assessments, jobs, and public comments can now be collected by place and dated project stage rather than inferred from a national average.

3. Regulation is a mechanism, not an outcome

Virginia's minimum-demand and contract provisions, Texas's proposed security and reporting framework, and Georgia's requested load and cost-recovery fields all address the possibility that a large customer can create stranded-capacity or allocation risk. That is evidence of institutional learning and contested governance. It is not evidence that the rules will prevent cost shifting, protect households, produce local skills, or make providers replaceable.

4. Public participation is part of infrastructure, but not yet legitimacy

The Virginia record includes substantial local hearing and written-comment participation. This creates an observable institutional route through which residents can contest routes and terms. Participation counts alone do not show who participated, whether comments were attributed to data-center growth, which remedies were requested, or whether the final decision changed those terms. Those fields are necessary before interpreting participation as trust, opposition, consent, or political power.

Georgia adds a sharper information-power boundary. Its September 2025 public filing notice describes a second, unexecuted large-load contract submitted for Commission Staff review before execution, while stating that the contract and all supporting documents are trade secret in their entirety. The public route therefore shows that review exists, but not what the customer promised, what the utility expected to recover, or whether other customer classes were protected. Confidentiality is not proof of misconduct; it is a measurable limit on public auditability and on the evidence available for testing cost causation.

5. The governance story has moved from rule design into implementation

The latest Virginia record opens a supplemental proceeding, PUR-2026-00131, to develop detailed line-extension amendments for direct-connect transmission facilities. That is a real institutional action, but it is not yet a completed cost assignment or customer payment. Separately, Dominion's September 2026 Rider T1 application files a proposed $2.90 monthly illustration for a typical 1,000-kWh residential customer and a systemwide transmission revenue requirement. The filing is not an approved bill and is not attributed solely to data-center load.

This should not be collapsed with the earlier commission comparison in which an approved method was modeled at $0.94 versus an alternative $2.90. The earlier comparison, the new application, and the supplemental implementation docket are different stages and artifacts. Their value is to show how the allocation question becomes inspectable over time, not to manufacture a before/after household effect.

Counterexamples kept visible

  • A very large planned footprint may be delayed, cancelled, or served by a lower-than-expected load; planned GFA is not realized capability.
  • A county can receive substantial tax revenue while public-service costs or environmental burdens offset some or all of the apparent benefit.
  • A protective tariff can reduce one modeled cost exposure while leaving other system, environmental, or reliability costs unresolved.
  • A state can impose long commitments and reporting without obtaining actual payment, compliance, transparency, or provider replaceability.
  • Foreign or private ownership can coexist with affordable, reliable service; dependence should be claimed only after a failed or costly switch, refusal, concession, or other observed constraint on choice.

Next end-to-end test

Build a dated matched-place ledger for Prince William and a comparison place with materially different utility governance. For each project or large-load account, preserve:

  1. planned, approved, under-construction, energized, and actual load;
  2. substation/transmission work, water and land requirements, and completion;
  3. tariff class, minimum bill, collateral, contract term, direct contribution, and cost-recovery source;
  4. assessed value, tax receipt, public-service cost, jobs, wages, vendors, and local procurement;
  5. household or customer-class bills and reliability outcomes;
  6. hearing participants, attributed concerns, requested remedies, and final decision changes; and
  7. ownership, portability, provider switching, refusal, renegotiation, or external-state response.

The decisive result would be a place-year comparison showing whether a project created usable local capability and public value while preserving inspection, affordability, and exit—or whether fiscal and infrastructure dependence reduced those options. Until that ledger exists, the strongest finding is that the allocation problem is now institutionally visible, not that it has been resolved.
