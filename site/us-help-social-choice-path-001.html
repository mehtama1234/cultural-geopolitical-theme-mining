When getting help becomes a social choice

A person may avoid a useful aid because using it tells other people that help is needed. The choice is not only about whether the tool works. It is also about what using it says about the person.

The argument

Private willpower can feel safer than visible help, even when an aid could make a hard change easier. A blocking app, nicotine patch, support group, or other commitment tool may be judged as a sign of weak control.

That judgment can change whether a person tries the tool. The social cost becomes part of the product’s price, alongside money, privacy, side effects, and fit.

This is an early signal about a barrier to trying help—not proof of a health, spending, or work outcome.

What the source shows

An HBS account of research by Julian Zlatev and Ariella Kristal describes commitment tools such as blocking apps, nicotine patches, and social penalties. In one trust task, 69% of participants chose the person described as using willpower over the person using a blocking app. Later studies reported lower integrity ratings and less tool choice when other people might find out.

The detail matters because the tool was judged as a sign of character. Imagine an employee who wants to limit distracting sites during work. A private setting may help, but a visible blocker can invite coworkers to read it as weakness. The employee may choose a harder private method, hide the problem, or avoid help entirely. A nicotine patch or support group can raise the same choice around health, money, and disclosure.

The opening page does not give a national US adoption rate or follow the same people from tool choice to later attention, health, spending, or care. A lower willingness to choose a tool may reflect price, privacy, side effects, or poor fit rather than shame alone.

HBS Working Knowledge

The chain to watch

unwanted habit or repeated use → visible aid could help → aid signals weakness or lack of control → person weighs help against being judged → private willpower, concealment, or no aid feels safer → harder change, repeated use, or delayed care

Price, privacy, side effects, ease, and fit may matter as much as the social signal. A tool can also reduce freedom if an employer or platform imposes it. The research path must measure these causes separately before calling the result shame or stigma.

Four views of the same choice

Household

A person weighs help against embarrassment, privacy, cost, and the chance that family, coworkers, or friends will notice. Keeping the choice private may itself be a real benefit.

Company

A product maker decides whether help looks like a normal setting, a medical need, or a public confession. Private controls may change who tries it, but hiding a tool does not prove that it works.

Money

Not using help may show up later as repeated purchases, missed work, or delayed care. A visible aid may also cost money or reduce earnings, so both paths need to be counted.

Public power

Schools, employers, health services, and public programs can make support ordinary and private. They should not force disclosure or turn support into monitoring.

What would change the finding

  • A US sample recording aid choice, visibility, price, privacy, and later behavior.
  • Whether the tool changed attention, health, spending, work, or care.
  • The same person’s view before and after others learned about the aid.
  • Differences by age, income, disability, work setting, family, and social group.
  • Private tools compared with shared promises or public rules.
  • Evidence that the tool helps, not only that people judge its user.

Next test

Offer the same aid with private, optional-sharing, and public-sharing choices. Follow use and behavior for six months while measuring cost, privacy, judgment, side effects, and actual benefit. Describe the tool either as a deliberate design choice or as evidence of weak control to separate the visible label from the tool itself.

Reading rule: do not call a person weak for using help. Do not call a tool effective because people judge it.
