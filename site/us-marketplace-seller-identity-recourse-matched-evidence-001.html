Connected US finding · marketplace identity and recovery

When a seller name is useful only if it helps the customer recover

Knowing who sold a product is not the same as having a refund, replacement, safe product, or appeal.

The argument

Seller disclosure can make a marketplace less anonymous and give a customer a place to start. It does not automatically create a remedy after purchase.

Judge transparency by the path from identification to recovery.

Follow the remedy, not the listing

A customer buys from a marketplace seller. The listing shows seller information. The product arrives damaged or unsafe. The customer contacts the seller, reports the listing, and asks the platform for help. The seller does not answer; the platform suspends the account; the refund is delayed. Identity created a starting point, not recovery.

seller disclosure → purchase → defect, deception, or delivery failure → report and proof → seller or platform decision → return, refund, replacement, appeal, or loss

The deeper finding

Identity, warning, responsibility, and remedy are separate controls. A platform can know the seller and still leave the buyer to prove the loss. It can suspend a seller and still fail to return the money. Investigation and return work are part of the product's real cost.

What remains unproven

The law strengthens identification and reporting, but the sources do not show whether disclosure changes refund rates, recovery time, unsafe listings, or trust. They do not follow the same purchase through complaint, return, refund, and final outcome.

Next test

Link seller disclosure to purchase, complaint, return, report, response time, refund, and remedy. Compare covered and smaller sellers. Record who carried each step and who absorbed the loss when the case failed.

Reading rule

Do not call seller disclosure customer protection until it shortens the path to a real remedy.
