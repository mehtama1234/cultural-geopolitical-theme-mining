Gallup 2026 moral values, government role, and cultural-polarization layer v1

Checked: 2026-09-17 Source: Gallup, Americans’ Rating of Moral Values Hits New Low; Moral Acceptability Falls for Several Behaviors; Americans Still Say More Religiosity Would Benefit U.S. Field dates: May 1–17, 2026 Values and Beliefs poll; the moral-values article reports a 1,001-adult national telephone survey Status: current cultural-meaning and public-role layer; not a measure of objective morality, policy effects, or individual moral change

Why this update matters

The atlas already records religious affiliation, switching, spirituality, and identity. This Gallup layer adds the public evaluation of the moral climate: whether Americans think values are good or worsening, whether government is seen as influential, whether it should promote values, and which behaviors are considered acceptable. It reveals an important political pattern: shared pessimism does not produce a shared solution.

Gallup also reports significant year-over-year declines in acceptance of birth control, having a baby outside marriage, gambling, teenage sex, and cloning animals. At the same time, most Americans still view birth control, divorce, sex between unmarried people, gay or lesbian relations, and several other behaviors as morally acceptable. The pattern is selective retrenchment, not a uniform return to one prior moral order.

What this adds to the broad goal

  1. Shared decline narratives can hide political disagreement. Democrats, Republicans, and independents all became more likely to rate moral values as poor, but the parties disagree about what government should do.
  2. Influence and legitimacy are different. Sixty-nine percent say policy affects moral values, while the public divides over whether government should promote them. Recognizing state influence is not the same as granting the state a legitimate cultural mandate.
  3. Religious resurgence is not a simple majority trend. Most adults say greater religiosity would help society, yet that share is down from 2013 and nonreligious adults are more likely to view it negatively.
  4. Moral change is issue-specific. Long-run liberalization has plateaued or pulled back on selected behaviors, while broad majorities remain permissive on others. “America is becoming more moral” or “less moral” is too coarse a description.
  5. Party labels are partly institutional and partly cultural. The same partisan groups that differ on government’s role also differ sharply on abortion, gender, sexuality, religion, and family norms. These are related cultural dimensions, not one latent attitude that can be assumed from party identity.

End-to-end position

The evidence supports this bounded chain:

perceived cultural/moral conditions
  -> attribution of government influence
  -> judgment about legitimate public role
  -> issue-specific moral acceptance or rejection

It does not observe a respondent’s personal conduct, policy exposure, local institution, religious practice, media environment, vote, protest, workplace encounter, or changed behavior. It cannot establish that government policy caused moral change, that moral concern caused a vote, or that issue attitudes translate into social cohesion or political realignment.

Next test

Use a repeated or event-linked survey to connect a dated policy or institutional encounter to prior religious and political identity, perceived moral threat or benefit, attribution, issue position, public action, and later trust or policy support. Preserve separate measures for national mood, government influence, government legitimacy, religious influence, moral acceptability, and observed behavior.

Boundaries and acquisition note

Gallup’s measures are survey judgments and trend comparisons. The values poll asks about selected behaviors whose wording and availability differ across the long series; issue acceptability is not legal status or observed conduct. The religiosity and government-role results are cross-sectional comparisons with older readings, not individual panel change.
