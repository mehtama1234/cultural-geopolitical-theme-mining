Finding 008: Worker voice is part of system design

Status: provisional
Last checked: 2026-09-11
Project: AI, work, and control

Finding

Worker voice is not only a remedy after an automated decision goes wrong. It can shape what the system is for, what it measures, how it is limited, and who is responsible before the tool becomes part of daily work.

The ILO’s global case-study review shows worker representatives taking part in AI and algorithmic-management decisions at several levels, from national and sectoral dialogue to company and workplace decisions. The cases do not show that participation always works. They show that worker participation is itself a practical control point.

What we directly know

  • The ILO working paper examines cases across Europe, North America, Asia, South America and the Caribbean, and Africa. It covers national, regional, sectoral, company, and workplace forms of social dialogue. ILO Global case studies
  • The study focuses on how worker representatives influence decisions about employment, algorithmic management, and working conditions. ILO Global case studies
  • The European Commission describes transparency, human oversight, safety, accountability, and the ability to contest decisions as central issues in algorithmic management. European Commission Future of Work

Causal chain under test

workers and representatives receive information -> they can question the purpose and design
-> rules change before deployment or during use -> data, pace, evaluation, and recourse are bounded
-> the system fits work better and causes fewer avoidable harms

The case study supports the existence of this governance path. Its five detailed cases include rules over digital replicas, retraining and workforce data, housekeeping task order, biometric access, and outsourced content work. It does not prove that participation produces better outcomes in every setting.

The Kenyan content-moderation dispute adds an important boundary condition. A trial court issued interim protections while workers’ claims against the contractor and lead firm were pending. In two related appellate judgments, the Court of Appeal set aside some interim orders in the Sama/Majorel appeal but dismissed Meta’s jurisdiction and continuation-of-orders appeals. Neither judgment decided the underlying merits. So legal reach, temporary protection, and durable remedy must be tracked as separate outcomes.

What to record in each case

  1. Who represents workers?
  2. At what level does the dialogue occur?
  3. What information is shared before deployment?
  4. Which choices can workers influence?
  5. Can they block, delay, or change the deployment?
  6. Are data, evaluation, pace, and health limits written down?
  7. What happens when management rejects the concern?
  8. Is there a later appeal or enforcement path?

Why this matters

An appeal is a late form of power. Participation before deployment is earlier power. The difference matters because a worker may be unable to challenge a system once staffing, pay, or discipline already depends on it.

This also changes how we read company claims about trust and training. A firm can offer training while keeping the purpose, data, and rules fixed. That is not the same as giving workers a say in the system.

Four-map reading

Map First signal Missing evidence
Material rules can shape pace, workload, and productivity measured outcomes before and after participation
Social participation may affect trust, status, and dignity worker voice by group and bargaining position
Institution dialogue can occur at several levels enforcement, coverage, and management compliance
Power workers may shape system purpose and limits veto, correction, appeal, exit, and ownership

Strongest challenge

Case studies often select visible or successful examples. Participation may be symbolic, late, or uneven. We need the actual terms, decisions, and outcomes in each case before calling it effective worker power.

What would change our mind

  • Evidence that worker participation does not change system purpose, limits, or outcomes.
  • Evidence that post-deployment appeals provide the same protection as pre-deployment participation.
  • Evidence that participation is equally available to contractors, migrants, women, disabled workers, and informal workers.
  • Evidence that a worker or union can retain protection after a contractor, vendor, or lead firm changes.

Next tests

  1. Compare the ILO case table with the actual agreements and legal records.
  2. Track participation before deployment, during use, and after a harmful decision.
  3. Add failed or symbolic consultation cases, public-sector cases, and contractor cases.
  4. Test whether participation changes monitoring, pace, pay, job quality, or data use.
