US firms report widespread algorithmic management before worker control is measured

Status: OECD employer-survey finding · Checked: 2026-09-13

The bounded finding

The OECD's 2024 employer survey places algorithmic management in a more consequential position than an “AI adoption” headline suggests. Across firms with 20 or more employees in France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States, 6,047 mid-level managers reported whether their firms provided software to instruct, monitor, or evaluate workers.

The US has the highest reported adoption in the comparison: 90% of managers said their firms had at least one such tool, 76% reported ten or more of the 15 tool categories, and 90% reported monitoring and evaluation tools. One particularly consequential category—monitoring the content or tone of conversations, calls, or emails—was reported by 55% of US firms, compared with 6% across the surveyed European countries and 8% in Japan.

The same survey does not support a simple “firms adopted tools, therefore workers lost control” conclusion. Managers also reported improved decision quality and nearly 90% reported at least one governance measure. At the same time, nearly two-thirds of users reported at least one worker-impact concern, with unclear accountability, opaque logic, and inadequate health protection among the leading concerns. Governance presence is not governance effectiveness.

The mechanism under test

software instructs, monitors, or evaluates work
  -> information, speed, and managerial decision capacity change
  -> worker visibility, accountability, privacy, discretion, and appeal may change
  -> pay, schedule, health, bargaining, and household security may change
  -> trust, retention, organizing, firm capacity, and regulation may change

The OECD source directly measures the first step, managers' perceived decision effects, concerns, and reported governance. It does not measure the worker, household, or political stages. The US-specific adoption result therefore strengthens the exposure and institutional-governance layers, not the worker- control conclusion.

Why the comparison matters

The cross-country contrast is not a ranking of national culture or regulation. It shows that the same label covers different tool mixes and intensities. The US combination of high overall adoption, high monitoring/evaluation prevalence, and high communication-monitoring prevalence creates a clear measurement need: the program must distinguish a scheduling aid from a system that determines access to work, sanctions performance, or creates an unreviewable record.

Worker consultation is a potential bridge between adoption and legitimacy, but the OECD measure records whether consultation exists, not whether workers can change the system, appeal a decision, or prevent health and privacy harms.

Counterexamples kept visible

  • A firm can provide many tools while using them for low-stakes coordination; tool count is not a control-loss index.
  • A governance measure can coexist with opaque logic or unclear accountability; a policy document is not a remedy.
  • Managers can perceive better decisions while workers experience more surveillance or less discretion; manager and worker outcomes may diverge.
  • A monitoring tool can support safety or compliance rather than discipline; the category alone does not identify intent or consequence.
  • Cross-country rates can reflect sector, firm-size, legal, and questionnaire differences; they are not a causal national ranking.

Method and limits

The study is an OECD employer survey of firms with 20 or more employees across six countries, conducted June–August 2024. A mid-level manager or supervisor reported on tools used to instruct, monitor, or evaluate workers, perceived effects, trustworthiness concerns, and governance. Country-specific figures are weighted rates; the public summary does not provide every country-specific unweighted respondent count. Algorithmic management includes software that may not use AI.

Next test

Match employer adoption to worker-side measures of exposure, notice, data collection, discretion, scheduling, pay, health, appeal, worker voice, and exit within the same occupation, sector, or workplace. Preserve firms where consultation exists but control remains low, and firms where high adoption does not reduce worker autonomy. Then connect the workplace outcome to household time/security and later institutional trust or collective action.

Evidence status: official cross-country employer evidence with US-specific adoption and tool-category estimates; worker control, household incidence, causality, and political consequences remain open.
