Finding 002: Losing the expanded Child Tax Credit changed how households judged the economy

Status: bounded policy-to-perception finding · Checked: 2026-09-22

The finding in simple words

When the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit ended, families who lost more money reported a worse view of their economic situation. The NBER study estimates that every $1,000 lost was associated with a 1.7-point drop in consumer sentiment, or about 2.4 percent. The largest effects were among lower-income families with multiple children, and the effect lasted nearly two years.

The route shown by this study is:

expanded benefit ends
  -> household loses cash
  -> reported economic mood gets worse
  -> [open] spending, trust, blame, or voting

The last line is not established by this paper. A person can feel worse about the economy because their budget became tighter without changing their party, trust in government, or vote.

What this adds to the broader atlas

This is evidence that public policy can affect not only a household's money, but also how people judge the economy. That matters for the cultural and political map because public mood is partly formed through lived changes in household room, not only through news or partisan messages.

It also shows unequal exposure: the reported effect was larger for lower-income families with multiple children. The same policy ending therefore did not carry the same meaning for every family.

What it does not show

  • It does not follow one family from benefit loss through later political action.
  • It does not measure a direct change in voting, turnout, trust, or spending.
  • It does not prove that sentiment fell for one single reason.
  • It does not show whether the effect changed housing, health, work, or later financial security.
  • The full paper is needed for the sample denominator and detailed subgroup estimates; this record uses the official NBER abstract for the bounded claim.

Next decisive test

Link comparable benefit-loss exposure to a separately measured outcome such as spending, government trust, political contact, turnout, or vote choice. Do not call lower sentiment a changed political behavior without that next measure.

Source

  • NBER Working Paper 35059: When Policy Shapes Perception: The 2021 Child Tax Credit and Consumer Sentiment
