# End-to-end program recovery brief v1

**Read this first after a crash.** The project is a broad US societal, cultural,
consumer, institutional, financial, political, and geopolitical theme-mining
program. It is not a one-household study. Household data is one measurement
layer inside the program.

## The actual goal

Extract and test recurring themes across many source families—surveys,
administrative records, public records, research, company filings, markets,
platforms, media and cultural measures, opinion and election data, and
international or supply-chain records where they explain a US exposure.

The target is a connected map of how conditions and decisions move through
people, consumers, workers, households, firms, institutions, communities, and
politics:

```text
condition / price / rule / technology / shock
  -> money, time, access, control, status, health, or security changes
  -> people and organizations adapt, delay, borrow, switch, stay, organize, or go without
  -> firms, employers, platforms, agencies, courts, media, parties, or foreign actors respond
  -> money, risk, data, assets, and decision power are redistributed
  -> trust, identity, culture, public demand, political action, firm exposure,
     sector capacity, or geopolitical leverage changes
```

Every arrow must be marked observed, reported, estimated, compared, inferred,
or open. The project must not promote a plausible story into a finding merely
because adjacent datasets point in the same direction.

## Theme inventory

1. Household room and consumption: prices, fees, subscriptions, credit, debt,
   savings, food, health care, housing, insurance, utilities, repairs, delay,
   substitution, borrowing, and giving things up.
2. Time as a hidden price: commuting, caregiving, waiting, paperwork,
   customer-service loops, multiple jobs, unpaid work, convenience, and
   automation.
3. Consumer power and recourse: understanding a deal, reaching a human,
   appealing, correcting a record, receiving a remedy, switching, leaving, and
   refusing data collection.
4. Platforms, data, and attention: ranking, reviews, identity checks,
   personalization, privacy, surveillance, automation, digital access,
   dependence, and platform rule-setting.
5. Work, control, and bargaining: wages, schedules, monitoring, AI,
   productivity, benefits, job lock, mobility, worker voice, and who receives
   gains or carries risk.
6. Care, health, and social reproduction: access, bills, delayed care,
   disability, mental health, caregiving, paid and unpaid support, family
   time, transport, and the work of keeping people functioning.
7. Housing, place, and mobility: rent, mortgages, ownership, repairs,
   insurance, energy, water, transport, climate and disaster exposure,
   displacement, staying, moving, and recovery.
8. Unequal exposure and status: race, class, age, gender, disability, family
   structure, migration, geography, ownership, institutional access, risk,
   treatment, voice, and recovery.
9. Trust, identity, and cultural meaning: dignity, humiliation, belonging,
   exclusion, institutional trust, distrust, blame, identity, community,
   fairness, and changing norms.
10. Public systems and policy feedback: benefits, eligibility, cliffs,
    enforcement, courts, regulation, public investment, take-up, burden, and
    later demand.
11. Political judgment and collective action: personal versus national
    economic perception, parties, elections, turnout, protest, organizing,
    lobbying, media narratives, demands, and political action.
12. Firm, sector, and market power: consolidation, pricing, risk transfer,
    labor strategy, investment, profitability, bankruptcy, ownership,
    competition, and the gap between company success and public security.
13. Infrastructure, technology, and dependency: energy, water, logistics,
    communications, AI capacity, supply chains, critical inputs, foreign
    dependence, resilience, and system control.
14. Geopolitical and state consequences: domestic affordability, industrial
    capacity, technology, energy, finance, migration, legitimacy, corporate
    exposure, alliances, leverage, and strategic room to act.

## What had been pursued before the crash

- The broad objective and 14-theme inventory were recorded.
- Five priority bridges were defined: price/payment to household room;
  service/platform rule to recourse, trust, and exit; work rule/tool to control
  and local power; housing/energy/insurance to health, mobility, and place;
  and public aid/rule to interpretation and political response.
- Each bridge has a source-to-measure evidence matrix, a synthesis, limits,
  counterexample requirement, and next test.
- The reader layer contains the housing/insurance bridge, while the health-cost
  paper scan records care, debt, work, caregiving, coverage, and credit as
  separate outcomes.
- SIPP was being used as one population-level material/work layer. Its
  tenure, resource, region, transition, and Fay-BRR comparisons are evidence
  for distributional patterns, not the end-to-end program itself.

Since that recovery state, the broad program has added and linked these

## What we should pursue now

Continue bridge by bridge. For each, assemble the strongest compatible source
layers, identify the unit and time for every arrow, compare exposed and
unexposed groups, record who controls the next decision, trace where money,
time, risk, data, or power moves, and then test whether the result reaches
cultural meaning, institutional trust, political action, firm/sector exposure,
or geopolitical leverage. Add counterevidence and publish only what the data
supports. If a join is invalid, keep the link open instead of inventing a
household-level or causal connection.
