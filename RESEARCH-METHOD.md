# Research method

## 1. Keep the layers separate

Every note uses six layers:

1. **Observation** — what a source directly reports.
2. **Study result** — what the authors estimate or compare.
3. **Mechanism** — the behavior or constraint that could produce the result.
4. **Distribution** — who receives the benefit, cost, risk, or choice.
5. **Theme** — how the mechanism connects to culture, society, institutions, or geopolitics.
6. **Implication** — what may change for people, firms, sectors, or states.

Never use a theme as if it were evidence. A theme is a map built from several tested findings.

## 2. Read sources in the right order

For an HBS story, record the title, date, authors, linked study, claim, example, and what the story leaves out. For an NBER paper, record the paper number, date, authors, abstract, research question, data, design, sample, result, limits, later version, and related program. When NBER metadata or data is available, save it with the paper record.

Working papers are provisional. Mark them `working-paper`, not `settled-study`.

## 3. Build a causal chain

Use short sentences:

```text
condition -> decision or constraint -> behavior -> measured result -> wider effect
```

If a link is assumed, mark it as an inference. If two sources disagree, keep both and explain why.

## 4. Mine across four maps

Each project gets four parallel maps:

- **Material map:** labor, money, energy, land, technology, supply chains, health.
- **Social map:** family, status, trust, identity, class, race, gender, migration, place.
- **Institution map:** firms, schools, courts, regulators, unions, parties, media, armed forces.
- **Power map:** who sets rules, who bears risk, who can exit, who owns the data or asset, and who can make others wait.

The strongest themes connect at least two maps with evidence from more than one source family.

## 5. Score coverage, not drama

Each topic receives a coverage record:

- source count and source types;
- time range and geography;
- agreement and disagreement;
- direct evidence versus inference;
- missing groups, places, and outcomes;
- known data and method limits;
- next source needed.

Do not call a topic exhausted because many articles repeat the same claim. It is closer to exhausted when new searches mostly return known evidence, key counterarguments have been checked, and the remaining gaps are named.

## 6. Writing rules

Use plain words. Prefer “shows,” “may,” “we infer,” and “we do not know” over inflated certainty. Avoid slogans, empty moral language, unexplained technical terms, and broad claims about “society” without naming the people and institutions involved.

Every published memo must include a source table, a limits section, and a “what would change our mind” section.
